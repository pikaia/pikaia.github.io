Since early 2023, Hyundai has been building electric cars in a seven-storey plant in the Jurong Innovation District — the first time cars have been assembled in Singapore in more than forty years. That gap is easy to read as the natural state of things: Singapore is small, has no car industry, taxes cars harder than almost anywhere on earth, and imports every one it drives. For about half a century, though, it did assemble them. The plants were never large and never lasted, and the last of them shut in 1980, but for a while "assembled in Singapore" was a label you could find on a new car.

## Fifty years of Ford

The story starts with Ford. The Ford Motor Company of Malaya was incorporated in November 1926 to run Ford's business across Malaya, Burma, Thailand and the Dutch East Indies from Singapore, and it opened a plant that same year in a rented shophouse off Enggor Street. Calling it an assembly plant is generous: the main jobs were fitting wheels and touching up the paint on Model T Fords that arrived largely built. In 1929 it moved to a warehouse on Prince Edward Road, and from 1930 it began putting together semi-knocked-down cars — kits of major sub-assemblies, shipped flat and bolted together locally — from Ford's Canadian and British factories, for sale around the region.

Ford did well out of Malaya. Its mass-produced cars undercut British and European makes on price, and by 1939, with war cutting off European supply, Ford held about 80 per cent of the Malayan market. In 1941 the company opened a purpose-built plant on Upper Bukit Timah Road, designed by the French engineer-architect Émile Brizay in a clean Art Deco style, with three tall green-glazed windows down one wing. It was the first full motor-vehicle assembly plant in Southeast Asia, and it started doing body assembly rather than just bolting kits together.

![Lieutenant-General Yamashita and Lieutenant-General Percival and their officers around a table inside the Ford factory during the British surrender of Singapore, 15 February 1942](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/4/40/Yamashita_and_Percival_discuss_surrender_terms.jpg)

*The surrender of Singapore, signed inside the Bukit Timah Ford plant on 15 February 1942. General Yamashita is seated at left; General Percival's party has its back to the camera. The building is a national monument today because of this meeting, not because of the cars. (Japanese official photograph, 1942; public domain, via Wikimedia Commons)*

The plant's most famous day had nothing to do with cars. On 15 February 1942, ten weeks after the Japanese landed in Malaya, Lieutenant-General Arthur Percival signed the surrender of Singapore at a table inside the Bukit Timah factory. Under the occupation the Japanese army used the building to assemble trucks under the Nissan name. Ford got it back in 1946, resumed car assembly in 1947, and kept the line running for another three decades, taking completely-knocked-down kits from the United States, Canada, Britain, Germany and Australia. By the time it closed in June 1980 it had built more than 150,000 vehicles and grown from sixteen workers to three hundred.

## The regional race Singapore mostly watched

Ford's Bukit Timah plant was the only car assembler in Singapore until 1965, and that is largely because the action was next door. Through the early 1960s, governments across the region were pushing import-substitution industrialisation — the idea that a country should make at home what it had been buying abroad, behind a wall of tariffs high enough to make local production pay. Cars were an obvious target. In 1963 the Malaysian government invited manufacturers to set up assembly plants, and by the middle of the decade a cluster of them was going up around Johor and Selangor, assembling everything from Austins and Fords to the first Mitsubishi Colts, with talk of a home-grown "national car" to follow one day.

Singapore was part of Malaysia for two of those years and separate from it after August 1965, and either way it was on the wrong side of the arithmetic. A national assembly industry needs a domestic market big enough to give a plant some scale, and Singapore's was tiny. The plants that mattered were built where the customers and the local-content rules were, which meant the Malaysian side of the Causeway. Kuala Lumpur's push eventually produced Proton in 1983; Singapore's produced nothing comparable.

It did, though, get a few assembly lines of its own. Cycle & Carriage opened what was described as Singapore's second car plant at Hillview Avenue in 1965, screwing together Mercedes-Benz saloons — mostly 200s and 280s — along with Daihatsu and Isuzu trucks and the chassis for Mercedes buses. By the 1970s there were four assemblers running in Singapore: Ford at Bukit Timah, Cycle & Carriage at Hillview, and lines for Nissan and Volvo. All of them existed for the same reason: a tariff on fully-built imported cars that made it cheaper to ship the cars in as kits and finish them here.

## Why Singapore let it go

The tariff was the whole business model, and Singapore's government had decided against that model. After separation, the Economic Development Board built its industrial strategy around exporting to the world rather than protecting a home market — electronics, oil refining, ship repair, later precision engineering — industries where a small, open economy with a good port and a disciplined workforce could compete without a tariff wall. Propping up half a dozen sub-scale car lines with duties on imports was the opposite kind of policy.

So the protection came off. In 1980 the government withdrew the tariff advantage for locally assembled cars, and with it the reason those cars were assembled here at all. Within the year, all four plants — Ford, Cycle & Carriage, Nissan and Volvo — stopped assembling. Ford left Singapore altogether. Cycle & Carriage turned its Hillview plant into a respray and service centre, and when the area was rezoned for housing, sold the land for terrace homes. The Bukit Timah Ford building went to a property group, lost half its floor area to a condominium in the 1990s, and survived as a monument only because of the seventy minutes in 1942 when Percival sat at that table.

## Forty years of importing

For the next four decades Singapore made no cars. The motor trade became a trade in the literal sense: importing finished vehicles, distributing them, servicing them, and — as ownership was deliberately capped through the Certificate of Entitlement system from 1990 — reselling the right to own one. A new car in Singapore is among the most expensive in the world, but essentially none of that money is paid to anyone who built it here.

The selling side left better buildings behind than the making side did, though not always as themselves. Malayan Motors, a Wearne Brothers company — the same family that had been Ford's first Singapore agent back in 1911 — ran a Swan & Maclaren-designed showroom at 14 Orchard Road from 1927, with Rover, Rolls-Royce, Morris and Studebaker on the floor. It made its last sale there in August 1980, the same month the assembly plants lost their protection, and the building went on to serve as an industry association's offices and then a private college. The 1928 shophouse block immediately beside it sold furniture and radios rather than cars, but it is the one that was given a full heritage restoration: acquired by the state and reopened in 2019 as Temasek Shophouse, a philanthropy hub with a café and event spaces open to the public.

## The plant in Jurong

Hyundai's Innovation Centre Singapore, which opened in the Jurong Innovation District in 2023, is a deliberate exception, and a deliberately different one. It can build around 30,000 electric vehicles a year — the Ioniq 5, including a driverless robotaxi version, with the Ioniq 6 to follow — which is small by the standards of a normal car plant. It is heavily automated, organised into flexible assembly "cells" rather than a single moving line, run against a digital simulation of itself, and topped with a 618-metre test track on the roof. Hyundai and the government describe it less as a factory than as a place to work out how cars might be made in future.

![A white Hyundai Ioniq 5 electric car, three-quarter front view](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/4/4d/Hyundai_Ioniq_5.jpg)

*The Ioniq 5, the model assembled at Hyundai's Jurong plant. (Photo: TTTNIS / Wikimedia Commons, released under Creative Commons Zero; this car photographed overseas.)*

That framing is the point. The assembly plants of the 1960s and 70s were low-skill lines kept alive by a tax on the alternative; they were exactly the kind of protected, labour-intensive work Singapore spent the following decades moving away from. The Jurong plant is capital-intensive, automation-heavy and pitched as research — the kind of manufacturing Singapore now says it wants. Cars are being built in Singapore again, but on terms almost opposite to the ones that applied the last time.

**Where it fits in the bigger story:** Local car assembly is a neat measure of how Singapore has chosen to make things. It flourished briefly when a tariff made it worthwhile, ended the moment the government decided tariffs were not how a small trading economy should grow, and has come back only in a form — small, automated, sold as research — that matches the kind of industry the country now wants its name on.
