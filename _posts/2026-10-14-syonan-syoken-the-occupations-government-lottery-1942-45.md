On the afternoon of 10 September 1942, seven months into the Japanese Occupation, a crowd gathered in the old Victoria Theatre to watch a lottery being drawn. Women nominated by the different communities had been chosen to turn the drums, and a million one-dollar tickets were narrowed down to 62 winners. The first prize of $100,000 went to a 33-year-old banker from Kuala Lumpur who had bought 223 tickets. It was the first of more than thirty government lotteries the Japanese military administration put on sale in Singapore and Malaya, and it was announced from the start as a way to take money back out of people's pockets.

## "Absorbing excess purchasing power"

<div style="float: right; max-width: 300px; width: 44%; margin: 0.25em 0 1em 1.5em;">
<img src="/assets/images/syonan-shimbun-1942-09-11-page-1.jpg" alt="The front page of the Syonan Times of 11 September 1942, with a photograph of the lottery draw at top right and the results below it" style="width: 100%; display: block; border-radius: 4px;">
<em style="display: block; font-size: 0.8em; margin-top: 0.5em;">The front page of the Syonan Times, 11 September 1942. The photograph at top right shows the draw of the Syonan Syoken the day before, with the results printed below it. (Singapore Press Holdings, via NewspaperSG, National Library Board Singapore, public domain)</em>
</div>

The lottery was first announced on 29 May 1942. According to a Domei news report in the Syonan Times, the Finance Bureau of the military administration would "shortly conduct a $1,000,000 lottery" with "the dual purpose of increasing revenue and absorbing excess purchasing power". The tickets would cost $1 each and be sold at the City Hall and at the branches of the Yokohama Specie Bank and the Bank of Taiwan. The report gave its reasons plainly. The lottery was "intended to absorb spending money and inflationary tendencies", which it blamed on the British defence building of the last months before the fall and on speculation by Chinese merchants who had pulled their money out of the stock market.

The announcement came at a hard moment for the city's money. In the same weeks, the Chinese community was being made to raise the [$50 million "gift"](/2026/10/08/the-50-million-gift-of-1942/) demanded by the military, and on 25 June it handed over a cheque that included $22 million borrowed from the Yokohama Specie Bank. The same bank was named as the place where lottery winners would be paid. At the same time the administration was paying its way with its own new notes, the currency that came to be called [banana money](/2026/09/19/banana-money-the-wartime-currency-that-inflated-to-nothing/), printed in ever larger amounts. A lottery that took in a million of those dollars and paid out only part of them was one small way of drawing some of that money back.

## How Syonan Syoken worked

<div style="float: left; max-width: 300px; width: 44%; margin: 0.25em 1.5em 1em 0;">
<img src="/assets/images/syonan-shimbun-1942-06-23-page-3.jpg" alt="Page 3 of the Syonan Times of 23 June 1942, a page of notices and advertisements" style="width: 100%; display: block; border-radius: 4px;">
<em style="display: block; font-size: 0.8em; margin-top: 0.5em;">Page 3 of the Syonan Times, 23 June 1942. A notice for the Syonan Syoken, the government lottery, runs in the advertisement columns, on the same page as a notice to Chinese contributors to the $50 million gift. (Singapore Press Holdings, via NewspaperSG, National Library Board Singapore, public domain)</em>
</div>

The government lottery was called Syonan Syoken. A notice in the paper on 1 July announced that the tickets were on sale from that day, and an advertisement later that month put the offer simply: "Do you want $100,000 or $50,000 or $10,000 or $1,000? Buy a ticket in our $1,000,000 government lottery and try your luck." There were 62 prizes, one of $100,000, one of $50,000, ten of $10,000 and fifty of $1,000, worth $300,000 in all. The paper was asked to make it clear that winners would pay no tax, and that all prizes would be paid in cash at the local office of the Yokohama Specie Bank.

![A black and white postcard of a pair of classical buildings joined by a tall clock tower, with cars parked in front](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/e/e6/Victoria_Theatre_and_Victoria_Memorial_Hall_-_c_1930.jpg)

*The Victoria Theatre and Victoria Memorial Hall on a postcard of about 1930. The lottery draws of the Occupation were held in the theatre. (Unknown author, public domain, via Wikimedia Commons)*

The draw was first set for 10 August at the former Victoria Theatre, which had been renamed for the Occupation, and was later moved to 10 September. By then, according to the head of the finance department, a Mr Hara, the whole million tickets had been sold "within a period of only two months". In a speech before the draw he said the lottery had been "schemed and decided soon after the termination of hostilities in Malaya", thanked "the three communities in Malaya, namely the Chinese, Indians and the Malays" for their co-operation, and promised that the money would be "spent for most fitting causes such as the relief of distressed people, the improvement of sanitary conditions and other suitable social services".

<div style="float: right; max-width: 300px; width: 44%; margin: 0.25em 0 1em 1.5em;">
<img src="/assets/images/syonan-shimbun-1942-09-13-page-1.jpg" alt="The front page of the Syonan Times of 13 September 1942, with a story about the first prize winner and his photograph in the centre columns" style="width: 100%; display: block; border-radius: 4px;">
<em style="display: block; font-size: 0.8em; margin-top: 0.5em;">The front page of the Syonan Times, 13 September 1942, with the story of the first prize winner, S. P. Letchuman Chettiar, and his photograph in the centre columns. (Singapore Press Holdings, via NewspaperSG, National Library Board Singapore, public domain)</em>
</div>

The winners were reported over the following days. The first prize went to S. P. Letchuman Chettiar of Ampang Street in Kuala Lumpur, who had bought 223 tickets, and a few days later the paper reported that he had given $7,500 of it to a fund for wounded and sick Japanese soldiers. The second prize was won by a Sikh police constable in Negri Sembilan. One of the $10,000 third prizes went to a dhoby shop on Exeter Road, where it was shared by ten people, most of the luck said to have come from the shop's cook.

On 3 October the Syonan Times reported what would happen to the $700,000 left over. It would be split into four equal parts, for the Chinese Society to help the poor, for leper asylums and mental hospitals, for the Labour Control Bureau and for the Health Department's preventive work. The official who announced it, a Mr S. Nakahira of the finance department, said $700,000 was "not a very large sum" for so many organisations and asked the people of Malaya to support "any further lotteries".

## Konan Saiken: a lottery every month

<div style="float: left; max-width: 300px; width: 44%; margin: 0.25em 1.5em 1em 0;">
<img src="https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/a/a8/JapLotteryTicket-SIN-Occupation_SINMuseum_20171213_04.jpg/1280px-JapLotteryTicket-SIN-Occupation_SINMuseum_20171213_04.jpg" alt="A cream-coloured lottery ticket with large Chinese characters across the top, a Japanese flag in a round frame in the centre, and short columns of Chinese characters down each side" style="width: 100%; display: block; border-radius: 4px;">
<em style="display: block; font-size: 0.8em; margin-top: 0.5em;">A ticket for the 9th draw of Konan Saiken. The characters down the left side read "health facilities" and those down the right "refugee relief". (Malai Gunseikanbu, photographed at the National Museum of Singapore by Zenwort, public domain, via Wikimedia Commons)</em>
</div>

There were further lotteries, and they came every month. In 1943 the administration began a Malaya-wide lottery, Konan Saiken, a name that roughly means "lottery for the rise of the South". Its tickets still cost $1, and the first three monthly issues of 300,000 tickets each were reported to have sold out within a few days, with tickets bought by the public changing hands at a premium. From the fourth issue, in August 1943, each lottery was raised to 400,000 tickets, half of them for Singapore, with 3,239 prizes worth $240,000 and a first prize of $50,000. Each ticket carried the stated purpose of the money, printed in Chinese characters down its two sides: health facilities on one side and relief for refugees on the other.

Winners were often reported giving some of their prize away. The winner of an early $50,000 first prize, a public works employee in Malacca, gave $5,000 to charity, and in October 1943 the staff of a Syonan municipal office who had shared a prize gave $600 to the Japanese Red Cross. The draws were held at the same hall, and in May 1943 the paper announced that those who could not get in would be able to hear the results on loudspeakers outside.

## What a prize was worth

![A blue and white banknote reading The Japanese Government Ten Dollars, with a bunch of bananas on a banana tree at right](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/c/c5/Ten_dollar_note_issued_by_the_Japanese_Government_during_the_occupation_of_Malaya%2C_North_Borneo%2C_Sarawak_and_Brunei_%281942%2C_obverse%29.jpg/1280px-Ten_dollar_note_issued_by_the_Japanese_Government_during_the_occupation_of_Malaya%2C_North_Borneo%2C_Sarawak_and_Brunei_%281942%2C_obverse%29.jpg)

*A $10 note issued by the Japanese government for Malaya in 1942. Its banana design gave the whole currency its nickname. (Scanned by Jacklee, public domain, via Wikimedia Commons)*

A lottery prize was a fixed sum in a currency that kept losing value. Banana money was issued at par with the Straits dollar in 1942, but by August 1945 the administration had put out something like $4,000 million of it, against a pre-war circulation for the whole region of about $220 million. According to a National Archives oral history quoted in the National Library's BiblioAsia journal, an egg that cost 3 cents before the war cost $100 by the end of the Occupation, and a kati of pork that had cost 30 cents cost $1,500. A $100,000 prize in September 1942 was a fortune. A $50,000 prize two years later bought far less, and by 1945 the administration had introduced $100 and $1,000 notes.

<div style="float: right; max-width: 300px; width: 44%; margin: 0.25em 0 1em 1.5em;">
<img src="/assets/images/syonan-shimbun-1945-08-02-page-2.jpg" alt="Page 2 of the Syonan Shimbun of 2 August 1945, a page of news and notices, with a lottery notice at top right" style="width: 100%; display: block; border-radius: 4px;">
<em style="display: block; font-size: 0.8em; margin-top: 0.5em;">Page 2 of the Syonan Shimbun, 2 August 1945. The notice at top right, "Sale of Government Lottery Tickets", announces the 27th issue of Konan Saiken, to be drawn on 5 September. (Singapore Press Holdings, via NewspaperSG, National Library Board Singapore, public domain)</em>
</div>

The lotteries went on to the end. On 1 June 1945 the 25th issue of Konan Saiken went on sale, now 500,000 tickets, still at $1 each, with 53,738 winning tickets. Beside it ran a second "relief lottery", Engo Saiken, at $5 a ticket, sold at banks, theatres, the Great World and New World amusement parks, community associations and pawnshops. On 2 August the paper carried the notice for the 27th issue of Konan Saiken. The drawing was set for 5 September 1945, and prizes were to be paid between 10 September and 31 October by the Nanpo Kaihatsu Kinko, the Japanese development bank that issued the banana notes, with the warning that "no prize money will be paid after the date fixed".

Japan surrendered on 15 August, and British troops landed back in Singapore on 5 September, the day the 27th draw was due. In September the British Military Administration declared banana money worthless, so even a prize that had been paid was paid in notes that no longer bought anything. The National Museum of Singapore, which holds several of the tickets, notes that many people who bought them reported that prizes were never paid, and that the money went to fund the war and the military administration. That account comes from the people who bought the tickets, and the published accounts of where the money went come from the administration itself, so neither can be fully checked today.

<div style="clear: both;"></div>

**Why it matters today:** Syonan Syoken is a small part of the Occupation's history, but it shows how a government lottery can serve more than one purpose at once. The administration said openly that it wanted to raise money and to soak up the cash in people's hands, and it printed its good causes on every ticket, while the currency the prizes were paid in kept losing its value. Singapore Pools was set up in 1968 to give people a legal alternative to illegal gambling. Its surplus now goes to the Tote Board, a statutory board that funds charities and community projects and reports its grants publicly each year. The wartime lottery is a reminder of why that kind of public accounting matters when the proceeds of a lottery are said to go to good causes.
