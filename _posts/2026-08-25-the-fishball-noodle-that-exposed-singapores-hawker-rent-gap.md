In July 2015, a 24-year-old hawker named Douglas Ng went to a tender briefing for a new government-run hawker centre in Bukit Panjang and came away furious. His handmade fishball noodles, he was told, had to sell for a minimum of $2.70. A stall selling pasta at the same centre could charge a minimum of $5.80. Ng did the math out loud on Facebook: at $2.70 a bowl, after ingredients and labour, he was clearing about fifty cents of profit.

![Golden Mile Food Centre, Singapore, December 2005](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/2/21/Golden_Mile_Food_Centre%2C_Dec_05.JPG)

*Golden Mile Food Centre on Beach Road, photographed in December 2005 — the hawker centre where Douglas Ng's stall, The Fishball Story, first opened in 2013. (Photo: Sengkang / Wikimedia Commons, copyrighted free use)*

Ng's post, later picked up by the Singapore food outlet Makansutra, put the grievance plainly: "They say my fishball noodle must be sold at a minimum of $2.70 as there are poor and needy people around. I understand and can accommodate. But they say if you sell pasta, you can sell at minimum of $5.80." It wasn't that Ng objected to keeping food affordable — he said outright that he understood and could work with that. What stung was that "affordable" seemed to apply to some cuisines and not others.

The dispute was about more than one man's bowl of noodles. Ng, then running The Fishball Story out of Golden Mile Food Centre, had entered NTUC Foodfare's open tender for a stall at the incoming Bukit Panjang Hawker Centre — one of more than 300 bids competing for 32 stalls. He wasn't selected. NTUC Foodfare later explained its process publicly: shortlisted bidders were scored on a system that weighted proposed rent at only 40%, with the remaining 60% covering food quality, variety, pricing, operating hours, and experience. Losing wasn't simply a matter of Ng's price.

But the price-floor complaint pointed at something real and structural underneath it — one that a single tender result doesn't fully explain.

![Bowl of dry fishball mee pok, Singapore](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/0/01/FishBallMeePok.jpg)

*A bowl of dry fishball mee pok, the dish at the centre of Douglas Ng's 2015 pricing dispute. (Photo: Lai Seet Ying / Wikimedia Commons, CC BY-SA 3.0)*

Singapore's hawker centres run on two very different rent structures, depending on who holds the lease. Hawkers who have kept their stalls since the original waves of government hawker resettlement decades ago pay heavily subsidized rents, often carried over with the stall itself. Newer hawkers, competing through open tender, pay whatever the bidding process sets. A September 2023 parliamentary reply from then-Minister for Sustainability and the Environment Grace Fu put real numbers to the gap: subsidized legacy rents at the time ran between $192 and $384 a month, while the median rent for a non-subsidized stall was $1,250 a month — three to six times as much, depending on which end of the subsidized range you compare it to. The same reply noted that the share of subsidized stalls had fallen from roughly 40% to roughly 30% over the preceding decade, as pioneer-generation hawkers retire and their stalls revert to market-rate tender.

That gap is the real reason "why can't you charge $2.50 like the auntie next door" doesn't hold up as a comparison. The auntie next door may be paying a few hundred dollars a month in rent. The newcomer trying to break into the trade could be paying five or six times that before a single bowl is sold.

Ng didn't leave the trade over it. The Fishball Story moved from Golden Mile Food Centre to a food court at NUS's University Town, then to the hawker-style food park Timbre+, picking up a Michelin Bib Gourmand recognition in 2016 along the way. By 2017 the stall had settled at Block 77, Circuit Road, in Macpherson — and as of the most recent public records, it's still there.

The tension Ng's post surfaced in 2015 hasn't gone away; if anything, it has sharpened. Cooked-food prices at hawker centres rose 6.1% in 2023 — the steepest increase since 2008, according to the Singapore Department of Statistics — and fishball noodles specifically climbed from a national average of $3.77 in 2022 to $4.13 in 2023.

![Entrance to Marine Parade Central Market and Food Centre, Singapore](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/f/fc/84_Marine_Parade_Central_Market_and_Food_Centre.jpg)

*Marine Parade Central Market and Food Centre, photographed in August 2025 — site of a record-setting stall tender bid a year earlier. (Photo: Kbseah / Wikimedia Commons, CC BY-SA 4.0)*

The rent side of the equation has kept climbing too. In August 2024, a single stall at Marine Parade Central Market and Food Centre drew a winning tender bid of $10,158 a month — a record that prompted Workers' Party MP Louis Chua to file a parliamentary motion that November calling for rental caps and a shift away from bid-driven tenders toward a "price-quality" scoring method, echoing, on a national scale, the kind of system NTUC Foodfare had already used in a smaller way back in 2015.

The government's response has moved in that direction, if incrementally. The National Environment Agency introduced a staggered rent-adjustment mechanism for hawker centre tenders in November 2024: on a stall's first lease renewal, rent moves only halfway toward the newly assessed market rate, with the full adjustment deferred to the second renewal, intended to blunt the kind of rent-spike bidding that produced the Marine Parade Central record. Separately, the Hawkers' Succession Scheme, launched in 2022, pairs retiring hawkers who have no family successor with vetted newcomers through a three-month apprenticeship, an attempt to pass on a stall's subsidized-rent status rather than let it revert automatically to the open market. The 2025 SG60 Budget added a one-off $600 rental support payment for every active hawker stallholder and a $1 billion, 20-to-30-year commitment to hawker centre infrastructure.

None of it closes the fundamental gap Ng pointed to in 2015 — a hawker on a decades-old subsidized lease and a hawker on a fresh market-rate tender are still, structurally, playing different games. But a decade on, it is at least being argued about in Parliament, rather than dismissed as one young hawker's Facebook complaint.

**Why it matters today:** Ng's fishball noodles never made him famous the way a price-cap protest might have — his stall's Michelin recognition did that instead. But the pricing rule he pushed back against in 2015 named a real fault line in how Singapore keeps hawker food cheap: not through some uniform national bargain, but by asking whichever hawker holds the newest lease to absorb most of the difference.
