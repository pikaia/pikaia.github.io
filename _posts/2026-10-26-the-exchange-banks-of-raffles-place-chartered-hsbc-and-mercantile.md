For a century, the money that moved Singapore's trade passed through a few buildings around one square. The Chartered Bank, the Hongkong and Shanghai Bank and the Mercantile Bank of India were British "exchange banks," set up to finance trade between Asia and Europe, and from the 1850s they made Raffles Place the colony's banking quarter. Their domed and columned offices were replaced, one after another, by the towers that stand there now, and two of those towers were built by the same banks on the same corners.

## What an exchange bank did

Singapore's first banks, which I wrote about in [an earlier post](/2026/09/28/before-the-banks-how-singapores-merchants-kept-their-money/), were branches of banks based in India. The exchange banks that followed were British banks chartered to work across the East. Their main business was trade finance: a merchant shipping rubber, tin or pepper to London could sell the bank a bill of exchange, a written order for payment due when the goods arrived, and be paid in Singapore straight away. Because the bills were paid in pounds in London and in dollars here, the banks dealt in foreign exchange every day, and their rates set the price of money in the port.

Until the government began issuing its own currency notes in 1899, the banks also issued banknotes, the Chartered and the Mercantile among them, as I described in [the post on the Straits dollar](/2026/10/09/the-straits-dollar-singapores-first-money-of-its-own-1903/). When the Chartered Bank laid the foundation stone of a new building in 1953, the Governor, Sir John Nicoll, credited the exchange banks with the underwriting, issue and service of government and municipal loans as well.

## The Mercantile Bank arrives first

The Mercantile Bank was founded in Bombay in 1853, received a royal charter in 1857 as the Mercantile Bank of India, London and China, and moved its head office to London. It came to Singapore in the mid-1850s. In 1961 the Straits Times noted that the bank and its predecessors had operated in Singapore for 105 years. By the late 1920s it had its own building on Raffles Place.

## The Chartered Bank, 1859

The Chartered Bank of India, Australia and China received its royal charter from Queen Victoria in 1853. It opened its first Singapore branch in leased premises on the square on the 19th of February 1859. The square had been Commercial Square until the year before, when it was renamed Raffles Place.

![A colour print of a large, ornate three-storey bank building with arched windows on a street corner](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/8/82/Singapore._Chartered_Bank_Buildings._Battery_Road_LCCN2017657651.jpg/1280px-Singapore._Chartered_Bank_Buildings._Battery_Road_LCCN2017657651.jpg)

*The Chartered Bank Buildings on Battery Road, on a photochrom print published by Photoglob around 1900. (Photoglob Co., Library of Congress, public domain, via Wikimedia Commons)*

In 1895 the bank moved into a building of its own, a three-storey palazzo at the corner of Battery Road and Flint Street designed by the architects Swan and Maclaren, which cost about $60,000. Within twenty years it had outgrown that too. In 1916 it moved to new premises at the corner of Battery Road and Bonham Street, facing Raffles Place, a four-storey building with a dome that also housed the Singapore Chamber of Commerce. The old palazzo was let to other tenants and later became the Bank of China.

## The Hongkong and Shanghai Bank, 1877

The Hongkong and Shanghai Bank was founded in Hong Kong in 1865, and it did business in Singapore through an agent from that year. It opened its own office on Collyer Quay in December 1877. A hundred years later, in 1977, the Straits Times reported that the bank was celebrating the centenary of its "special agency," which had in fact been a branch.

![A tall, richly decorated bank building with turrets and gables beside a waterfront and a pier](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/8/81/Photographic_Views_of_Singapore_Plate_05_Hongkong_and_Shanghai_Bank.jpg)

*The Hongkong and Shanghai Bank's building at the corner of Collyer Quay and Battery Road, around 1900. (G. R. Lambert and Co., public domain, via Wikimedia Commons)*

In 1890 the bank bought the corner site where Battery Road meets Collyer Quay, and in October 1894 it opened a three-storey building there, also by Swan and Maclaren, in a mix of Gothic, Renaissance and Baroque styles. It was pulled down in 1919, and its replacement, the Hongkong Bank Chambers, was finished in the mid-1920s.

## Rubber, tin and war

The early 20th century was the exchange banks' busiest time. Rubber and tin from Malaya passed through Singapore to the world, and the banks financed it. Raffles Place filled with motor cars, and other banks arrived around the square, among them the Bank of Taiwan, which took over the Chartered Bank's old palazzo in 1916.

The war ended that world. Raffles Place was hit in the first Japanese air raid on Singapore, on the 8th of December 1941. After the British surrender in February 1942, the British banks were closed and their staff interned. The Yokohama Specie Bank set up in the Hongkong and Shanghai Bank's building, and later in the occupation the Japanese administration paid out part of the deposits of the "enemy banks," as I described in [the post on the Yokohama Specie Bank](/2026/10/05/the-yokohama-specie-bank-japans-bank-in-prewar-singapore/). The British banks reopened after the Japanese surrender in 1945.

## From domes to towers

The banks began to rebuild in the 1950s. On the 31st of July 1953 the Governor laid the foundation stone of a new seven-storey building for the Chartered Bank, which he called "as clear an indication as there can be of the Bank's confidence in the future of Singapore." Into a cavity beneath the stone went newly minted Coronation coins, the previous day's newspapers and the bank's annual reports.

In 1959 the Hongkong and Shanghai Bank bought the Mercantile Bank, which kept its own name for many years afterwards, and in 1984 the Hongkong bank sold it to Citibank. The Mercantile's name is no longer seen in Singapore. The Chartered Bank merged with the Standard Bank in 1969 to form Standard Chartered.

In 1975 the Hongkong Bank Chambers were pulled down, and the bank moved for a while into the Ocean Building next door. Its new tower at the corner of Collyer Quay and Battery Road opened in January 1983. The Standard Chartered Building, a 44-storey tower at Six Battery Road, on the corner the bank had occupied since 1916, was opened on the 24th of October 1984.

From 1987 to 1997 I worked for Security Pacific, an American bank whose Singapore office was in the HSBC Building. For ten years I went to work on the corner where the Hongkong and Shanghai Bank had stood since the 1890s, without knowing how long it had been there.

Both banks have since moved their main Singapore offices to Marina Bay, but the HSBC Building and Six Battery Road still stand where their first buildings rose.

**Where it fits in the bigger story:** The exchange banks were the plumbing of colonial Singapore's trade, and Raffles Place was where that plumbing met. The square's buildings changed several times, from palazzos to domes to towers, but the banks' addresses changed far less. The towers that two of them built still stand on the corners they took in the 19th and early 20th centuries, which makes the oldest part of the financial district one of its most continuous.
