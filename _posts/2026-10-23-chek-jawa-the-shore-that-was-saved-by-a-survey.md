In December 2000 a botanist named Joseph Lai was guiding a group of schoolgirls around Pulau Ubin when the tide went out at Chek Jawa, the island's eastern tip. The flats it left behind were alive with sea stars, sea anemones, sponges, seagrass and crabs. Lai later found out that the shore was due to be reclaimed. Over the next year volunteers, scientists and more than a thousand members of the public came to see it, and on 20 December 2001, days before work was due to begin, the Ministry of National Development announced that the reclamation would be deferred. Chek Jawa is still there today, a boardwalk away from the Ubin jetty, and it is still officially a deferment rather than a reserve.

## The island and the point

![A map of Pulau Ubin off Singapore's north-east coast, marking Ubin village and its jetty on the south coast, Chek Jawa at the eastern tip, and the Changi Point Ferry Terminal across the strait](/assets/images/chek-jawa-map.png)

*Pulau Ubin and Chek Jawa. Positions are approximate. (Map data © OpenStreetMap contributors)*

Pulau Ubin lies in the Johor Strait off Changi, about 10 square kilometres of hills, old quarries and villages, ten minutes by bumboat from Changi Point. Its name is usually traced to a word for squared stone, and from the 1840s Chinese quarry companies cut granite there. At its peak in the 1950s to the 1970s the island had about 2,000 residents. By 2001 fewer than 200 were left.

Chek Jawa is the point at the island's eastern end. Although it covers only about one square kilometre, it holds six distinct habitats: coastal forest, mangrove, a sandy beach, sand flats with a seagrass lagoon, coral rubble, and a tiny rocky island called Pulau Sekudu, or Frog Island. A marine biologist at the National University of Singapore, Tan Koh Siang, has described it as one of the few estuarine ecosystems left in Singapore.

![A two-storey Tudor-style house with black timber framing, white walls and a steep red-tiled roof, on a lawn with tall pine trees behind](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/c/c0/Chek_Jawa%2C_Aug_07.JPG/1280px-Chek_Jawa%2C_Aug_07.JPG)

*House No. 1, built in the 1930s for a British chief surveyor, after its restoration as the Chek Jawa visitor centre, August 2007. (Sengkang, CC BY-SA 3.0, via Wikimedia Commons)*

On the point stands an unusual building. House No. 1 was built in the 1930s as a holiday house for the colonial chief surveyor, Langdon Williams, in the style of an English Tudor cottage, with black timber framing, a steep tiled roof, a fireplace and corner stones cut from Ubin granite. After the British left it became a local family's weekend home. Its last private owner was the businessman Lee Thor Seng, and the government acquired it in the 1990s.

## The plan

Singapore has been adding land from the sea since the 1820s. Its total land area grew from 581.5 square kilometres in 1960 to 682.3 square kilometres in 2001, and to 744.3 square kilometres by 2025, according to the Singapore Land Authority's figures. The story of how that began is told in an [earlier post on the first reclamations at Telok Ayer](/2026/09/29/when-telok-ayer-street-was-the-beach-singapores-first-land-reclamations/).

In 1992 the Urban Redevelopment Authority approved a plan to reclaim the eastern tip of Pulau Ubin, including Chek Jawa. The plan appeared again in the 1997 development guide plan and the Master Plan 1998. The new land was to be "reserve land", eventually to be used for military training, and the reclamation was scheduled for the end of 2001.

## The survey

Before the reclamation began, the National Parks Board asked the Raffles Museum of Biodiversity Research to make a salvage collection: a record of what lived on the shore, with photographs and specimens, before it disappeared. The museum's teams surveyed Chek Jawa over six days in May, July and August 2001.

On 11 May 2001 the URA held a public forum on its Concept Plan 2001, chaired by the Minister for National Development, Mah Bow Tan. Joseph Lai spoke up about Chek Jawa, and it was the first time the issue had been raised in public. The press picked it up, and letters followed from nature lovers, teachers and Ubin residents, many of them arguing that the shore would make an outdoor classroom.

On 27 July 2001 the URA replied in a letter to the Straits Times that the reclamation would go ahead as planned. It explained that the land was part of a long-term plan to make sure Singapore had enough space for military training. It cited a study commissioned by the Housing and Development Board which had found that the seagrass was patchy and that there did not seem to be a resident population of dugongs, the sea mammals that feed on it, and it said that NParks would transplant affected plants to other parts of the island.

The volunteers carried on. A group led by Joseph Lai went on documenting the shore, and the Raffles Museum and the Nature Society (Singapore) ran public walks there. On 20 October 2001 more than a thousand people turned up. Eric Lin, a filmmaker, recorded the survey for a documentary he called *Remember Chek Jawa*. Over the second half of the year the URA received field data and detailed reports on the shore's biodiversity, along with petitions, leaflets, talks and photo exhibitions.

## The deferral

![Visitors in hats and backpacks on a railed boardwalk beside a muddy shore with stranded branches, with mangrove trees behind](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/f/f6/Chek_Jawa_12%2C_Aug_07.JPG/1280px-Chek_Jawa_12%2C_Aug_07.JPG)

*Visitors on the boardwalk above the mudflats, August 2007. (Sengkang, CC BY-SA 3.0, via Wikimedia Commons)*

On the afternoon of 20 December 2001, Mah Bow Tan met representatives of nature and biodiversity groups and members of the public. The same day his ministry issued a short press release. "The Ministry of National Development (MND) has decided to defer the reclamation works at Tanjung Chek Jawa originally slated for end of 2001," it began. "This will allow MND to discuss with relevant experts on how best the marine life there can be protected." The ministry said it had considered all the public submissions and consulted the URA, NParks, the Housing and Development Board and marine biologists from the National University of Singapore and other institutions. It also asked the public not to rush to the shore: "there is no longer an urgent need to rush to visit Chek Jawa."

A second statement on 14 January 2002 went further. Reclamation works on Pulau Ubin would be deferred "for as long as the island is not required for development". Reclaiming only a small area would not be cost-effective, the ministry said, and scientists from the National Institute of Education, the Raffles Museum and the Nature Society had recommended that any reclamation be kept very small so as not to harm the shore. NParks would set up a committee with the museum, the Nature Society and other experts to plan how to look after it.

## After the reprieve

The news brought crowds, and the crowds became a problem of their own. NParks brought in a booking system, marked routes and compulsory guides, and asked visitors to wear rubber boots and to touch nothing. About 20,000 people joined the guided walks between 2002 and 2007.

Then came the weather. Heavy rain in December 2006 poured fresh water over the flats and upset their salinity, and much of the marine life died, including sea anemones, sea stars and sponges. In late March 2007 NParks suspended the tours to let the shore recover.

Chek Jawa reopened in July 2007 with new facilities costing S$6.2 million: a 1.1-kilometre boardwalk over the mangroves and the flats, a 20-metre viewing tower, and a visitor centre in House No. 1, which the URA had gazetted for conservation in 2003 and which had been restored from 2005. The restoration won one of the URA's Architectural Heritage Awards that year. Visitors no longer needed a guide to walk the boardwalk. Three years later, on 26 May 2010, two ships collided off Changi and spilled about 2,500 tonnes of crude oil, which reached the shore at Chek Jawa, and volunteers were recruited to help clean it.

Chek Jawa Wetlands is managed today by NParks under the Parks and Trees Act, and the rules there forbid collecting any plant or animal, fishing and swimming. It is not one of Singapore's four gazetted nature reserves, though, and the land is still held under the deferral of 2002 rather than set aside for good. Government land-use plans in 2013 still marked Chek Jawa and parts of Pulau Ubin as reserve sites, and later master plans have kept development of the island as a long-term possibility. The boardwalk, the tower and the old house are open to anyone who takes a bumboat across from Changi, and at a low spring tide the flats are still there to see.

[See more photos related to this post →](/gallery/chek-jawa-the-shore-that-was-saved-by-a-survey/)

**Where it fits in the bigger story:** Singapore has been reclaiming land for two centuries, and much of its natural coastline has been built over in that time. Chek Jawa's reprieve came after a botanist's chance visit, a museum's salvage survey and months of documentation, which put the shore's value on record while the decision was still open, and after the government weighed that record and deferred the plan. More than twenty years on, it is still a deferment rather than a reserve, and one of the few places in Singapore where a natural shore of this kind can still be seen.
