Order breakfast in a Singapore kopitiam and it arrives in a form that has barely changed in a century: two soft-boiled eggs cracked into a saucer, with dark soy sauce and a shake of white pepper; kaya toast; and a cup of kopi or tea. In 2019 the National Heritage Board listed this "Traditional Breakfast of Kaya and Kopi" in Singapore's inventory of intangible cultural heritage. Yet for roughly fifty of the years that Singaporeans were eating it, the two eggs in the saucer sat at the centre of one of modern nutrition's longest-running scares — officially limited, warned against, and portioned out by the week. The guidance swung, swung back, and swung again. The breakfast never moved.

  function splitLongSentence(text, offset, duration) {

## Two eggs in a saucer

The eggs are not boiled, exactly. They are steeped: room-temperature eggs go into a covered pot or enamel mug, boiling water is poured over them, and they sit in the falling heat — home cooks time it at anything from five and a half to ten minutes, depending on the vessel — until the whites are just set and the yolks still run. Cracked into a saucer, they are seasoned the local way. The British breakfast this descends from used salt and pepper; Hainanese cooks substituted dark soy sauce for the salt, and the substitution stuck.

Why two eggs, and not one or three? The honest answer is that nobody ever wrote it down. The Heritage Board's listing records "two soft boiled eggs" as the form of the dish, and stops there. The explanations that circulate — one egg looks mean, a pair balances the toast — are folk reasoning after the fact. The two-egg serving is a documented custom with no documented origin, which may be the most Singaporean thing about it: it does not need a reason, because it has always been done this way.

The toast beside the eggs is charcoal-grilled, crusts trimmed, spread with kaya and butter — or margarine; the official sources split on that too, the Heritage Board's account saying margarine where other descriptions say butter, and diners have never much cared which. And the drink is ordered in a vocabulary of its own: kopi with condensed milk, kopi-o black with sugar, kopi-o kosong with none, and the adjustments — siu dai for less sugar, ga dai for more — that regulars issue as reflexively as their own names.

## The Hainanese coffee shop

The kopitiam — the word marries Malay *kopi*, coffee, to Hokkien *tiam*, shop — was largely a Hainanese creation. Hainanese migrants were in Singapore from 1821, but they arrived later than the other Chinese dialect groups and found many trades already taken. Many went into service as cooks, waiters and kitchen hands in European and Peranakan households, on ships and in hotels — acquiring, in the process, exactly the skills a Western-style breakfast trade would need. Between the 1920s and the 1950s they made the coffee shop their niche, and after 1945, with rents cheap, kopitiams became common across the island.

The documented pioneer still stands at 67 Killiney Road. Kheng Hoe Heng Coffeeshop was founded there in late 1919 by the Hainanese Foo family, known for charcoal-grilled toast and coffee brewed the Hainanese way. In 1992 a former broker, Woon Tek Seng, bought it for S$800,000 and renamed it Killiney Kopitiam; three Hainanese staff from the old shop stayed on to pass on the skills, and in 2008 the shop entered the Singapore Book of Records as the oldest existing Hainanese coffeeshop.

The other founding name took the opposite path, from one stall to a chain. Loi Ah Koon arrived from Hainan in 1926, aged fifteen, worked as a coffee-stall assistant, and set up his own stall at Telok Ayer Basin; the business was formally registered in 1944 — the year Ya Kun still prints on its signs. His wife, who joined him in 1936, perfected the kaya recipe the chain still trades on.

Ya Kun has since gone international — the same set is served from Jakarta to Tokyo. One Tokyo outlet opened in 2021 in the Kasumigaseki Building (it has since closed), and that building carries a personal footnote: it is where I once worked, during my AOTS time in Japan. Loi Ah Koon, roasting beans over firewood at Telok Ayer, could not have imagined his breakfast turning up there.

## Kaya: the jam with two birthplaces

Kaya is an egg jam: eggs, coconut milk, sugar and pandan, cooked slowly into a spread. (The word is also Malay for "rich".) It comes in two regional styles — a green kaya coloured by pandan, associated with Peranakan makers, and a brown one coloured by caramelised sugar, the Hainanese kopitiam style.

Where it came from is a genuine scholarly argument, and the post owes the reader both sides. The older, popular account makes kaya a local adaptation of a Portuguese egg jam, coconut milk standing in for the dairy of the original. The revisionist account, argued by the food historian Khir Johari and by a peer-reviewed article in the journal *Sejarah* in 2024, holds that the Malay *serikaya* family of egg-and-coconut confections predates the Portuguese in Southeast Asia — and may have travelled the other way, from Melaka to Goa to Portugal. The evidence stands in print on both sides; the jam, like the eggs, is unbothered.

## The scare

The case against the egg was built in America. In 1958 the researcher Ancel Keys launched his Seven Countries Study, the foundation of the diet-heart hypothesis linking dietary fat and cholesterol to heart disease. A decade later, in 1968, the American Heart Association issued the recommendation that started the egg's exile: less than 300 milligrams of dietary cholesterol a day, and **no more than three whole eggs a week**. It was the first time a single everyday food had been portioned out like medicine. The US Senate's Dietary Goals echoed the limits in 1977; *Time* magazine put cholesterol on its cover in March 1984; and by 1995 every major American authority — the Heart Association, the national cholesterol programme, the federal dietary guidelines, the food label itself — had converged on the same figure: 300 milligrams a day.

Now weigh the saucer against that budget. The cholesterol in one egg is itself a number that will not sit still: 186 milligrams in the current US Department of Agriculture figure, 213 in its older one, about 200 in a 2021 *Singapore Medical Journal* review, 240 in the Singapore Heart Foundation's table. But take any of them. Two kopitiam eggs deliver somewhere between 372 and 480 milligrams — which is to say, the traditional serving, eaten before nine in the morning, meets or exceeds the entire day's allowance on its own. For fifty years, by the official arithmetic of the scare years, the kopitiam breakfast was over the limit before the toast was finished.

## The long swing back

The science began to move in 1999, when a *JAMA* study following more than 117,000 men and women found no difference in cardiovascular risk between eating one egg a week and one egg a day in healthy people (people with diabetes were the exception in that study). In 2002 the Heart Association quietly dropped its specific egg restriction, though it kept the 300-milligram cap. In 2013 its joint guideline with the American College of Cardiology concluded there was "insufficient evidence" that lowering dietary cholesterol reduces LDL cholesterol at all — and dropped the cap. In February 2015 the federal Dietary Guidelines Advisory Committee went further in words that were widely quoted: cholesterol was "not a nutrient of concern for overconsumption", and the evidence showed no appreciable relationship between the cholesterol people eat and the cholesterol in their blood.

The popular version of that story — "in 2015, America declared eggs fine again" — is too simple, and the record says so. The final 2015–2020 Dietary Guidelines removed the number but kept the caution, advising Americans to eat "as little dietary cholesterol as possible". And the argument did not end. In March 2019 a *JAMA* analysis pooling six American cohorts — 29,615 people followed for a median of seventeen and a half years — reported that each additional 300 milligrams of dietary cholesterol a day was associated with a higher risk of cardiovascular disease and death, and each additional half egg a day with a smaller but real increase (though the egg associations faded once dietary cholesterol itself was accounted for, and the study's critics noted that diet had been measured only once, at the start). In December 2019 the Heart Association's Science Advisory settled where the evidence had landed: no specific numeric target, an emphasis on the whole dietary pattern — healthy people could include up to one whole egg a day, and healthy older people up to two. In 2020 a *BMJ* analysis of three cohorts and a meta-analysis covering 1.72 million people found no association between eating at least one egg a day and cardiovascular disease at all.

These are observational studies — associations, not proofs — and their authors say so. The fair summary is narrower than either camp's headline: a hard cap for forty-five years, then no cap with cautionary wording, then pattern-based advice, with large studies still landing on both sides. The guidance swung for half a century. The saucer did not.

## Singapore kept the old numbers

Singapore's published advice, notably, did not follow the American swing. On HealthHub, the Health Promotion Board's portal, a dietitian's answer updated on 22 December 2021 concedes the modern point — dietary cholesterol, it says, has a "minor effect on serum cholesterol" — and then states the old limits anyway: the Health Promotion Board advises less than 300 milligrams of cholesterol a day, and fewer than four egg yolks a week, with the real concern directed at saturated and trans fats. The Board's My Healthy Plate materials of the same period repeat the four-yolk figure, and the Singapore Heart Foundation's table, citing Ministry of Health guidelines from 2016, keeps the 300-milligram limit too. Whether the Board has formally revised the advice since those 2021 pages could not be confirmed for this post; what is documented is that Singapore's printed guidance long outlasted the American original it was modelled on. Through all of it, Singaporeans went on ordering the two eggs — on the Board's own numbers, a single breakfast saucer holds more than a whole day's advised cholesterol.

## And the kopi-o?

It is fair to ask whether the coffee itself is harmful — and it turns out to have had a scare of its own. Kopitiam coffee is not ordinary coffee to begin with. The Nanyang roast, as the Heritage Board records it, is eight parts beans to two parts sugar and margarine, roasted twenty-five minutes at 180 degrees, with salt added midway and sugar at the end for a caramelised finish (the National Museum's catalogue says butter where the Heritage Board says margarine — the split again). The brew is strained through a cloth sock and poured back and forth between two long-spouted jugs. Even a kopi-o kosong is therefore not quite a plain black coffee: the beans arrived pre-sweetened by the roast.

In 1991 the International Agency for Research on Cancer, the World Health Organization's cancer agency, classified coffee in Group 2B — "possibly carcinogenic to humans" — on limited evidence linking it to bladder cancer. There it sat for twenty-five years. In June 2016, after reviewing more than a thousand studies, the agency moved coffee to Group 3, "not classifiable as to its carcinogenicity" — no conclusive effect at any site, with the 1991 bladder evidence weakened by smoking's confounding role. Group 3 is an absence-of-evidence verdict, not a certificate of safety, and precision matters here. In the same evaluation, the agency classified drinking *very hot* beverages — above 65 degrees — as Group 2A, "probably carcinogenic": the temperature, not the drink. A 2017 umbrella review in the *BMJ*, covering over two hundred meta-analyses, concluded that coffee consumption was "more often associated with benefit than harm", with the largest risk reductions at three to four cups a day — while cautioning that the evidence is observational and mostly of low quality, and that the associations ran the other way in pregnancy.

The live issue in a modern kopi-o is not the coffee but the sugar. The Health Promotion Board's food database figures, as reported in secondary analyses, put a 250-millilitre kopi-o at about 11.7 grams of sugar, against none at all in a kosong; other published tables give different figures for the same drinks, so treat any single number as an approximation with a source attached, not a fact of nature. What is official is the grading: under Nutri-Grade, the Board's own worked examples grade a kopi or teh O kosong A, siu dai B, the standard cup C, and ga dai D. The labels became mandatory on pre-packaged drinks in December 2022 and were extended to freshly prepared drinks — the kopitiam cup included — in December 2023. On government premises, coffee and tea are now served kosong by default, sugar on the side. The kopi-o has not been banned, taxed or portioned by the week; it has been graded, and the grading points at the spoon, not the bean.

## The breakfast today

Singapore eats eggs on a scale the scare never dented. By the Singapore Food Agency's 2024 statistics, the country consumed roughly 2,259 million eggs in a year — on the order of 370 eggs per person — with local farms supplying about a third and the rest imported, mainly from Malaysia and Thailand. That dependence has shown itself exactly once in the breakfast's modern history: in 2004, during the bird flu outbreak, Singapore banned Malaysian poultry imports for six weeks. Killiney Kopitiam, which typically used up to 4,000 fresh eggs a day, received less than half its supply, reserved what eggs it had for kaya, watched prices spike from 12 to 45 cents — and took the soft-boiled eggs off the menu across its fourteen branches. The only thing that has ever actually removed the two eggs from the saucer was not a guideline. It was a shortage.

So the set endures, graded but intact: the eggs steeping in their pot behind the counter, the toast on the grill, the sock going back and forth between the jugs. It has been heritage-listed, cholesterol-capped, carcinogen-listed, de-listed, and Nutri-Graded, and it is still what it was when Kheng Hoe Heng opened in 1919 — because a breakfast, unlike a guideline, is not revised by committee. It is revised by nobody, one morning at a time, by the people who keep ordering it.

**Where it fits in the bigger story:** Singapore's most ordinary breakfast turns out to be a small monument to the difference between custom and advice. The advice was expert, official and — by its own later admission — built on evidence too weak to hold; it governed the egg for fifty years and then withdrew. The custom asked for no evidence at all, and outlasted it. The lesser-known thing is not that the two eggs survived the scare. It is that nobody ever decided they should.
