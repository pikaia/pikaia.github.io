In the 1880s, three hills stood along the waterfront south of Singapore's town: Mount Erskine, Mount Wallich and Mount Palmer. Within fifty years, Mount Wallich and most of Mount Palmer had been blasted, dug up and carted into the sea to make new land, and Mount Erskine has gone too. Today the only reminders are a few street names and one small knoll with a shrine on top.

## A hillier island than it looks

![A shaded relief map of Singapore, with the hills of the centre and west in brown, Bukit Timah Hill and Mount Faber marked, and a box around the city centre](/assets/images/hills-island.png)

*Singapore's terrain today, from elevation data. Browner is higher, and the heights are exaggerated so the relief shows. (Map by Lesser Known Singapore; elevation data from Terrain Tiles on AWS, derived from NASA's SRTM survey)*

Singapore is not flat. Its highest point, Bukit Timah Hill, rises to 164 metres, and ridges run from the centre of the island down to Mount Faber and the southern coast. In the early town, the waterfront to the south was hilly too, but most of those hills are gone. They sat close to the sea at a time when the colony needed earth to fill it, and they stood between the town and the new docks at Tanjong Pagar.

The smallest story belongs to Mount Wallich. In 1822 the Danish botanist Nathaniel Wallich, the superintendent of the Calcutta botanic garden, fell ill on a visit to Singapore and spent about five months recovering in a house called Botany Hall on a hill near the shore. During his stay Stamford Raffles persuaded him to propose a botanic and experimental garden for the settlement, and the hill was later named after him.

Mount Erskine stood to the north of it, behind Telok Ayer Street. It lasted longer than its neighbour: the Straits Times of 25 August 1913 reported that the government had acquired it. Beside it was Ann Siang Hill, which still survives as a small park.

Mount Palmer was the largest of the three, about 119 feet (36 metres) high. It was named after John Palmer, a Calcutta merchant who bought the hill soon after 1819 and built a bungalow on top. When his business ran into trouble, he sold part of it in 1828 to a Parsi, and its slopes became the burial ground of Singapore's Parsi community, so the hill was also known as Mount Parsee or Bukit Parsi. Part of it overlooking the harbour was fortified as Fort Palmer, which by 1864 had five 56-pounder guns. Its later owners included Hoo Ah Kay, the merchant better known as [Whampoa](/2026/09/15/whampoa-the-merchant-who-brought-singapores-first-ice/).

The [Telok Ayer reclamation](/2026/09/29/when-telok-ayer-street-was-the-beach-singapores-first-land-reclamations/), begun in 1879, needed earth, and the nearest earth was in the hills behind the bay. On a Thursday evening in November 1884, according to the Straits Times Weekly Issue, a charge of 1,000 pounds of gunpowder and a few pounds of dynamite was fired in a 50-foot tunnel bored into the face of Mount Wallich, beside the new road to Tanjong Pagar. The explosion made the ground tremble for some distance, and the paper reported that about 50,000 tons of rock and earth were brought down in one blast, broken small enough to be carted away.

The hill was taken down over the following years, and its earth went into the new land behind Raffles Quay. Wallich Street, named in 1899, runs across the site of the vanished hill today.

## Cutting down Mount Palmer

The hills were also in the way. Goods from the [docks at Tanjong Pagar](/2026/09/25/the-private-company-that-ran-singapores-harbour-1864-1905/) had to be carried by bullock cart over or around them to reach the town, and from the early 1870s the removal of Mount Palmer was being suggested. In 1905 Fort Palmer, the last military post in Tanjong Pagar, was demolished, and the hill was cut down to fill the Telok Ayer Basin, the second, larger reclamation offshore. By the time the basin was completed in 1932, most of Mount Palmer had been levelled, except for a small knoll that held the remains of the Parsi burial ground and the back of the Fook Tet Soo Khek temple. The road laid out across the site was named Palmer Battery Road, later shortened to Palmer Road.

## What is left

On that last knoll stands the Keramat Habib Noh, the shrine of a 19th-century Muslim saint, Habib Noh bin Mohammed al-Habshi, with the Masjid Haji Muhammad Salleh, built in 1903, beside it. In 2016, when the Prince Edward MRT station was announced, what remained of the foothills made way for the new station and bus terminal, while the shrine, the mosque and the Fook Tet Soo Khek temple were kept.

The other names are all on street signs: Wallich Street, Erskine Road, Palmer Road and Parsi Road. Ann Siang Hill, the one hill of the group that was not cut down, is now a small park above Club Street, with a boardwalk through the trees.

## After 1965

The largest cutting of hills came after independence. For the East Coast reclamation, which began in 1966 and added 1,525 hectares of land from Bedok to Tanjong Rhu, hills at Siglap, Bedok and Tampines were cut away by bucket-wheel excavators, and conveyor belts carried the earth to a jetty at Bedok, where it was loaded onto barges and taken out to sea.

[See more historical photos related to this post →](/gallery/the-hills-singapore-dug-into-the-sea/)

**Where it fits in the bigger story:** Singapore's reclamation is usually told as land made from the sea. Much of that land was first a hill somewhere else. The waterfront south of the old town lost three hills this way, and their names on today's street signs are the only sign they were ever there.
