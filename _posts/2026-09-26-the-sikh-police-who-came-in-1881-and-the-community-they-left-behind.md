On 26 March 1881 an assistant superintendent and 54 Sikh recruits from Punjab arrived in Singapore to join a police force that was struggling to keep order in a crowded, fast-growing port. By the end of the year the Sikh Police Contingent numbered 165 men. The contingent was disbanded in 1945, but the community that grew up around it, with its own gurdwara on Silat Road, is still in Singapore today.

  var MAX_CHARS = 100;
  function splitLongSentence(text, offset, duration) {
    if (text.length <= MAX_CHARS) return [{ text: text, offset_s: offset, duration_s: duration }];
    var parts = text.split(/(?<=[:;])\s+/);
    var chunks = [];
    parts.forEach(function (p) {
      if (p.length <= MAX_CHARS) { chunks.push(p); return; }
      var words = p.split(' ');
      var cur = '';
      words.forEach(function (w) {
        if ((cur + ' ' + w).trim().length > MAX_CHARS) { chunks.push(cur.trim()); cur = w; }
        else { cur = (cur + ' ' + w).trim(); }
      });
      if (cur) chunks.push(cur.trim());
    });
    var totalChars = chunks.reduce(function (a, c) { return a + c.length; }, 0);
    var t = offset;
    return chunks.map(function (c) {
      var share = duration * (c.length / totalChars);
      var entry = { text: c, offset_s: t, duration_s: share };
      t += share;
      return entry;
    });
  }

  });

## A police force under strain

The first Sikhs recorded in Singapore did not come as policemen. In 1850 two Sikh political prisoners, Bhai Maharaj Singh and Khurruck Singh, were exiled to Singapore by the British after they annexed Punjab. The police recruitment came three decades later, and it had a practical cause.

In the 1870s the Straits Settlements Police faced high crime and the influence of the Chinese secret societies, and the force had long had trouble keeping men. A police report in 1856 had already complained that officers could not understand Chinese speakers, that pay was low, and that many Indian policemen went home after two or three years. In 1879 a commission of enquiry into the police recommended recruiting Sikhs from Punjab. According to the Singapore Police Force's own history, the colonial government chose them because of the reputation Sikh soldiers had in British service, and because Sikh police had already been used in Hong Kong and the Malay States.

## The contingent

The first group landed on 26 March 1881, the day after a batch of European constables. About 100 Sikh policemen were in Singapore by August, and a full contingent of 165 had assembled by November. The European constables did not adapt well to local conditions and were disbanded by 1906, while the Sikhs became a lasting part of the force.

The terms of service were strict. Recruits had to be at least 175 centimetres tall with a chest of more than 96.5 centimetres, and they were medically examined and sworn in before a magistrate. They were also baptised at the contingent's own gurdwara and had to follow the code of conduct for baptised Sikhs, which meant keeping their hair and beards uncut and wearing a turban.

The contingent kept order in town and guarded government buildings. A riot squad of 50 men was kept on standby at all times, and Sikh officers accompanied visiting dignitaries and took part in ceremonial duties. Later they also took on beat and traffic duties. A detachment was sent to Christmas Island, then part of the Straits Settlements, where it dealt with labour unrest in the early 1900s.

<div style="clear: both;"></div>

## Guards for the port

The contingent's reputation made Sikh guards the choice of private employers too. The Tanjong Pagar Dock Company hired Sikhs as its dock police. Others guarded the Straits Trading Company's tin smelter on Pulau Brani, the Shell Company's oil storage tanks on Pulau Sambu, and the granite quarries at Bukit Timah Hill.

## From barracks to community

The contingent built its own gurdwara at its barracks in 1882, the year after it arrived, and it served civilian Sikhs as well as the police. As more Sikhs came to Singapore, the community grew beyond the force. In 1912 civilian Sikhs, in a committee led by the merchant Wassiamull Assomull Mahtani, bought a compound on Queen Street for a gurdwara of their own.

The police Sikhs then petitioned for land of their own, and in 1922 they obtained a site on Silat Road from the Singapore Harbour Board, the body that had taken over the Tanjong Pagar Dock Company. The Straits Times of 19 December 1922 reported that the foundation stone had been laid the previous morning by the Inspector-General of Police, Captain A. R. Chancellor, "in the presence of practically the entire Sikh community in Singapore." The address read to him that day explained that Singapore's Sikhs had long needed a place of worship and a lodging house close to the wharves, for the members of their community passing through the port. The paper reported that $22,000 had already been raised, with contributions from Sikhs across the peninsula and from Sikh police in other parts of the Far East. That evening the Sikhs held a farewell reception for Chancellor at the Pearl's Hill barracks.

The gurdwara was completed in 1924 at a cost of $54,000, most of it raised by Sikh police in Singapore and Malaya. It was the first gurdwara in Singapore built in the traditional Sikh style, with domes and arches. During the Japanese occupation it housed and looked after the widows and children of Sikhs, and after the war many of the widows were offered free passage home to Punjab.

## 1945 and after

The Sikh Police Contingent did not survive the war. The National Library Board's history of the police attributes its disbandment in 1945 to a high attrition rate and low morale after the Japanese occupation, during which some Sikhs had faced what it calls divided loyalties, and to shrinking recruitment once India and Pakistan became independent. Individual Sikh officers continued to serve in the police afterwards. In 1949 the police formed a new guard force recruited from Nepal, the Gurkha Contingent, to take over the Sikh unit's role.

The community outlasted the contingent. Singapore had 12,051 resident Sikhs in 2020, and the Silat Road gurdwara, rebuilt behind its original façade and reopened in 1995, was declared a historic site by the National Heritage Board in 1999.

**Where it fits in the bigger story:** Singapore's police history is usually told through its commissioners and its reforms. The Sikh Police Contingent was recruited to solve a specific problem, a force that could not keep men or be trusted to stay clear of the secret societies, and it did that job for more than sixty years. The men it brought from Punjab, and the guards and families who followed them, built a gurdwara near the wharves with their own money, and it is the community they built, rather than the police unit, that has lasted.
