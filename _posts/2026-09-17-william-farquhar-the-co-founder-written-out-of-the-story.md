At the foot of Fort Canning Green sits a small garden planted with gambier, guava, wild nutmeg and rattan, each one framed like a picture — a deliberate echo of a set of watercolour drawings commissioned two centuries ago by the man the garden is named after. Almost nobody who walks through it could tell you who William Farquhar actually was.

*Major-General William Farquhar (1774–1839), circa 1830. (Unknown artist, public domain, via Wikimedia Commons)*

He ran Singapore for nearly four and a half years. Stamford Raffles, whose name is now more or less synonymous with the country's founding, was physically present for a total of about eight months. That imbalance alone would be an odd footnote in most national histories. In Singapore's case, it produced a founding myth with one name on it and a man who built the actual settlement all but written out.

## Fifteen years in Malacca

Farquhar arrived in Malacca in 1795 as an East India Company officer, became its Commandant in 1803, and by 1812 held the combined post of Commandant and Resident. He held it for the better part of two decades, transforming the colony's finances, pushing through anti-slavery measures, and earning consistent praise across nine successive Penang governors — a run of institutional trust that's hard to overstate for the period. He was also, by inclination, a naturalist. Over his years in Malacca he commissioned Chinese artists to produce detailed watercolours of the region's plants and animals: 477 drawings in total, now known as the William Farquhar Collection of Natural History Drawings, and about as far from a soldier-administrator's usual paper trail as it gets.

It's this same collection the Farquhar Garden at Fort Canning is built to echo — plants he found worth recording, framed again two centuries later for visitors who mostly don't know the connection.

## Left in charge

When Britain returned Malacca to the Dutch under the 1815 Congress of Vienna, Penang's merchants worried openly about losing their trading position, and Farquhar was drawn into the search for a replacement base. In August 1818 he personally negotiated a trade treaty with the Sultan of Johor. When the Carimon Islands turned out unsuitable, it was Farquhar who proposed Singapore instead, in early 1819. The Governor-General in Bengal put him in charge of any new post that came of it — but subordinate to Raffles.

Raffles landed on 28 January 1819, exploited a succession dispute to install Sultan Hussein on the Johor throne, and signed the treaty securing Singapore on 6 February. He left the very next day. Farquhar stayed on as Resident and Commandant, and would remain in that post for the next four and a half years.

## The years Raffles wasn't there

What Farquhar built in that time is not really in dispute, even if it's rarely put on a plaque. He cleared more than 650,000 square yards of jungle and swamp, built reservoirs, roads and defence works, and set up Singapore's first police force. He commissioned the island's first proper survey and drew up its first town plan in 1821 — a document that still shapes how the old core of the city reads on a map today.

![Plan of the Town of Singapore, 1822, by Lieutenant Philip Jackson](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/6/60/Plan_of_the_Town_of_Singapore_%281822%29_by_Lieutenant_Philip_Jackson.jpg)

*The 1822 Plan of the Town of Singapore, surveyed under Lieutenant Philip Jackson during Farquhar's residency. (Public domain, via Wikimedia Commons)*

He rediscovered and charted the deep-water harbour later renamed Keppel Harbour, corresponded with regional rulers to draw in trade, and persuaded merchant families to relocate from Malacca. He granted asylum to Prince Belawa and some 500 Bugis refugees fleeing Dutch-controlled Rhio. He backed the settlement's first school and served as president of the Singapore Institution. By late 1821 the population had grown to roughly 5,000, with commerce already thriving. None of it happened because Raffles was managing it from Bengal and Bencoolen — it happened because Farquhar was there.

## Where they actually disagreed

The two men did clash on real substance, not just personality. Raffles ordered Farquhar to introduce opium revenue licences — a policy Raffles' own supporters later blamed on Farquhar, despite Raffles having authorised it and taken a personal commission. When merchants objected that Raffles' planned European quarter at East Beach was unsuited to trade, Farquhar pragmatically allowed temporary warehouses on the north bank instead; Raffles later characterised this as outright disobedience, although it was Farquhar's approach that kept the young settlement's commerce from stalling. Underneath the specific disputes sat a genuine difference in how each man saw the place: Raffles wanted a British port. Farquhar, working from the original treaty, still saw Singapore as sitting within a Malay political order.

## Raffles comes back

Raffles returned in October 1822, after three and a half years away, and at first praised how far the settlement had come. That didn't last. He excluded Farquhar from a newly formed Town Committee, took over the weekly Resident's court himself, and wrote to Bengal accusing Farquhar of overspending and improper land dealings — charges he would later withdraw. On 11 January 1823 Raffles wrote that he didn't consider Farquhar capable of the job. The very next day, in a letter to his cousin, he was celebrating Singapore's rise as "a great emporium" — the emporium Farquhar had spent four and a half years building.

On 1 May 1823, Raffles dismissed Farquhar as Resident, technically without the authority to do so — only the Governor-General in Bengal had appointed him. When John Crawfurd arrived to take over, Raffles dismissed Farquhar a second time, now as Commandant. Farquhar appealed to Bengal and lost.

## The farewell, and the erasure

Farquhar left Singapore on 28 December 1823, dismissed twice over and humiliated in the official record. The reception from the people who'd actually lived under his administration told a different story: the Bugis, Chinese and Indian communities gave him emotional farewell addresses, and local merchants raised $3,000 to have silverware made in his honour.

The official record won out anyway. Raffles' 1897 biographer, Demetrius Boulger, set the template that stuck — Raffles as visionary founder, Farquhar reduced to a footnote — and it held. Raffles got a statue at his landing site and, eventually, an MRT station carrying his name. Farquhar Street, Mount Farquhar and Farquhar's Strait have all since disappeared from the map. Modern historians — John Bastin, Mary Turnbull, Ernest Chew among them — have pushed back on the imbalance for decades, but the popular version never really moved. Farquhar's own tombstone, in Perth, Scotland, still describes him more plainly than Singapore ever has: Resident of Malacca, and "afterwards at Singapore, which later settlement he founded."

**Why it matters today:** Singapore's founding story gets told as the work of one visionary Englishman who saw the island's potential and built accordingly. The actual record shows something messier and more interesting — a company officer who did the physical work of founding a settlement for four and a half years, was overruled and dismissed by the man who then took sole credit, and today survives mostly as the name of a small garden at Fort Canning that most visitors walk straight past.
