At the corner of Neil Road and Tanjong Pagar Road stands a small, ornate building of exposed brick and white plaster, with an arched five-foot-way and a square corner tower capped by an octagonal dome. It is a wine bar, a restaurant and some offices now, and for a while the deed carried Jackie Chan's name. It was built in 1903 for a single purpose: to register every rickshaw in Singapore and license the men who pulled them. At the peak there were close to thirty thousand rickshaws on the streets and perhaps fifty thousand men working the shafts in shifts, most of them used up within a few years. The building was preserved. The men left almost nothing.

## The building on the corner

Rickshaws reached Singapore in 1880, the first batch shipped from Shanghai and the rest from Japan, where the vehicle had been invented a decade earlier. They were cheaper and quicker than the horse-drawn gharries they soon put out of business, and within a few years there were thousands of them. In 1888 the Municipal Commission set up a Jinrikisha Department to register every rickshaw and inspect it regularly for roadworthiness. The department worked out of rented houses on Beach Road, South Bridge Road and at Fort Canning, then a purpose-built station at the Middle Road and Prinsep Street junction in 1899, which cost 34,000 dollars and was outgrown almost at once.

In 1898 the Commission bought a wedge of land where Neil Road meets Tanjong Pagar Road, from a Johor noble, at a dollar a foot. The Jinrikisha Station was designed by the municipal engineer Samuel Tomlinson and the municipal architect David McLeod Craik, built through 1903 and opened the following year. It followed the shape of its awkward triangular site: a curved corner facade under a square tower with an octagonal cupola, exposed brickwork set off by white plaster mouldings, and the Municipal Council's crest — a lion on an island beneath a palm — over the entrance. Inside, clerks registered new rickshaws, inspected the ones already on the road, and issued the pullers their licences. By 1902 there were 22,629 rickshaws to keep track of, and the number kept climbing for another twenty years.

## Who pulled

The men who pulled were overwhelmingly from two poor coastal districts of Fujian province — Hokchew, around Fuzhou, and Henghua, further down the coast. Most were illiterate peasants who had come south through the coolie trade with a passage debt to work off and little else. Rickshaw-pulling asked for no skill, only a back and a pair of legs, so it was often the first paid work a new arrival could get.

Almost none of them owned the rickshaw they pulled. A new one cost more than a puller earned in a year, so the men hired them by the shift from owners who ran fleets of hundreds. Out of a day's takings of roughly a dollar, twenty to thirty cents went straight back to the owner as rent. What was left had to cover food and a place to sleep, which for most meant a "coolie keng" — a shophouse cubicle in Chinatown with plank bunks used in rotation by day and night shifts, no running water, and, more often than not, an opium lamp burning in the corner.

## The work

![A Chinatown street lined with shophouses and dozens of rickshaws parked and being repaired along both sides, pullers resting on the shafts, around 1930](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/3/3b/Jinricksha_Station_aan_Neil_Road_te_Singapore%2C_KITLV_104799.tiff/lossy-page1-1280px-Jinricksha_Station_aan_Neil_Road_te_Singapore%2C_KITLV_104799.tiff.jpg)

*A rickshaw stand near the Jinrikisha Station, Chinatown, around 1930. Photographer unknown; Leiden University Library (KITLV collection), [CC BY 4.0](https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by/4.0/).*

A working puller covered perhaps twenty-five to forty kilometres a day at a jog, barefoot or in straw sandals, through the heat and the traffic, and many chewed or smoked opium to keep moving. The *Straits Times* in 1912 called rickshaw-pulling "the deadliest occupation in the East, and the most degrading for human beings to pursue." James Francis Warren, whose history of the trade was built from the colonial coroner's records, found that most men lasted five to seven years before a lung failed, a tram or motor car caught them, or they went home worn out. Sago Lane and its death houses were a few streets from the stand.

The pullers were not silent about their conditions. The trade was shut down by major strikes in 1919, 1920 and 1938, usually triggered by a new registration fee or rule, and the 1919 stoppage came as the price of rice was climbing steeply after the First World War. Each time, the town fell quiet for a few days, and each time the government gave a little ground.

## The slow end

The pedal trishaw was first licensed in Singapore in 1914, but it spread slowly. What really finished the rickshaw was the motor car. From the late 1920s the colonial government began cutting the number of rickshaw licences in the interest of road safety as motor traffic thickened, and by 1940 only 3,693 rickshaws were left. The Japanese Occupation handed the trishaw its opening — with no fuel, tyres or spare parts to be had, pedal power won by default — and by 1945 it had taken over. In 1947 the returning British administration banned human-pulled rickshaws outright, as an affront to human dignity. Most of the men still pulling switched to a trishaw and carried on.

## What's left

![The two-storey Jinrikisha Station building today, exposed brick with white arches and a corner tower topped by a green octagonal dome, at a Tanjong Pagar road junction with modern towers behind](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/1/1e/Jinrikisha_Station_%2812848777624%29.jpg/1280px-Jinrikisha_Station_%2812848777624%29.jpg)

*The Jinrikisha Station today, at Neil Road and Tanjong Pagar Road. Photo by KimonBerlin, [CC BY-SA 2.0](https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by-sa/2.0/), via Wikimedia Commons.*

With no rickshaws to register, the station was put to other work: a family-planning clinic, then a maternal and child health centre. In 1987 the Urban Redevelopment Authority drew up the Tanjong Pagar conservation area and offered the building for tender on condition that whoever won it restored the important features within a year. L&B Holdings took it in 1989 for close to two million dollars and turned it into a seafood restaurant with shops and a nightclub upstairs. In December 2007 the Hong Kong actor Jackie Chan bought the building for eleven million dollars; it trades today under its address, 1 Neil Road.

The building was judged worth keeping. The trade it existed to regulate produced no monument of its own — a handful of studio portraits taken as souvenirs, some newspaper reports of strikes, and the shelves of coroner's inquest files that Warren read a lifetime later to work out who these men had been.

What is conserved is the architecture. The station's heritage signage explains the brickwork, the octagonal tower and the Municipal crest over the door; it does not mention the men who queued there for a licence. A few streets away, Sago Lane — where many of them lodged, and where the dying were carried to the death houses — is a temple and a parade of shops now, with little to show what it was. The merchants and philanthropists of the same decades, the men who held the capital, are remembered in statues, street names and foundations that still give money away. The pullers survive mainly as background: a bronze figure in an "old Singapore" street tableau, a display case in a heritage centre, the pages of a single historian's book.

[See more photographs of Singapore's rickshaw men →](/gallery/the-rickshaw-men-and-the-jinricksha-station/)

**Where it fits in the bigger story:** Early Singapore ran on imported muscle — rickshaw pullers, wharf coolies, the men who dug the docks and loaded the godowns — who arrived owing money, worked until their bodies failed, and mostly went home or died without leaving a name in the record. What tends to survive them is the building that processed them: the registry office, the coolie depot, the inspection station. The infrastructure of control outlasts the people it was built to control.
