In January 1984 the American archaeologist John Miksic and a small team spent ten days digging test pits on Fort Canning Hill, beside the shrine known as the Keramat Iskandar Shah. For the first few days they found almost nothing of interest. Then the colour of the soil changed, and out came pieces of blue-and-white Chinese porcelain made in the 1300s. It was the first hard evidence that a town had stood on the hill some five hundred years before the British arrived, and that the Singapura of the old Malay chronicles was more than a story.

## Ten days in January

In the early 1980s there were plans to turn Fort Canning Hill into an entertainment and cultural centre, with an underground car park for 300 cars. Some of the staff at the National Museum knew the old traditions that a 14th-century palace had stood on the hill, and they invited Miksic, then teaching at Gadjah Mada University in Yogyakarta, to see whether anything was left in the ground before the builders arrived.

The dig ran from 18 to 28 January 1984. Miksic's crew was a mix of museum staff, hired labourers and national servicemen who had been put on the job as a punishment, though by his account they became interested once he explained what they were looking for. Heavy rain slowed the work, and the first finds were a goat skeleton, an old British army helmet and a few bricks from the 19th or 20th century. Then, in Miksic's words, "suddenly we saw the soil colour change and we started finding different kinds of artefacts."

Among them were pieces of blue-and-white porcelain from the Yuan dynasty, which ruled China from 1279 to 1368. In all, the ten days produced 1,346 sherds of pottery, weighing a little over 14 kilograms, lying where they had been dropped in the 14th century. The Straits Times, reporting on the finds that March, asked whether the "fabulous kingdom of Temasek", known from Chinese, Javanese and Portuguese texts, had really been Singapore. The plans for the hill were changed, and the parks authorities later hired Miksic as a consultant to keep digging.

The finds made sense of a set of clues that had been piling up for more than 160 years.

## The hill nobody would climb

When Stamford Raffles and William Farquhar landed at the end of January 1819, they asked the Temenggong, the Malay chief of the island, about the hill behind the plain. Wa Hakim, who was a boy on the island at the time and told the story many years later, remembered the answer: it was Bukit Larangan, the Forbidden Hill, "because the Rajah resided there in old times and erected his palace there, and would allow no one to go up".

The tombs of the old rulers were believed to be on the hill, and it was held to be haunted. Munshi Abdullah, the Malay scribe who came to Singapore a few months later, recorded that when Farquhar wanted to go up, none of the Temenggong's people would go with him, for fear of ghosts. Farquhar took his Malacca men instead and had a gun dragged to the top, where a salute of twelve shots was fired and a flagpole put up. Then he ordered the hill cleared and a road cut to the summit. Raffles later put a bungalow there, which became Government House, and for decades afterwards Malays still climbed the hill to decorate the old graves.

## What Crawfurd found

![An engraving of a wide view from a hilltop over tall trees and scattered houses to a harbour full of ships](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/8/84/View_of_the_town_and_roads_of_Singapore_from_the_government_hill_published_1828.jpg/1280px-View_of_the_town_and_roads_of_Singapore_from_the_government_hill_published_1828.jpg)

*The town and harbour seen from the top of the hill, drawn by Robert Elliot in the early 1820s and published in 1828 in Crawfurd's own journal of his mission. (Robert Elliot, public domain, via Wikimedia Commons)*

John Crawfurd, who later became the second Resident of Singapore, spent two days in February 1822 looking at what was left of the old town. On the morning of 3 February he walked its boundary. To the north, an earth wall about 16 feet (5 metres) wide at the base and still eight or nine feet high ran for almost a mile from the shore to the foot of the hill, with a stream along it that served as a moat. The sea and the river creek made up the other sides.

The hill itself, about 150 feet (45 metres) high, had just been cleared of forest, and Crawfurd found the west and north slopes covered with the foundations of buildings, some of them of good baked brick. The most striking was a square terrace about 40 feet (12 metres) on each side, near the summit. Around its edge lay fourteen large blocks of sandstone, each with a hole in it, which he took to be the bases of wooden posts, so the building above had been made of timber. In the middle was a ring of rough stones around a hollow that might once have held an image, and he guessed the whole thing had been a Buddhist temple.

A second terrace on the north slope was said to be the grave of Iskandar Shah, the last king of Singapura, and a simple shelter had already been put up over it, where Muslims, Hindus and Chinese all came to pray. On the east side of the hill Crawfurd found durian, rambutan, duku and pomelo trees, gone wild, which he believed had been planted by the old inhabitants. Among the ruins lay broken Chinese and local pottery, and Chinese brass coins of the 10th and 11th centuries.

## The kingdom in the chronicles

![A page of a Malay manuscript in Jawi script, written in black ink with some words picked out in red](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/2/2d/Excerpt_from_the_Sulalatus_Salatin_manuscript%2C_copied_in_the_year_1808%2C_chapter_on_the_Death_of_Hang_Tuah.jpg)

*A page from a copy of the Sejarah Melayu, the Malay Annals, made in 1808. The Annals tell the story of the five kings of Singapura. (Osman bin Kuri bin Amat Utat, the copyist, CC0, via Wikimedia Commons)*

The Sejarah Melayu, the Malay Annals, tells how a prince from Palembang named Sang Nila Utama landed on the island of Temasek, saw a strange beast he took for a lion, and named the place Singapura, the Lion City. Five kings ruled there, the Annals say, until the last of them, Iskandar Shah, lost the city to an attack from Java after one of his own ministers betrayed him. The king fled up the coast, and his line went on to found Melaka. The Annals date none of this, and the dates often given for the five reigns, from 1299 to 1398, are a 20th-century reconstruction.

There is one outside witness. Around 1330 the Chinese traveller Wang Dayuan visited a place he called Danmaxi, usually taken to be Temasek, and described two settlements. One, Longyamen, lay by the strait and was home to pirates. The other, Banzu, lay on a hill behind it, and its people were honest, wore their hair short and had a chief of their own. Banzu may be a Chinese rendering of the Malay pancur, a spring, and Fort Canning had one: a spring on the west side of the hill, known as the forbidden spring, where the ruler's women were said to have bathed. Wang also wrote that a Siamese fleet of about 70 ships had besieged the place for a month a few years before his visit.

For most of the 20th century, many historians treated the Annals' account of Singapura as more legend than history. They were written down two centuries after the events they describe, for the court of Melaka, and apart from Crawfurd's ruins and the [Singapore Stone](/2026/10/11/the-singapore-stone-the-inscribed-rock-singapore-blew-up-in-1843/), there was very little on the ground to check them against.

## Gold under the summit

![A gold armlet made of fine chains joined by a square plaque, with two small gold ornaments beside it, on a dark blue display](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/b/bf/JavaneseGoldOrnaments-14C-NationalMuseumofSingapore-20090712.jpg/1280px-JavaneseGoldOrnaments-14C-NationalMuseumofSingapore-20090712.jpg)

*The gold armlet with its kala plaque, and two smaller ornaments, found on Fort Canning in 1928 and now in the National Museum of Singapore. (Jon Callas, CC BY 2.0, via Wikimedia Commons)*

On 7 July 1928, labourers clearing the summit of Fort Canning for a reservoir dug up a cache of eleven gold ornaments. There was a pair of armlets with flexible chains, a clasp, an elliptical ornament, an inscribed ring and several more rings. The engineer on the site recorded that they lay just below the top of the soil laid down before the British arrived. The finest piece was an armlet with a plaque showing a kala, the guardian face that Javanese builders carved over temple doorways.

The Dutch archaeologist P. V. van Stein Callenfels, asked for his opinion, identified the work as East Javanese, from the time of the Majapahit empire, and the historian Richard Winstedt described the find in a short article that year. Only the armlet and a pair of earrings are in the National Museum today; the rest were later lost. A study published in 2023 by Natalie Ong questioned whether the armlet is Javanese at all.

The gold showed that someone wealthy had lived on the hill in the 14th century, but it was a single hoard with no record of what lay around it, and on its own it proved little about a town.

## What the hill gave up

The 1984 test pits were followed by seven more excavations on the hill by 1995, including one in 1987 run as part of an ASEAN archaeology programme. Miksic joined the National University of Singapore that year, and from then on members of the public could volunteer to dig.

![Fragments of an inscribed porcelain dish and many smaller white porcelain chips laid out on a grey display stand](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/9/98/At_Fort_Canning_2023_031.jpg/1280px-At_Fort_Canning_2023_031.jpg)

*The Chinese porcelain compass found on the hill, with inscriptions probably used for geomancy, on display at Fort Canning beside sherds of porcelain pillows. (Mike Peel, CC BY-SA 4.0, via Wikimedia Commons)*

Over the years the hill produced more than 10,000 glass beads, fine white Chinese porcelain with cobalt-blue decoration, fragments of elaborate glass vessels from China, and a Chinese porcelain compass that is described as the only one of its kind known. The diggers also found a workshop where gold was worked and broken glass was melted down and made into bangles. Taken together, the finds point to a high-status quarter, used through the 14th century and into the early 15th, which fits with the Annals' account of the city falling around 1400. A ten-week dig led by Goh Geok Yian and Miksic in 2018 found more of the same kinds of stoneware and porcelain.

## Down to the river

Later digs showed that the old town did not stop at the foot of the hill. Between November 1995 and January 1996, before the new Parliament House was built, an excavation beside the river found layers of sand and charcoal from the 14th to the 16th centuries, with debris from copper and bronze working, fish hooks, and Chinese coins in large numbers. In 1998, before the Empress Place Building became the Asian Civilisations Museum, a dig on the riverbank there found goods from Vietnam, Thailand, Java, Borneo, the Philippines, China and Persia, the remains of a place where cargo was loaded, unloaded and passed on. Further digs in 2003, at a corner of the Singapore Cricket Club's ground on the Padang and at St Andrew's Cathedral, found older material there too, a sign that the settlement spread well beyond the river mouth.

![A long cream-coloured colonial building behind large rain trees on the far bank of a river](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/4/4a/Empress_Place_Building%2C_Singapore_-_20140723.jpg/1280px-Empress_Place_Building%2C_Singapore_-_20140723.jpg)

*The Empress Place Building on the Singapore River, now the Asian Civilisations Museum. The riverbank in front of it was dug in 1998. (Lobster1, CC BY-SA 3.0, via Wikimedia Commons)*

Miksic went on digging in Singapore for the rest of his career. His book Singapore and the Silk Road of the Sea, 1300–1800 (2013) won the first Singapore History Prize in 2018, and he received the Public Service Medal in 2023. He died on 25 October 2025, at the age of 79. "We were not just a minor fishing village," he once told an interviewer. "We were actually an important place."

## The hill today

![An open wooden pavilion with a steep tiled roof and a tall gable, sheltering a dark tomb-like block on a brick floor among trees](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/f/fb/Keramat_Iskandar_Syah%2C_Fort_Canning_Park%2C_Singapore_-_20110924.jpg/1280px-Keramat_Iskandar_Syah%2C_Fort_Canning_Park%2C_Singapore_-_20110924.jpg)

*The Keramat Iskandar Shah on Fort Canning Hill in 2011. The 1984 test pits were dug close by. (janebelindasmith, CC BY 2.0, via Wikimedia Commons)*

The Keramat Iskandar Shah still stands on the hill, a tiled wooden pavilion over a cenotaph, and scholars do not think it is a real grave: Iskandar Shah is believed to have died in Melaka, and no burial has been found at the site. Part of the excavation near the summit has been kept open under a roof, so visitors can look down into the trenches and see the layers of soil the diggers worked through, and the Sang Nila Utama Garden nearby has been laid out with a split gateway in the style of old Javanese temples. Much of the rest of the hill's later history, from Raffles's house to the [forts](/2026/10/04/fort-palmer-fort-canning-and-the-forts-that-guarded-a-free-port/) and the wartime [bunker](/2026/07/18/the-bunker-under-fort-canning/), sits on top of the ground that Miksic's team dug into.

**Why it matters today:** For a long time Singapore's story was usually told as starting in 1819, when Raffles found what was often described as a small fishing village. The 1984 dig, and the ones that followed, showed that there had been a working port town here in the 14th century, trading with China, Java, India and beyond, with a high-status quarter on the hill that its later inhabitants still remembered as forbidden ground. When Singapore marked 200 years since Raffles's landing in 2019, the commemoration was framed as a 700-year history, and its centrepiece, the Bicentennial Experience, was staged on Fort Canning Hill itself. Most of that older story rests on a few square metres of soil, dug out a trowel at a time.
