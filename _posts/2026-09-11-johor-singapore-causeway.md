The Johor–Singapore Causeway has two rush hours, and they run in opposite directions. Before dawn, tens of thousands of Malaysians who live in Johor Bahru pour south across it to jobs in Singapore. On Friday nights and through the weekend the current reverses, as Singaporeans head the other way — not to work, but to fill their petrol tanks, buy groceries, see a dentist and have lunch, all at prices their own dollar buys far more of on the Malaysian side. It is one kilometre of road on a bank of rock, and the same currency gap is worked from both ends of it.

## A road, not a bridge

For most of the 19th century, everything that moved between Singapore and the Malay Peninsula went by ferry across the narrow Johor Strait — tin and rubber heading for Singapore's port, people and goods heading back. By 1911 the ferries were running around the clock and still could not keep up. The colonial authorities wanted something permanent, and in 1917 they hired the consulting engineers Coode, Fitzmaurice, Wilson & Mitchell to design it.

What they chose was not a bridge. Steel was expensive and a bridge would cost too much to maintain, so the plan was a *causeway*: a solid embankment of rock a kilometre long, carrying a railway, a road, and the pipelines that would bring Johor water to Singapore, with a small lock and a lift-bridge at the Johor end so boats could still pass. The contract went to the London firm Topham, Jones & Railton in 1919, and work began that August, quarrying granite from Pulau Ubin and Bukit Timah and tipping it into the strait — around 1.6 million cubic yards of stone in all. An economic slump in the early 1920s nearly killed the project; public criticism of the cost, an estimated 17 million Straits dollars split between the Federated Malay States, Johor and the Straits Settlements, almost stopped the work.

![A 1945 view of the Causeway curving across the Johor Strait towards Johor Bahru, with a railway line running along it](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/0/01/The_Causeway_joining_Singapore_Island_with_Johore_in_1945.jpg)

*The Causeway in September 1945, just after the war, looking across to Johor Bahru. The structure is essentially the 1923 rock embankment; it has been widened three times since but never replaced. (Photograph, 1945; public domain, via Wikimedia Commons.)*

It was finished anyway. The strait was sealed by June 1923, goods trains crossed from September, and on 1 October 1923 the first passenger train ran through to Singapore. The formal opening followed on 28 June 1924, in Johor Bahru, where a public holiday was declared and the Malay rulers and British officials were driven across in a convoy of eleven cars before the road was thrown open. The Causeway had joined the island physically to the peninsula, and it made Singapore the terminus of the mainland railway.

## The gap, 1942

The Causeway's most dramatic day came eighteen years later. As the Japanese advanced down Malaya in January 1942, the retreating British and Commonwealth forces fell back across the strait onto Singapore island. The last unit over, in the early hours of 31 January, was the 2nd Battalion of the Argyll and Sutherland Highlanders, marched across to two pipers playing "A Hundred Pipers" and "Highland Laddie". Behind them, Royal Engineers set off two charges: the first wrecked the lock's lift-bridge, the second blew a gap about 21 metres wide in the Causeway itself and severed the water pipelines.

It bought almost no time. The Japanese landed on Singapore's north-west coast a week later, built a girder bridge over the gap, and took the island on 15 February. After the war the girder bridge was replaced with Bailey-bridge sections, the lock and lift-bridge were sealed up for good, and the Causeway carried on much as before.

## The pipe

The pipelines the Royal Engineers cut in 1942 are still there, and they still matter. Under water agreements signed in 1961 and 1962, Singapore has the right to draw raw water from the Johor River — up to 250 million gallons a day — and pipe it across the Causeway to treat and use, selling a smaller quantity of treated water back to Johor. The 1962 agreement runs until 2061.

The prices in it have never changed: Singapore pays 3 Malaysian sen per thousand gallons of raw water, and sells treated water back at 50 sen. Both figures were set more than sixty years ago, and both governments regard them as unfinished business — Malaysia has periodically called the raw-water price far too low, Singapore has pointed out that the agreement was a package neither side reopened when it could have. Singapore now makes much of its own water, from recycling and desalination, but Johor water still comes across that kilometre of rock, and the arrangement is a reminder that the Causeway carries more than traffic.

## The commute north

On an ordinary weekday the Causeway and the newer Second Link at Tuas together see something over 300,000 crossings a day, which makes this the busiest land border on earth; on 6 September 2025 the two checkpoints recorded 543,000 crossings in a single day. A large share of that is one specific movement: Malaysians who live in Johor Bahru, where housing and daily costs are a fraction of Singapore's, and who work in Singapore, where wages are not.

The gap is stark. Average gross pay in Singapore runs several times the Malaysian figure, and for a Johor household the difference compounds: a salary earned in Singapore dollars and spent in ringgit stretches to a house, a car and savings that the same job on the Malaysian side would not support. So a few hundred thousand people make the crossing a daily routine — up at four or five in the morning, onto a motorbike or a bus, through immigration on both sides, and back again twelve or more hours later. Whole parts of Singapore's economy, from food courts and building sites to hospital support staff, are staffed this way. When the ringgit strengthened by around six per cent against the Singapore dollar over the past year, it did more to narrow the wage gap than any policy announcement — and Malaysian commuters felt it immediately.

The clearance itself has actually got faster. For decades a crossing meant handing a passport to an officer for a stamp — the "chop", inked with a "W" for Woodlands or a "T" for Tuas. Now most travellers pass through an automated gate that scans the passport, or a QR code in the vehicle lanes, and takes a photo to match against it — no officer, no ink. Changi Airport has gone a step further, to clearance on iris and face alone with no passport at all, and that fuller version is being extended to the land checkpoints in stages: facial recognition for motorcyclists from 2026, automated in-vehicle clearance from 2027. What has not changed is the arithmetic: several hundred thousand people trying to pass two sets of controls in the same few hours each morning and evening. Automating the counter does not widen the road.

## The run south

Going the other way is the second rush hour, and it is almost entirely discretionary. One Singapore dollar buys roughly 3.3 ringgit — up from about 2.5 a decade ago — and that exchange rate turns a routine errand into a saving worth a morning's queue.

Petrol is the classic reason to go: Malaysian RON95 is price-controlled and costs well under half what it does in Singapore, enough that Singapore requires every departing local vehicle to leave with its tank at least three-quarters full, and turns back the ones that don't. Once across, the trip tends to expand. Groceries at the big Johor hypermarkets run a third to a half cheaper for the same brands. A haircut, a car service, a massage, new spectacles — all noticeably less. The food is the familiar Malayan repertoire, cooked a little differently and priced at Johor levels. And medical and dental work is the category where the gap is widest: a scale-and-polish, a filling, a crown, a health screening in a Johor Bahru clinic can cost a fraction of the Singapore price, and the clinics near the checkpoint advertise their fees in Singapore dollars to make the comparison easy.

None of it is comfortable. A bad weekend queue at the Causeway can run to two, three, four hours in a stationary bus or car, in the heat, for the return leg alone. People do it anyway, because the arithmetic still works out in their favour even after the time is counted.

## The links that keep being promised

For as long as the Causeway has been congested, there have been plans to relieve it. A second crossing, the Malaysia–Singapore Second Link between Tuas and Gelang Patah, opened in 1998 and took some of the load without ever really easing the Causeway itself. Malaysia has proposed replacing the Causeway with a bridge more than once since 1996, most seriously in the early 2000s; the idea foundered on the fact that the Causeway cannot legally be altered without both governments agreeing, and Singapore did not agree.

The current fix is a train. The Johor Bahru–Singapore Rapid Transit System, a short cross-border rail shuttle between Woodlands North and Bukit Chagar, has been under construction since 2021 and is due to open around the start of 2027. It is designed to move up to 10,000 passengers an hour in each direction and to cross in about five minutes, and the hope is that it takes enough daily commuters off the road to make a visible difference. A high-speed rail line to Kuala Lumpur, agreed in 2016 and cancelled in 2021, is being talked about again. Singapore's Woodlands checkpoint is being rebuilt to handle far more people by the early 2030s.

Between March 2020 and early 2022, none of this mattered, because the border was shut. For close to two years the daily crossing simply stopped: commuters had to choose a side and stay on it, sometimes sleeping in Singapore for months away from families in Johor, and the day-trippers had nowhere to go. The photograph at the top of this post is from that period. It is the only time the Causeway has been empty since 1945.

## Where it fits in the bigger story

The Causeway works because the two ends of it are unequal. The wage on one side and the prices on the other are what fill it every day, in both directions, and every proposed fix — a second bridge, a shuttle train, a special economic zone — is really an attempt to manage the traffic that inequality generates rather than to remove the cause. A kilometre of rock laid down in 1923 has become the place where the difference between two economies is settled, one tank of petrol and one dental appointment at a time.
