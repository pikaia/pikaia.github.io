On the morning of 1 October 1935, a new bank opened its doors at the corner of Chulia Street and Bonham Street, facing Raffles Place, in premises formerly occupied by the Yokohama Specie Bank. It was called the United Chinese Bank, it had seven founders and $1 million of paid-up capital, and it was set up to serve Singapore's Hokkien merchants in the middle of the Great Depression. Ninety years later, as United Overseas Bank, it is one of Singapore's three big local banks. Much of that growth came from buying other banks, one of them older than itself.

## Seven founders, 1935

The United Chinese Bank was incorporated on 6 August 1935 by a group of seven Hokkien businessmen led by Wee Kheng Chiang. Wee was born in Kuching, Sarawak, in 1890, and had already founded a bank there, the Bian Chiang Bank, in 1924. He was the largest shareholder and became the new bank's chairman.

The Straits Times reported the opening on 1 October under the headline "Chinese Bank Opened Today." The bank had an authorised capital of $4,000,000, of which $1,000,000 had been issued, and it intended to handle foreign exchange as well as ordinary banking, current accounts and fixed deposits. Ong Piah Teng was managing director, and there were five other directors. The manager, Chua Keh Hai, was a former municipal commissioner, and the paper described him as the first banker to introduce foreign exchange business among the local Chinese.

<div style="clear: both;"></div>

The bank's own advertisement, which ran in the Malaya Tribune the next day, gave its address as 31-A Chulia Street, in the Bonham Building. It was a single office, and its customers were mainly Hokkien traders looking for short-term credit. It joined a crowded field. Singapore already had a number of Chinese banks, each tied to a dialect group, and three of them had merged in 1932 to form the [Oversea-Chinese Banking Corporation](/2026/09/30/the-chinese-banks-that-became-ocbc/).

## The second generation

Little has been published about how the bank fared during the Japanese Occupation. Wee Kheng Chiang's fourth son, Wee Cho Yaw, born in 1929, spent most of the Occupation with his family in Karimun, in what is now Indonesia. In 1958 he joined the bank's board as its youngest director. On 1 July 1960, when his father stepped down as managing director, Wee Cho Yaw took over the post. He was 31.

In the late 1950s and early 1960s the bank grew in small steps. It drove its first mobile branch in 1958, opened its first branch outside the head office, on Beach Road, in 1959, and in 1962, by its own account, became the first bank to focus on women as customers.

## A new name, 1965

On 23 January 1965 the United Chinese Bank became United Overseas Bank. The change avoided confusion with another United Chinese Bank in Hong Kong, where the bank opened its first overseas branch the same year.

## Growing by buying, 1971 to 2001

From 1971 the bank grew mainly by acquiring other banks, each an independent bank with its own founders and customers. The first was Chung Khiaw Bank, founded by the Tiger Balm magnate Aw Boon Haw and opened in 1950. UOB bought a majority stake in it in 1971, after the Aw family's holding company had passed to the British investment group Slater Walker, and Chung Khiaw went on trading under its own name until 1999. In 1973 UOB acquired Lee Wah Bank, a Cantonese bank founded in 1920 and older than UOB itself. It took a majority interest in Far Eastern Bank, founded in 1958, in 1984, and in the Industrial & Commercial Bank, founded in 1953, in 1987.

In 1974 Wee Kheng Chiang retired and Wee Cho Yaw became chairman as well as chief executive. The same year the bank moved into a new 30-storey building on the site of the Bonham Building, where it had rented its first office. The taller UOB Plaza One, on the same block, opened in 1995.

The largest purchase came in 2001. Overseas Union Bank had been founded by Lien Ying Chow and opened on 5 February 1949, the first new bank in postwar Singapore, and by the 1970s it was one of the four big local banks. In June 2001, when local banks were under pressure to consolidate, the Development Bank of Singapore made a hostile bid of S$9.4 billion for it. UOB answered with a bid of S$10 billion that had the support of OUB's board and of Lien himself. In September 2001 UOB announced that it had won acceptances for more than 90 per cent of OUB's shares, and the deal made it the largest local bank in Singapore, with about S$115 billion in assets. The OUB name was gone by the end of 2003.

Wee Cho Yaw stepped down as chief executive in 2007, handing the post to his eldest son, Wee Ee Cheong, who still holds it. He retired from the board in 2018 and died in February 2024, aged 95.

## What changed

In 1935 the United Chinese Bank was one of many small Chinese banks in Singapore, most of them serving a single dialect community from a single office. Today Singapore has three large local banks, UOB, OCBC and DBS, and several of the banks of that earlier era survive only inside them. Mergers and acquisitions did that over seven decades, and the last big round, in 2001, left the three that remain.

[See more photos related to this post →](/gallery/uob-the-united-chinese-bank-of-1935-and-the-family-that-built-it/)

**Where it fits in the bigger story:** UOB is one of the most familiar names in Singapore, but its origin, a single rented office in a Depression-era building run for Hokkien merchants, is rarely told. Its history is also a history of the smaller banks it absorbed, from a Cantonese bank of 1920 to the Tiger Balm family's bank, whose names have largely been forgotten.
