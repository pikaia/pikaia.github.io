In September 1942, in a prisoner-of-war hospital at Changi, a British bombardier named Stanley Warren began painting Bible scenes on the walls of a small chapel. He was so ill with dysentery and kidney disease that he could only work for ten to fifteen minutes at a time, and his paints were scrounged camouflage paint and billiard chalk. Within two years his five murals had vanished under coats of distemper, and Warren went home believing they had been destroyed. They are still there today, in a building inside an air force camp that the public cannot enter.

## A hospital in the barracks

Singapore surrendered to the Japanese on the 15th of February 1942, and tens of thousands of British and Australian prisoners were marched to Changi, at the eastern tip of the island. They were held in and around the British Army's own barracks there, and Roberts Barracks was turned into a hospital for the prisoners.

Stanley Warren had been a commercial designer before the war, and had reached Malaya early in 1942 with the 15th Field Regiment, Royal Artillery. On the 23rd of May 1942 he was sent to the hospital at Roberts Barracks, and by the middle of August he was in the dysentery wing, Block 151, a three-storey building that held more than a thousand patients. On its ground floor the prisoners had made a small room into a chapel, dedicated to St Luke the Physician, the patron saint of doctors.

Warren had painted before in captivity. At a camp at Bukit Batok, a chaplain had asked him to decorate the chapel there, and he drew two religious scenes in charcoal. At Roberts Barracks, two chaplains, Padres Chambers and Payne, asked him to do the same for St Luke's Chapel. Warren, who had joined the chapel choir after hearing the hymns from his bed, agreed as a way of giving thanks for his partial recovery.

![A black and white photograph of a crowded barrack square, packed with makeshift shelters and thousands of men, with three-storey barrack blocks behind](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/e/e3/Australian_War_Memorial_132935.JPG)

*Prisoners crowded into the square of Selarang Barracks, Changi, on the 4th of September 1942, during the Selarang Barracks incident. (George Aspinall, Australian War Memorial 132935, public domain, via Wikimedia Commons)*

He was still working on his sketches when the [Selarang Barracks incident](/2026/10/24/the-selarang-square-squeeze-1942/) began on the 30th of August 1942. The Japanese ordered the prisoners to sign a pledge not to escape, and when almost all of them refused, nearly 17,000 men were crowded into and around the square of Selarang Barracks until the 5th of September. Warren began painting in early September. That month the men of his work party were listed to go north to the Thai-Burma Railway, and a colonel stepped in so that he could stay at the hospital and finish the chapel.

## Five murals

Warren painted five murals, each about three metres long. In order, they showed the Nativity, the Ascension, the Crucifixion, the Last Supper, and St Luke in Prison. The Nativity was finished by Christmas 1942. Paint was almost impossible to get, so fellow prisoners found him brown camouflage paint, a little crimson, some white oil paint, and billiard chalk crushed for colour. With so little to work with, he used broad brush strokes and large areas of flat colour.

He never signed the murals, because he considered them "a gift to God".

## Hidden under distemper

In May 1944, Block 151 was turned into a store for a nearby airfield. A doorway was knocked through the wall into the next room, which almost completely destroyed the lower part of St Luke in Prison, and the chapel walls were covered over with distemper. Warren himself was moved to a camp at Kranji, where he stayed until the Japanese surrender in August 1945. He went back to England believing that the murals had been destroyed in Allied bombing, and became an art teacher in London.

## Found again

After the war, the barracks became part of RAF Changi. Some years later, airmen clearing out an unused storeroom in Block 151 noticed pictures under the layers of distemper. Accounts differ on when this happened. The RAF Changi Association remembers it as the early 1950s, while other accounts give 1958. The story reached the Singapore papers in January 1959, when the Sunday Times printed a photograph of one mural under the headline "Do you know who painted this PoW camp mural?"

![A black and white photograph of low military buildings and palm trees at RAF Changi](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/0/06/Changi_Air_Base_1958.jpg)

*RAF Changi in 1958. (Bob Waddell, CC BY-SA 3.0, via Wikimedia Commons)*

The Sunday Times suggested that the murals might be the work of Ronald Searle, the cartoonist, who had also been a prisoner at Changi. His wife told reporters in London that he had not painted them. A reader in the RAF Changi Education Library then found Warren's name in a book, "The Churches of the Captivity in Malaya", and on the 1st of February 1959 the Sunday Times reported that the mystery had been solved. The Daily Mirror in London took up the search, and that month Warren was found living in London with his wife and son.

## Restoring his own work

Once the distemper had been scraped away, four complete murals and the top quarter of the fifth could be seen. In 1960 the RAF asked Warren to come back and restore them. He was reluctant at first, because the work brought back painful memories, but he made three trips to Singapore to do it, in December 1963, July 1982 and May 1988. On his first visit, the Straits Times described him as "a man with a mission". The 1982 trip was the most intensive, and officers and boys of the SAF Boys' School helped him with much of the work.

St Luke in Prison was never fully restored, because Warren could no longer remember the missing lower part. In 1985 his original drawing for it turned up among the belongings of a fellow prisoner, Wally Hammond, and it was given to the National Archives of Singapore. By 1988 Warren was not fit enough to repaint the mural, so he made a small picture from the drawing, which was placed beneath the surviving fragment. Stanley Warren died at his home in Bridport, in Dorset, on the 20th of February 1992, at the age of 75.

In October 1968 a British member of parliament, Charles Morris, asked the Defence Secretary, Denis Healey, to consider moving the murals to England, but nothing came of it, and in 1969 Singapore's Ministry of Defence took responsibility for them. In the 1990s the National Heritage Board marked the site as one of Singapore's historic sites. Most of the buildings around Block 151 were pulled down in July 2003, but the block itself still stands inside what is now the Changi Air Base camp, and access to the camp is restricted.

![A darkened exhibition gallery with a large projection screen and a wall panel headed The Interned](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/d/d9/Changi_chapel_museum.jpg/1280px-Changi_chapel_museum.jpg)

*Inside the Changi Chapel and Museum in July 2023. (Liaw Xin Ru, CC BY-SA 4.0, via Wikimedia Commons)*

Most visitors see the murals as replicas. The Changi Chapel and Museum, on Upper Changi Road North, has shown copies of them for many years, and the museum reopened in May 2021 after a redevelopment, with the replicas again among its main exhibits. A copy of one of the murals also hangs in the Garrison Church at Larkhill, in Wiltshire, England.

[See more photos of wartime Changi, RAF Changi and the museum →](/gallery/the-changi-murals-stanley-warrens-chapel-paintings-at-roberts-barracks/)

**Where it fits in the bigger story:** The Changi Murals were painted by a sick prisoner with scrounged materials, hidden for years under distemper, and traced back to their painter partly by chance and partly through a newspaper appeal. They are still on the walls where they were painted, and the man who painted them came back three times to repair them. Because the originals sit inside a working military camp, most Singaporeans know them, if at all, only through the replicas at the museum down the road.
