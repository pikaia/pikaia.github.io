In 1929 a pound of Malayan rubber sold for an average of 34 cents. By June 1932 it sold for 4.95 cents, the lowest price ever recorded. Singapore did not grow rubber, but it lived by processing, financing and shipping the rubber and tin of the Malay Peninsula, and much of both went to the United States, where the car industry had collapsed after the Wall Street crash of 1929. Singapore's later recessions have arrived by other routes as well, and setting them side by side shows a pattern: a crash abroad does the most damage when it cuts demand for the goods Singapore depends on most. That pattern is worth knowing today, when a large part of the island's growth comes from making the chips and equipment behind artificial intelligence.

## Rubber, tin and the American car

![Workers on an assembly line fitting parts to a row of motor cars in a Ford factory in the 1920s](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/8/86/Ford_Motor_Company_assembly_line.jpg)

*A Ford assembly line in 1928. American car makers were the largest buyers of Malayan rubber, for tyres. (Photographer unknown, from the Literary Digest, public domain, via Wikimedia Commons)*

Much of Malaya's rubber passed through Singapore to be graded, packed, insured and shipped. Tin from the mines of Perak and Selangor was smelted on Pulau Brani, an island off Singapore's harbour, and shipped from there too. According to the National Library Board's account of the Depression, Singapore was "dependent on international trade and the export of Malayan rubber and tin to the American market".

When the New York stock market crashed in October 1929, the American economy went into a slump, and the car industry with it. Fewer cars meant fewer tyres, and the price of rubber fell month after month. On 2 April 1931 the Straits Times ran the headline "Still Falling: New Low Record for Rubber" across the top of a page. The price in London had fallen to three and a quarter pence a pound, against an average of ten and a quarter pence in 1929, and the paper gave the reasons as large stocks, insufficient demand and "continued depression in America." A Singapore broker told the paper he was "resigned to anything now."

Price and volume fell together. Between 1929 and 1932, the value of Singapore's rubber exports fell by 84 per cent and the value of its tin exports by 68 per cent. By the middle of 1930, about 500 shops in Singapore had closed.

The slump then moved from goods to jobs. Estates and mines laid off workers, and the colony began sending unemployed labourers home. Recruiting of labourers in India was stopped on 1 August 1930. In the Federated Malay States, the government gave free passages to China to unemployed Chinese labourers, and in May 1932 the Straits Times reported that three thousand had left Perak in a single week. From August 1930 a quota limited the number of Chinese men allowed to land. Chinese male arrivals fell from 242,139 in 1930 to 27,796 in 1933, and between 1931 and 1933 about 443,000 more Chinese left Malaya than arrived.

The banks were next. Many customers of the Chinese banks were rubber planters and dealers, and in 1932 three of the banks merged to survive, forming the Oversea-Chinese Banking Corporation, the story of [an earlier post](/2026/09/30/the-chinese-banks-that-became-ocbc/).

The slump eased only when prices recovered. In July 1933 the Governor, Sir Cecil Clementi, declared that "the worst of the slump is over," and rising rubber and tin prices brought immigrants back in the mid-1930s.

The chain in 1929 to 1932 ran in one direction: a crash abroad, then a fall in demand for the one or two goods Singapore depended on, then prices and volumes falling together, then jobs, migrant workers and finally the banks.

**1985.** Singapore's first recession after independence was largely home-made. According to the National Library Board, a construction boom had ended, with the market saturated by the end of 1984, and high costs, together with a slowdown in the United States, hurt manufacturing. Construction fell by 17 per cent and manufacturing by 7.3 per cent, together taking 3.6 points off growth, while finance grew and added 1.6 points.

**1998.** The Asian financial crisis began with the collapse of the Thai baht in July 1997 and spread through currencies, banks and investors' confidence. In Singapore the biggest fall was in finance and insurance, down 20.2 per cent, which took 2.4 points off growth, more than the economy's whole fall of 2.2 per cent, while manufacturing barely changed. This was a crisis of money more than of goods.

**2001.** The dot-com bust is the closest match to the 1930s. In 2000, electronics made up 69 per cent of Singapore's non-oil domestic exports, the goods the island makes for export. When American technology spending collapsed, electronics exports fell by 19.9 per cent in 2001 and manufacturing by 11.6 per cent. Manufacturing alone took 3.0 points off growth, and the economy shrank by 1.1 per cent. Chips and computer parts had taken the place of rubber and tin.

**2008–09.** The global financial crisis began in American banks and reached Singapore through trade: transport and storage fell by 8.8 per cent in 2009 and manufacturing by 4.2 per cent, and electronics exports fell in both 2008 and 2009. It was partly a crisis of money and partly a crisis of goods.

**2020.** The pandemic followed neither route. Borders closed and, under the "circuit breaker" from 7 April to 1 June 2020, most workplaces shut. Construction fell by 41.7 per cent, hotels and food services by 20.2 per cent and business services by 12.4 per cent, while manufacturing grew by 7.5 per cent and added 1.5 points.

Taken together, Singapore's crises have arrived by two main routes. The goods route, a fall in demand for what Singapore makes and ships, was the route of 1930 and 2001 and part of 2009. The money route, through banks, currencies and confidence, was the route of 1998 and part of 2008.

The size of an industry matters as much as the size of its fall. Construction's 41.7 per cent drop in 2020 was the steepest of any industry in these five crises, but construction was then only about 3.5 per cent of the economy, so it cost 1.5 points of growth. Manufacturing's fall of 11.6 per cent in 2001 was far smaller in percentage terms, but manufacturing was about a quarter of the economy in 2000, and it cost 3.0 points. An economy that leans on one large industry is exposed when that industry falls. One spread across several industries of similar size is less exposed, because a fall in one can be offset by growth in others, as finance offset manufacturing in 1985 and 2001, and manufacturing offset construction and transport in 2020. Diversification does not help much against a shock that hits nearly everything at once, as the lockdown of 2020 did.

Singapore's economy is more evenly spread than it was in 2000. Measured at current prices, manufacturing's share of GDP fell from 25.9 per cent in 2000 to 17.4 per cent in 2025, while wholesale and retail trade grew from 12.5 to 19.7 per cent, and finance and insurance from 9.4 to 13.2 per cent.

## The AI question

Electronics matters to Singapore again. In August 2026 the Ministry of Trade and Industry, renamed the Ministry of Energy, Trade and Industry on 1 October 2026, raised its forecast for growth in 2026 to between 4.5 and 5.5 per cent, citing "the acceleration in global AI-related capital expenditure": spending, much of it in the United States, on the data centres, chips and equipment behind artificial intelligence. Electronics now makes up about a quarter of non-oil domestic exports, against more than two-thirds in 2000. Electronics exports have also fallen sharply twice in recent years without the economy shrinking. They dropped by 22.5 per cent in 2019 and by 19.7 per cent in 2023, while the economy grew by 1.2 and 1.5 per cent. A large financial and services sector, and exports such as pharmaceuticals, chemicals and machinery, now carry much of the load that electronics once did. What has not changed is the route. If a shock comes from technology spending abroad, it would reach Singapore by the goods route, the same route that carried the rubber slump in 1930 and the dot-com bust in 2001.

**Where it fits in the bigger story:** The Great Depression is usually remembered as an American and European disaster, but it reached Singapore within months, through the price of rubber and the tyres on American cars. Nearly a century later, the route a crisis takes into Singapore still depends on what the world is buying from it.
