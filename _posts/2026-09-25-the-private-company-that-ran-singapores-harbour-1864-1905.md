In 1905 the government of the Straits Settlements took over the Tanjong Pagar Dock Company and paid its shareholders about $755 for every $100 share, a price that was still being questioned in the British House of Commons more than a year later. The company had begun in 1864 as the first local joint-stock company to offer shares to the public in Singapore, and by the time it was bought out it controlled nearly all of the docks and wharves at the deep-water harbour that had made Singapore a coaling stop for steamships.

## Coal first, docks second

Steamships had to be fed. When the Peninsular and Oriental Steam Navigation Company (P&O) began monthly sailings to the Far East in 1845, with a stop at Singapore, the coal for its ships was carried out from ships anchored at the mouth of the Singapore River on small lighters, stored in godowns along the river bank, and then lightered out again to each arriving steamer. It was slow work, worse in choppy weather when a lighter could capsize, and damp stored coal caught fire easily.

In 1852 P&O became the first shipping company to move its coaling station from the river to New Harbour, where it built its own wharf. The anchorage was sheltered, the water was deep, and there was room for coal stores and warehouses, so other firms followed. New Harbour was renamed Keppel Harbour in 1900, after Admiral Henry Keppel, who had found deep water so close to the shore in 1848 when he sailed HMS Meander in for repairs. Ship repair was the next need. In 1859 a British mariner, Captain William Cloughton, built Singapore's first dry dock at New Harbour, and the Patent Slip and Dock Company was formed in 1861 to take it over.

## A company anyone could buy into

In 1864 a group of investors decided to build another dry dock at New Harbour. To raise the money they set up a joint-stock limited liability company, the Tanjong Pagar Dock Company, which was registered on 29 September 1864 and was the first local company of that kind to offer shares to the public in Singapore. The agency house Guthrie & Co. and the Chinese merchant Tan Kim Ching were among its founders. The company hoped to raise $200,000 in Singapore, in 2,000 shares of $100 each, but not all of the shares were taken up locally and the balance was sold to investors in London. Its articles of association gave a London Consulting Committee a veto over spending above $20,000, an arrangement that would matter forty years later.

Tan Kim Ching was the eldest of the three sons of Tan Tock Seng, and after his father's death he ran the family firm, which became Kim Ching & Co. and owned rice mills at Saigon and in Siam. He was Consul-General for Siam in the Straits Settlements. Song Ong Siang's 1923 history records that his name was on the committee of promoters when the dock company was started in 1863, that he was made a Justice of the Peace in 1865, and that when he died in February 1892 he owned the steamers *Siam* and *Singapore*. The same book notes that Ong Kew Ho, a Malacca-born merchant, was one of the few Chinese merchants who believed in the future of the enterprise and attended its annual general meeting in 1871.

## A price war, the Suez Canal and a monopoly

The company's first dock, Victoria Dock, was opened by Governor Harry St George Ord on 17 October 1868. It began badly. The Patent Slip and Dock Company, which now faced a competitor, started a price war, and the Tanjong Pagar company had to cut its own prices and made losses. Then the Suez Canal opened in 1869, more steamships called at Singapore, and by the following year the docks were profitable. The Patent company built a second dock in 1870 and became the New Harbour Dock Company in 1875, and in 1879 the Tanjong Pagar company opened Albert Dock, east of Victoria Dock, to meet the growing demand.

In 1871 a meeting was held at the office of Boustead & Co. to form a company to build a railway from the town to the company's wharves. The Hon. H. A. K. Whampoa was elected one of its provisional directors and was also a director of the dock company, which was then one of only two limited-liability companies in existence besides the proposed railway company, the other being the gas company. Meanwhile, the company bought up smaller rivals. In 1899 it merged with the New Harbour Dock Company, and with that purchase it controlled the whole of the shipping, dockyard and wharf business at New Harbour except the P&O wharf.

## The people who worked the wharves

Behind the docks was a large workforce. Song Ong Siang's history describes Chinese coolies carrying baskets of coal at the coal wharves, and the company's own album has a photograph titled "Coal coolies quarters." Much of the labour was arranged through contractors. Gan Eng Seng, who died in 1899, was labour contractor to the company and made a considerable part of his fortune from it, and from December 1901 Tan Kheam Hock held the same role, with a permanent labour force numbering some thousands. The company also kept its own police force and fire brigade.

## 1904–05: a modernisation plan and a takeover

By 1904 Singapore was the seventh-largest port in the world, but it faced strong competition from Hong Kong, and the company's wharf system was under strain because no major improvements had been carried out since 1885. The Singapore board and the London Consulting Committee disagreed about capital spending. In March 1904 the company submitted a $12-million plan to upgrade and expand its facilities, and it was rejected by its Europe-based shareholders, who feared the cost would "endanger a dividend of 12 per cent." The company then asked the Straits Settlements government for financial support. The government decided instead to take over the company. The London committee, for its part, had said it would not agree to cede control unless the dividend was assured at the existing rate.

The Tanjong Pagar Dock Bill was introduced in the Legislative Council on 20 January 1905, and its second reading was on 3 March. The Governor's stated ground, as recorded by Song Ong Siang, was that the company had obtained a practical monopoly of the port's shipping facilities, and that it was undesirable for commerce that they should all be under one company's control. The Hon. Mr Napier, supporting the bill, said that public opinion outside those interested in the company was practically unanimous in favour. He recalled that the first chairman, Thomas Scott, had said the company's motto should be "Live and let live," and argued that the company had since taken over the Slipway, the Borneo Wharf, Jardine's Wharf and finally the New Harbour Dock. The Hon. Tan Jiak Kim added that the company was not popular with the Chinese, and that for some years Chinese shipping firms had been trying to avoid sending their steamers and cargoes to Tanjong Pagar because of its "enormous charges." The bill passed on 13 April 1905, and on 1 July the government took over the management as the Tanjong Pagar Dock Board.

## What a share was worth

Compensation was settled by arbitration, with Lord St Aldwyn as umpire, and the award was declared on 14 July 1906. When the Under-Secretary of State for the Colonies, Winston Churchill, answered questions about it in the House of Commons on 28 November 1906, he said it worked out at about $755 per share. The National Library Board's account gives $761, against the $600 that is recorded as the highest price the share reached on the market.

The questioner, Josiah Wedgwood, put the other side of the case to the House: that the shares had been quoted at about $240 when expropriation was decided on, that the directors had mentioned $700 to the Governor, and that with a total capital of $3.7 million on which 12 per cent was paid, the award came to about $28.5 million, or sixty-five years' purchase of the annual dividend, against the fourteen years' purchase the Governor had said was usual. Churchill accepted that the figures were correct except in two respects. Twelve per cent, he said, was the smallest dividend paid in recent years and dividends did not accurately measure the value of the property, and the government had taken over a considerable amount of land not needed for the company's business. The Governor, he added, had told the directors that 7 per cent was the ordinary rate of interest in Singapore, which on that basis meant fourteen years' purchase of the ordinary dividend. He also confirmed that the arbitration would cost well over £40,000, of which Lord St Aldwyn received £5,750.

## What came after

The Tanjong Pagar Dock Board was reconstituted in 1913 as a statutory body, the Singapore Harbour Board, which that year opened King's Dock, the largest dock east of Suez, and completed Empire Dock in 1917. The Board kept the company's ship-repair business and controlled the whole chain of repair work at Keppel Harbour for the next five decades. Through later changes of name it became the Port of Singapore Authority in 1964, and eventually the Maritime and Port Authority of Singapore.

[See more historical photos related to this post →](/gallery/the-private-company-that-ran-singapores-harbour-1864-1905/)

**Where it fits in the bigger story:** Singapore's deep-water harbour is often told as a story about governors and navies. This part of it began with an agency house, a Chinese rice merchant and shareholders in Singapore and London who wanted a dry dock, and it ended with the government paying more than seven times face value to own what they had built, at a price still being argued over in London a year later. The docks, the coaling wharves and the people who worked them stayed in place. What changed in 1905 was who owned the port.
