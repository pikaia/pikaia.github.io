Dunman Road in Katong and Dunman High School carry the name of a police chief who was almost never seen in a uniform. In 1843, when gang robberies were a nightly event and Singapore's police were few, badly paid and poorly led, the East India Company's government handed the force to Thomas Dunman, a clerk in a merchant firm with no police training at all. He ran it for the next 28 years, and by the time he retired in 1871 he had turned it into a disciplined, full-time force.

## A town with too few police

Singapore's first police force was tiny. In 1820 William Farquhar, the first British Resident, put his son-in-law Francis James Bernard in charge of twelve men, among them a writer, a jailer, a sergeant and eight constables. In 1821 the merchants started a Night Watch Fund to pay for a few extra men to patrol after dark. By 1831 the force had only 18 men, and even after it was enlarged in the early 1840s to about 130, it was run part-time by the Sitting Magistrate, who had other duties.

The town grew much faster than its police. The surveyor John Turnbull Thomson, who arrived in 1841, wrote that the real head of the police was paid only £60 a year, while the governor received £5,000. Gangs of fifty or sixty men robbed houses near the town, and Thomson recalled finding the bodies of murdered men on the public roads. In February 1843 the government called a public meeting about the state of the police, and the European merchants sent strong complaints to the Company's government in Calcutta.

## A merchant's clerk takes charge

</div>

Dunman was born in England in 1814 and came to Singapore in 1840 as an assistant in the merchant house of Martin Dyce & Co. Later in 1843 the government appointed him Deputy Magistrate and Superintendent of Police. The Singapore Free Press welcomed the choice, writing that his "intimate acquaintance with the manners and habits of the natives" should help him build a better police.

The appointment was unusual. Senior posts in the Company's territories normally went to its own "covenanted" officers, who had signed a bond of service in London. Dunman was a "free" Englishman from outside that service. Thomson wrote that the government gave him only the title of deputy superintendent, so as not to set a precedent of an outsider holding the full post, and paid him a quarter of what one of its own men would have received.

Before he joined the police, Dunman had been well known in town as an amateur actor. He performed under the stage name "Mr. Johnson", and the historian Charles Burton Buckley later called him the best comic actor Singapore had seen. Once he was in charge of the police, the authorities hinted that the stage and the night watch did not go together, and he gave up acting.

## Building a force

Dunman's methods were personal. According to Buckley, he spent little time in his office and was never seen in a uniform. He drove around the town and the country roads at all hours, sometimes out at four in the morning and home at midnight. When a resident complained that he had seen Dunman driving along Orchard Road at night without lights, Dunman admitted it was not the right thing, but explained that with lights on he could not tell whether the police at the station were keeping watch. The heads of the Chinese community and even of the secret societies trusted him not to give away where his information came from.

He worked on the men as well as the system. By 1846 he had drawn up the force's first rules and regulations, set boundaries for each patrol beat and introduced a standard uniform. The Indian constables wore a red turban, a blue frock coat and white trousers, and the Malay constables a dark batik headcloth, a blue jacket and a dark blue sarong. He ran night classes in the police stations, because no man could be promoted to corporal until he could read and write, and he pressed for better pay, shorter hours and a pension scheme. By 1848 the force had a Superintendent, a Deputy, five European constables and 187 local men.

Some problems were beyond him. In 1846 he told the Recorder, the colony's judge, that the illegal gambling houses could not be put down, mainly because their keepers had the money to bribe the constables. A report by his deputy in 1856 found that the men did not share a common language, so that Chinese was not understood by the Indian and Malay constables, and each man kept his record books in his own language. Pay stayed low, and many Indian constables went home after two or three years.

His work also reached beyond policing. He served on the committee that ran the fund for the Pauper Hospital founded by Tan Tock Seng, and Buckley described him as "a sturdy beggar for helpless beggars", collecting money from wealthy merchants for the patients. After raids on cockfights he sometimes sent the hospital a handcart full of the dead birds to add to its meagre food supply.

## Riots and secret societies

<div style="float: left; max-width: 220px; width: 36%; margin: 0.25em 1.5em 1em 0;">
<img src="https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/2/2d/Membership_certificate_of_the_Ghee_Hin_Kongsi_from_William_Stirling_Collection.jpg" alt="A tall printed paper certificate with columns of Chinese characters inside a decorated border" style="width: 100%; display: block; border-radius: 4px;">
<em style="display: block; font-size: 0.8em; margin-top: 0.5em;">A membership certificate of the Ghee Hin, one of the largest Chinese secret societies in nineteenth-century Singapore. (National Museum of Singapore, CC BY-SA 4.0, via Wikimedia Commons)</em>
</div>

The greatest danger to order came from the Chinese secret societies, which were rival brotherhoods with thousands of members. In March 1846 the head of one society died, and the magistrates agreed to a funeral procession of no more than a hundred followers, keeping to the direct road to the burial ground. Nearly six thousand people gathered instead and set off to march through the town. When Dunman went to reason with the society's leaders, the crowd turned on his Chinese interpreter, and Dunman drove the attackers off with the butt of a musket and brought the interpreter away. Troops had to block the streets to keep the procession out of the town.

The worst violence came in May 1854. On the 5th, a quarrel over the weight of a measure of rice turned into fighting between Hokkien and Teochew Chinese, which spread through the town and into the countryside and lasted for ten to twelve days. Buckley put the dead at 400 or more, with about 300 houses burned. Dunman told Governor William Butterworth that the police could not stop it alone, but the governor rode into the town himself, was pelted by the mob and had to withdraw, and only then sent for the troops. About seventy European residents were sworn in as special constables, and Dunman, acting as a magistrate, read the Riot Act before a party of sepoys entered a house in Church Street from which the police had been fired on.

Buckley judged that the police had been "in good order and quite equal to what could be expected from their small number" against thousands of rioters. For his work during the riots Dunman received the thanks of the Governor-General of India in Council, and Butterworth presented him with a sword of honour.

## The first full-time Commissioner

Dunman had been made Superintendent of Police in January 1851, a promotion the Free Press called "a very tardy act of justice". He still had to share his time with work as a magistrate and with collecting the town's property tax. In 1856 a grand jury criticised what it called "the present disgraceful inefficiency" of the police, and the magistrates voted to give Dunman sole management of the force.

The Police Act of 1856 then separated the post of Commissioner of Police from that of the Resident Councillor, the senior official in Singapore. In 1857 Dunman became the first full-time Commissioner, on a salary of 1,000 rupees a month, and the Free Press noted a marked improvement in the force once he could give it all his time. In the years that followed the police set up stations along the coast against piracy, a Marine Police and a Detective Department to deal with the secret societies.

In 1865, after clashes between two rival societies among the Indian community the year before, Dunman and Governor Orfeur Cavenagh banned the Muharram procession, and the societies' leaders were later tried and convicted. Dunman retired on a pension in 1871, after 28 years in charge.

## After the police

<img src="https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/d/d9/John_Edmund_Taylor%2C_Under_the_Cocoa_Nut_Trees._The_Bungalows._Tanjong_Katong%2C_Singapore._%281879%2C_Wellcome_V0037492%29.jpg/1280px-John_Edmund_Taylor%2C_Under_the_Cocoa_Nut_Trees._The_Bungalows._Tanjong_Katong%2C_Singapore._%281879%2C_Wellcome_V0037492%29.jpg" alt="A watercolour of tall coconut palms over sandy ground, with low bungalows with red roofs among the trees" style="width: 100%; display: block; border-radius: 4px;">

*Bungalows under the coconut palms at Tanjong Katong, painted in 1879. (John Edmund Taylor, CC BY 4.0, via Wikimedia Commons)*

Dunman had long had interests outside the police. He owned a large coconut plantation at Tanjong Katong, which became known as Grove Estate and stretched along what is now Mountbatten Road. He built his own house there and three bungalows on the shore, which Buckley described as the first seaside houses at Tanjong Katong for holidays and honeymoons. In town, he had a swampy piece of land opposite the police station filled in, and the open space became known as Dunman's Green. He was also a founding member of the Orchard Road Presbyterian Church, and in 1865 he chaired the interim committee that founded the Tanglin Club.

After he retired, Dunman spent four more years in Singapore looking after his plantation, then left for England in 1875. He died at Bournemouth in October 1887, aged 73. In 1876 Dunman's Green was renamed Hong Lim Green after the merchant Cheang Hong Lim, who had bought the land and given it to the government, and it is now Hong Lim Park.

## What came after him

The force he left behind kept changing. The Police Force Ordinance of 1871, which took effect in 1872, was the first official definition of police duties in the Straits Settlements. In 1877 the government set up the Chinese Protectorate under William Pickering to deal with Chinese immigration and the secret societies, a commission of inquiry examined the police in 1879, and in 1881 the first Sikh policemen were recruited. Buckley, writing in 1902, believed the police had never been quite the same after Dunman left, and that a force in such a mixed town worked best under someone who knew its people rather than an officer brought in from elsewhere.

That idea has returned in a modern form. In 1983 the Singapore Police Force began setting up Neighbourhood Police Posts, adapted from the *kōban* police boxes of Japan, so that officers would be based in housing estates and get to know the residents. By 1993 there were 91 of them across the island, and in the following years they were replaced by larger Neighbourhood Police Centres. In 2020 the Singapore Police Force marked 200 years since Farquhar's first twelve men.

Dunman Road runs through Katong, close to where his coconut estate once stood, and the nearby Dunman Food Centre and Dunman Lane share the name. Dunman High School, which moved to Dunman Road in 1957, was named after him, and so was Dunman Secondary School. A plaque in the Orchard Road Presbyterian Church remembers him as a founder member and benefactor of the congregation.

**Why it matters today:** Singapore's reputation as a safe city took a long time to build, and its first steps came from an unlikely source. A merchant's clerk who was hired because no Company officer was suitable showed that a small, poorly paid police force could win the trust of a mixed and often divided town by knowing its people. His name is on a road, a food centre and two schools, while the story of how he built the force is far less well known.
