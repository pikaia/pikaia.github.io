For most of its recorded history, the island we now call Sentosa was Pulau Blakang Mati, a working island of fishing villages, fruit gardens and, from 1878, British forts and barracks. By 1959 about 1,000 people lived in two kampongs on its northern shore, many of them working for the soldiers. In 1970 the island was renamed Sentosa and rebuilt as a resort, and its villagers moved to new flats across the harbour at Telok Blangah.

## A name and an island

<div style="float: left; max-width: 300px; margin: 0 1.5em 1em 0;">
<img src="https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/f/f6/Map_of_Singapura_by_Godinho_de_Er%C3%A9dia_1604.jpg" alt="An old hand-drawn map of the island of Singapore and the small islands to its south, with place names in Portuguese" style="width: 100%;">
<em>Manuel Godinho de Erédia's map of Singapore, first drawn in 1604, which marks a small island called "Blacanmati" to the south. (Manuel Godinho de Erédia, public domain, via Wikimedia Commons)</em>
</div>

The name is old. The Portuguese mapmaker Manuel Godinho de Erédia, who was born in Malacca, marked an island called "Blacanmati" south of Singapore on his map of 1604, and later European maps gave it other names, among them Pulau Panjang, "long island". In Malay, Blakang Mati is usually translated as "behind death" or "dead back", and nobody is sure how it came about. One story ties it to the pirates who preyed on ships in the narrow strait beside it. Another says that islanders who died fighting pirates were buried there.

The island sat beside the main passage into what became Keppel Harbour, and ships often anchored off its western tip to wait for the tide. In the early 1830s a visiting naturalist found neat Malay houses near a sandy beach, coconut palms and small plantations of sugar cane and yams. In 1833 the British put up a flag signal station on Mount Serapong to warn the town of approaching ships, but so many of its staff fell ill with fever that it was moved to Mount Faber in 1845.

In 1848 a British doctor, Robert Little, published an account of the island after visiting it to investigate that fever. He described three villages, one of Bugis settlers, one of Malays and one of Bugis and Chinese. He estimated that pineapples covered at least 200 acres, and he called Blakang Mati "one vast pinery". The Bugis, he wrote, earned more than the average in Singapore from their fruit and fishing, and their women wove sarongs of silk and gold thread.

## The fortress island

As early as 1827 a British officer warned that an enemy who took Blakang Mati would command the harbour. Arguments over strategy and cost delayed the defences for half a century, until work began in 1878 on gun batteries at Mount Siloso, in the west, and at the eastern end of the island, later named Fort Connaught. Forts at Serapong and Imbiah followed. By 1887 the colonial government had bought out all the private land on the island, which may have meant clearing some of the old villages, but in the 1890s it began issuing leases again for villages, fishing grounds and plantations. The [Fort Palmer post](/2026/10/04/fort-palmer-fort-canning-and-the-forts-that-guarded-a-free-port/) tells the story of the guns themselves.

![A small white building with a red tiled roof beside a long covered walkway and gun emplacements on a green, wooded hillside](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/c/c3/Fort_Siloso%2C_Singapore_202410.jpg/1280px-Fort_Siloso%2C_Singapore_202410.jpg)

*Fort Siloso, at the western tip of the island, in 2024. (FN-082, CC BY-SA 4.0, via Wikimedia Commons)*

Around the forts grew a garrison town: barracks blocks around a parade ground, bungalows for officers, an Officers' Mess on a hill, a military hospital, cookhouses, canteens, sea-bathing enclosures and a cinema. Life there followed the colonial order. The Indian gunners of the Hong Kong and Singapore Royal Artillery had separate quarters and lower pay than British soldiers, and spent their free time in different places. Local people did much of the work: Indian laundrymen washed uniforms, Chinese cooks and waiters ran the dining rooms, and a store in the village near the landing sold butter, tinned milk and sweets on credit. A sampan to town cost 10 or 20 cents.

## War

When war came to Singapore, Blakang Mati had the largest concentration of guns in the fire command that guarded the harbour. About 100 bombs fell on the island on 18 January. From 11 February its guns fired inland at Japanese positions in the west, and Fort Connaught shelled the captured airfield at Tengah. Early on 14 February, with false reports of Japanese landings coming in through the smoke of burning oil tanks, Fort Siloso's guns were blown up, and Connaught's followed about two hours later. Singapore surrendered the next day.

![A coloured military map of Pulau Blakang Mati and the islands around it, with contour lines, roads, buildings and place names such as Pulau Brani, Berhala Reping and Tanjong China](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/3/31/P_Blakang_Mati_1945_map.jpg)

*Pulau Blakang Mati on a British military map published in 1945, before reclamation changed the island's shape. (Terry Fitzpatrick, extract from a 1945 Survey Production Centre map, public domain, via Wikimedia Commons)*

The Japanese renamed the island Kashima, "deer island", and an air force supply unit stored its bombs in the island's magazines. Between 400 and 1,000 Allied prisoners of war were held in two of its barrack blocks and made to load and maintain the bombs. Days after the surrender, prisoners on the island watched as Chinese civilians, their hands tied, were pushed from boats into the sea and shot. They were among the victims of the Sook Ching, the Japanese military's purge of the Chinese in Singapore. One officer later told a war crimes trial that the prisoners recovered and buried about 118 bodies, most of them workers of the Singapore Harbour Board. A historic site marker at Berhala Reping, now inside Sentosa's golf club, records the killings.

## The kampongs

After the war the British returned, and so did the islanders. In 1946 there were 377 Malay, Chinese and Tamil residents. By 1959 there were about 1,000, living mostly in two kampongs on the northern shore. The village by the jetty, facing Keppel Harbour, was mainly Hokkien, and stood where Universal Studios is today. Many of its families were Yeos from Meishan village in Dongshan county, Fujian, who had settled on the island early in the 20th century and spoke the Zhao'an variety of Hokkien. The village further west was mainly Malay, with a few Chinese families who ran its provision shops. When the Prime Minister, Lee Kuan Yew, visited in 1964, he noted that about three-quarters of its people had roots in Riau and Java. Former residents remember the two villages sending each other food at Chinese New Year and Hari Raya.

In the first half of the century most islanders who did not work for the army fished or ran small ferry boats. By the 1950s fishing was mostly a pastime, and the military and the boats were the main employers. People worked for the garrison as tailors, shoemakers, laundrymen, cooks and domestic servants. A tailor made suits for British officers going home on leave and white shirts for the Gurkhas, and a local photographer took the army's ceremonial pictures. In the early 1960s, as more ships anchored off Singapore, many men took up ferrying passengers between the ships and the shore. One boatman's son recalled that once his father had a boat, the family no longer worried about money for Chinese New Year.

The island still belonged to the British War Department, and the villagers lived there on its terms. Since 1945 they had travelled free on the army ferries to Jardine Steps, but in June 1948 the army announced that from 1 July the ferries would carry only service personnel, their families and people employed by the services, because it had fewer boats and crews. Everyone else had to pay 30 to 50 cents a trip by sampan. Islanders wrote to the newspapers asking the army to be a good neighbour, and were reminded that people not working for it had no legal right to live on the island at all.

Until 1959 the villages drew water from springs and wells, or bought it from the army's reservoirs, and in the 1950s, when the wells ran short, the Rural Board paid the army to ship water over from the mainland. That year the British Army laid a 3.2-kilometre pipeline from Keppel Harbour, through Pulau Brani, to a reservoir on the island, with free public standpipes for the villagers. Children went to an army primary school, which took some kampong children, or to a small Chinese school, Sin Hwa. In 1964 the government built the Blakang Mati Integrated Primary School, which taught in Malay, English and Chinese. The island also had a mosque near the ferry landing, a surau, a Chinese temple, a Hindu temple and a chapel for the soldiers. From 1957 to 1969 its barracks housed two battalions of Gurkhas, some of them with their families, who went to the island's weddings and other gatherings.

## From Blakang Mati to Sentosa

Under the agreement that brought Singapore into Malaysia in 1963, Britain released 658 acres of Blakang Mati, and on 1 September 1967 the island passed to Singapore. The Singapore Armed Forces took over the barracks, and the new government weighed several uses for the island: a free port, a deep-water port, military bases and a tourist resort. In 1967 the Minister for Law and National Development, E. W. Barker, summed up the competing claims and added, "I sincerely hope tourism will not be left out." For a time an oil refinery for Esso was planned there. The government's Dutch economic adviser, Albert Winsemius, argued that a richer Singapore would one day need space for leisure, and after he and the planner Alan Choe put their case to the Prime Minister, Esso agreed to build in Jurong instead.

By late 1968 the government had decided to make the island a resort. A contest to rename it was launched in November 1969, and from September 1970 Blakang Mati became Sentosa, Malay for "tranquillity". The Sentosa Development Corporation was set up in 1972, and the cable car from Mount Faber, its first major attraction, opened in February 1974. In the early 1970s the villagers were moved to the mainland, many of them to new Housing and Development Board flats at Telok Blangah. One former resident remembered that many people were sad to leave, afraid that the community would be split up, and that she still went back to stay a day or two at her old house. Some islanders found jobs at the new attractions, and the integrated school's site became Sentosa's Maritime Museum.

## An island for unusual jobs

Sitting apart from the main island, Sentosa was chosen for some unusual tasks. In 1971 Singapore's first satellite earth station was built on the slopes of Mount Serapong, away from buildings that could interfere with its signals, and on 8 August that year it brought in Singapore's first television pictures by satellite from overseas, of the Apollo 15 splashdown. In May 1989 Chia Thye Poh, a former Barisan Sosialis member of parliament, was released after more than 22 years of detention without trial under the Internal Security Act. The government said he had been a member of the Communist Party of Malaya, which he denied. He was required to live on Sentosa, in a reconstructed guardhouse at Fort Siloso, and later worked there as a freelance translator for the Sentosa Development Corporation, until he was allowed to move to the mainland in November 1992.

![A man in a dark suit seen from behind, walking along a white colonnade with tall shuttered openings and a red carpet](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/3/34/Trump_walking_in_the_colonnade_of_the_Capella_Hotel.jpg)

*Donald Trump walking along the colonnade of the Capella hotel during the summit, 12 June 2018. (Shealah Craighead, The White House, public domain, via Wikimedia Commons)*

On 12 June 2018 the island hosted the first meeting between a serving United States president, Donald Trump, and a North Korean leader, Kim Jong Un. They met at the Capella hotel, whose front is formed by the restored Officers' Mess of the old garrison. Among the police who secured it were members of the Gurkha Contingent of the Singapore Police Force, half a century after the Gurkha battalions had left the island.

## What lasted

Fort Siloso is now a museum of the island's military history, and in 2022 eleven of its structures were gazetted together as a National Monument. The barracks around the old parade ground were conserved in 2004 and turned into hotels, restaurants and a spa, and the parade ground is now the Barracks Lawn. The Officers' Mess, conserved in 2000, is part of the Capella, and the old military hospital became the waxworks museum Madame Tussauds. Of the kampongs nothing is left: the Hokkien village by the jetty lies under Universal Studios, part of Resorts World Sentosa, which opened in 2010 with Singapore's first casino. The Yeo clan association set up in the 1960s still brings former islanders together for dinners and festivals in honour of the deity Tua Pek Kong.

**Why it matters:** Sentosa is one of Singapore's most visited places, but few of the people who go there for its beaches and theme parks know that it was a home for fishing families and boatmen, a garrison for British and Indian gunners, a prisoner-of-war camp and a place of wartime killings. The island's conserved forts and barracks keep the military story visible, while the kampong story survives mainly in oral histories and in the clan association its former residents still keep up. Next time you take the cable car across, look for the red roofs of the old barracks among the trees, and remember the villages that stood by the jetty below.
