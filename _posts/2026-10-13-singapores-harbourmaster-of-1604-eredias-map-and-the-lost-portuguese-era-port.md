In 1604 the Malacca-born Portuguese mapmaker Manuel Godinho de Erédia drew a small sketch map of the waters around Singapore. Along the island's shore he wrote a handful of Malay place names, among them Tanah Merah, Bedok and Tanjong Rhu, which are still on the map today, and one word which is not: Xabandaria, the quarters of the shahbandar, the harbourmaster. Two hundred years after the fall of the Singapura of the Malay Annals, and two hundred years before Raffles, the island still had a port, and someone was in charge of it.

## The map

The sketch is titled, in Portuguese, a "chorographic description of the straits of Singapore and Sabam", and it is dated 1604. It is one of the maps in Erédia's Description of Malacca, Southern India and Cathay, a book he finished in about 1613. The original manuscript is in the Royal Library in Brussels, and it became widely known after a facsimile was printed there in 1882.

The map shows Singapore as an island set in the mouth of the Johor River, with the two channels that ships could take past it. The Old Strait ran north of the island along today's Johor Strait, and the New Strait ran south of it, past Blakang Mati, the island now called Sentosa. Along the east coast Erédia wrote, in Portuguese spellings, the names of Tanjong Rusa near Changi, Tanah Merah, Sungei Bedok and Tanjong Rhu, and then the word Xabandaria. Its exact spot is uncertain. The National Library reads it as lying west of Tanjong Rhu, and the historian Peter Borschberg, who has written most about these maps, places it somewhere between Tanjong Rhu and Pulau Brani, the stretch of shore that takes in the mouth of the Singapore River.

Erédia was born in Malacca on 16 July 1563. His father, João de Erédia Aquaviva, was a Portuguese official, and his mother, Dona Elena Vessiva, was a Bugis noblewoman from Sulawesi, the daughter of a recently baptised king of Suppa. He went to the Jesuit school in Malacca and at 13 was sent to the Jesuit college in Goa, where he learned mathematics, astronomy and mapmaking. He spent most of his career in Portuguese India as a cosmographer, engineer and soldier.

In 1601 he was sent back to Malacca to lead an expedition in search of "Meridional India", a land of gold believed to lie to the south. The war around the Straits kept him there for four years instead. Borschberg writes that in 1604 Erédia led a Portuguese squadron exploring Singapore, the straits and the Johor River, which is when the sketch is dated, though the National Library's account doubts that he ever set foot on the island. He fell ill, went back to Goa in about 1605, and spent his last years writing. He died in 1623, and none of his books was printed in his lifetime.

## The harbourmaster

The word shahbandar comes from Persian and means, roughly, the lord of the harbour. In the Malay port kingdoms he was one of the great officials of the court. The 15th-century laws of Melaka name four of them, each in charge of the foreign merchants from one part of the world. The shahbandar looked after the foreign merchants and their ships, including the dues they owed the ruler.

<div style="float: right; max-width: 280px; width: 40%; margin: 0.25em 0 1em 1.5em;">
<img src="https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/2/28/Malaca_%28manuel_godinho_de_eredia_1604%29.jpg" alt="A pen-and-ink plan of a walled town by a river, with numbered buildings and a key below" style="width: 100%; display: block; border-radius: 4px;">
<em style="display: block; font-size: 0.8em; margin-top: 0.5em;">Erédia's plan of Portuguese Malacca within its walls, also dated 1604, from the same manuscript. (Manuel Godinho de Erédia, public domain, via Wikimedia Commons)</em>
</div>

After the Portuguese captured Melaka in 1511, the sultan's family fled south and set up the sultanate of Johor, with its capitals up the Johor River. Singapore became one of Johor's outposts, guarding the approach to the river. A shahbandar on the island means that enough foreign ships still called there for the sultan to keep a senior official on the spot.

<div style="float: left; max-width: 280px; width: 40%; margin: 0.25em 1.5em 1em 0;">
<img src="https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/7/76/Cornelis_Matelieff_de_Jonge_%281570-1632%29%2C_gekozen_in_1602%2C_SK-A-4491.jpg/1280px-Cornelis_Matelieff_de_Jonge_%281570-1632%29%2C_gekozen_in_1602%2C_SK-A-4491.jpg" alt="An oval painted portrait of a bearded man in a black coat with a white ruff collar" style="width: 100%; display: block; border-radius: 4px;">
<em style="display: block; font-size: 0.8em; margin-top: 0.5em;">Cornelis Matelief, the Dutch admiral whom the shahbandar of Singapore took to meet the sultan of Johor in 1606, in a portrait in the Rijksmuseum. (Rijksmuseum, CC0, via Wikimedia Commons)</em>
</div>

The Dutch records name him. In 1606 the Dutch admiral Cornelis Matelief met the "Sabander van Sincapura", the shahbandar of Singapore, who took him to meet Sultan Alauddin Riayat Shah III. The meeting led to an alliance between Johor and the Dutch against the Portuguese. The Flemish gem trader Jacques de Coutre, who spent the 1590s and early 1600s trading in these waters, called Singapore "Ysla de la Sabandaria Vieja", the island of the old shahbandar's quarters, and wrote that its port was one of the best that served the East Indies.

<div style="clear: both;"></div>

## A contested strait

<div style="float: left; max-width: 240px; width: 40%; margin: 0.25em 1.5em 1em 0;">
<img src="https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/4/4e/Portret_van_Jacob_van_Heemskerck%2C_RP-P-OB-48.603.jpg/1280px-Portret_van_Jacob_van_Heemskerck%2C_RP-P-OB-48.603.jpg" alt="An engraved oval portrait of a bearded man in armour with a lace collar" style="width: 100%; display: block; border-radius: 4px;">
<em style="display: block; font-size: 0.8em; margin-top: 0.5em;">Jacob van Heemskerck, the Dutch admiral who captured the Santa Catarina off Singapore in 1603, in an 18th-century engraving. (Rijksmuseum, CC0, via Wikimedia Commons)</em>
</div>

In the late 16th century the waters around Singapore became a war zone. In 1587 a Portuguese fleet under Dom Paulo de Lima Pereira destroyed Johor Lama, the sultan's fortified capital on the Johor River, and the court moved further upstream to Batu Sawar. From the 1590s the Dutch arrived to compete for the spice and China trade, and Johor's rulers saw in them a counterweight to Portuguese Malacca. In the months before the trading season of 1603, the Portuguese commander André Furtado de Mendonça blockaded the Johor River.

On 25 February 1603, three Dutch ships under Admiral Jacob van Heemskerck caught the Portuguese carrack Santa Catarina off the east coast of Singapore. She was a ship of about 1,500 tons, on her way from Macau to Malacca, and after a few hours of fighting her crew gave up the ship and her cargo in return for their lives. The cargo included about 1,200 bales of Chinese raw silk and a great quantity of Ming porcelain, and when the porcelain was sold in the Netherlands this kind of blue-and-white became known there as "kraak" porcelain, after the carracks that carried it. The sale raised the capital of the new Dutch East India Company by more than half.

Not everyone in the Netherlands approved of the seizure, and the Company asked a young lawyer, Hugo Grotius, to defend it. His argument, part of which was published in 1609 as Mare Liberum, "The Free Sea", held that the oceans belonged to no one and were open to the ships of every nation. It became one of the founding texts of international law, and it began with a carrack taken off Singapore.


<div style="clear: both;"></div>

## What the ground says

The map and the Dutch and Portuguese reports are not the only evidence. In 1998, before the Empress Place Building on the north bank of the Singapore River became the Asian Civilisations Museum, archaeologists led by John Miksic dug on the riverbank in front of it. They found that the site had been in use from the 14th through the 16th centuries, through the time of Melaka and then of Johor. There was plenty of Ming Chinese porcelain, including pieces from the 15th century, which is rare outside China, and goods from Vietnam, Thailand, Java, Borneo, the Philippines and Persia. In their report on the dig, Goh Geok Yian and Miksic describe it as a place where goods from much of maritime Asia were loaded, unloaded and passed on, and suggest that the harbourmaster named on Erédia's map probably lived on or near the site.

## 1613

![A hand-coloured 16th-century map of Southeast Asia with the Malay Peninsula, Sumatra, Borneo and the coast of China, decorated with compass roses and sea creatures](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/0/02/Exacta_et_Accurata_Delineatio_1595.jpg/1280px-Exacta_et_Accurata_Delineatio_1595.jpg)

*A Dutch map of Southeast Asia published in 1595, drawn from Portuguese sources and used by the first Dutch fleets that came to compete for the trade of these waters. (Hendrik Florisz. van Langren, public domain, via Wikimedia Commons)*

The port did not survive the wars for long. On 4 May 1613 a great fleet sent by Sultan Iskandar Muda of Aceh, in northern Sumatra, sailed into the Johor River. Batu Sawar fell on 6 June, and the Acehnese attacked the other towns of the river and the coast, among them the town of Singapore. Borschberg writes that Singapura was burned along with the upstream towns, perhaps because Johor's fleet was based there. Many older histories tell it differently, saying that the Portuguese burned the settlement at the mouth of the Singapore River in 1613. Either way, the island's port does not recover in the written record.

The name lingered for a while. A Portuguese chart of the straits drawn in 1654 still marks a "Xebandaria" on the island in one of its copies. After that the island drops out of the records for most of two centuries, home to communities of orang laut, the sea people, and passed by the ships that used its straits. In 1811 about a hundred Malays came over from Johor under the Temenggong and settled at the mouth of the Singapore River, and that was the village Raffles found when he arrived in January 1819.

**Why it matters today:** Singapore's story is often told as if Raffles found an empty island in 1819, but Erédia's map shows that two centuries earlier it was still a working port with a harbourmaster, a place where ships stopped, paid their dues and traded, and where a quarrel between European powers over a captured cargo helped give the world the idea of the free sea. The job the shahbandar did then is done today by the Maritime and Port Authority of Singapore, set up in 1996, which runs one of the busiest ports in the world. Two words on a small Portuguese sketch are among the few traces left of the port that came before it.
