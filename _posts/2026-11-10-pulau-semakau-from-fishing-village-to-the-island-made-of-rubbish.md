In 1977, Minah Bte Gap and her husband Rani Bin Omar were resettled from Pulau Semakau to a one-room rental flat at Telok Blangah, on the Singapore mainland. They rarely slept in it. They gave the flat to their son, went back across the water, and lived on in a run-down beach hut on the island they had been moved out of — fishing, selling the good catches to the islanders of neighbouring Pulau Seking, cooking fish and rice on a kerosene stove. Minah was ninety when the makers of the *Island Nation* documentary found the story. "Our hearts are attached to the island," she said. "We can't do the same things on Singapore, no?" The island she could not leave is now Singapore's landfill — the only one it has left, an island quite literally made of the country's rubbish. This is the story of the two fishing villages that stood where the rubbish goes, of the strange living island the landfill turned out to be, and of the newest turn in its life: the effort to dig the rubbish back up, in a manner of speaking, and build with it.

## Two islands, two villages

Pulau Semakau lies about eight kilometres south of the main island, among the southern islands. Its name is thought to come from *bakau*, the mangrove: in 1844 it was recorded as Mangrove Island, home to about a dozen Chinese smallholders growing vegetables, raising poultry and burning charcoal. More settlers came in the early twentieth century, principally Teochew Chinese, and the village that grew up — Kampong Tengah, some 160 houses on the island's western side — was a mixed Chinese and Malay community. By 1955 it held about 780 people, who farmed, kept goats and poultry, and fished from small *kolek* boats with traps and nets: bream, mullet, grouper, snapper, stingray. Most of the catch was sold to middlemen on the mainland.

It was a self-contained world, and a hard one. The wells were unreliable, so fresh water was ferried over from Pulau Bukom. Electricity came only in the evenings, when the generator ran. There were no sewers; the communal latrines stood over the water. Medical care arrived once a week, in the person of a nurse who came by boat from Pulau Brani and was known as the Floating Dispensary. But the village also had a mosque, a community centre, a football field, a police post and burial grounds, and from 1951 a primary school with seven teachers. One of those seven was a young man named Sidek Saniff — who would grow up to become Singapore's Senior Minister of State for the Environment, in the ministry whose agency now runs the landfill on his old school's island. In the late 1960s the village took in neighbours: families resettled from Pulau Bukom as the petrochemical works there expanded. The southern islands' people were being shuffled from island to island long before anyone mentioned a landfill.

Ten minutes away by sampan lay the second island, and the older world. Pulau Seking — also spelled Sakeng — was tiny, about five hundred metres by two hundred, its houses on pillars over the water around most of its coast. Its people considered themselves Orang Selat, "people of the Straits", one of the peoples collectively called Orang Laut, the sea people who were the original inhabitants of these shores. They had lived on Seking since before colonial times; finds on the island date to the fourteenth century or earlier, and anthropologists who worked there in the late 1970s called it "the final link to pre-Raffles Singapore", its families still kin to the islanders of Pulau Pangkil across the water in Riau. In 1957 the census counted 217 people; by 1980 there were 525. They fished and built boats, as their ancestors had, and as Pulau Bukom grew into an oil refinery many of them crossed over to work there as technicians. The island had its mosque, its schools, its clinic, its police post, and a provision shop run from a stilt house by two brothers, Teo Yen Eng and Teo Yen Teck. Island lore said the place took its name from Keng, a woman of Riau blood who arrived more than two hundred years ago, healed the sick, defended the island against pirates, and became its chieftain; her shrine stood on the hill until the bulldozers came. The southern islands also took turns hosting a year-end carnival with the Riau islanders — swimming, diving and rowing races, a greasy-pole contest, sepak takraw, top-spinning — a whole sea-going society's calendar, which faded as the visits grew harder and the islands emptied one by one.

## Two endings

The villages did not end together, and they did not end for the same reason. Kampong Tengah was relocated to the mainland in 1977, almost twenty years before the landfill, for reasons of the islands' general development. Some went quietly; some did not. Mak Imah, ordered to resettle, dismantled her house on Semakau, carried it across to Pulau Pemping in Riau, and rebuilt it there, rather than move to a mainland flat. Minah and Rani took the flat and then, in effect, declined it. The island was left to nature for about twenty years; along its western shore you can still find the eroded piles and foundations of the village.

Seking's goodbye was longer and harder, because Seking's people had further to fall — from the oldest continuous community in Singapore's waters to none at all. Development was first announced in June 1975. In 1983 it was announced that Jurong Town Corporation would take over the island by the end of the decade or the early 1990s, and there were public calls to save it; the reply was that it was earmarked for long-term industrial use, with no immediate plan. By 1993 the population had fallen to 139. On 11 November 1993 the Minister for the Environment, Mah Bow Tan, told Parliament that the landfill would be built in the sea between Seking and Semakau. Kanwaljit Soin, a Nominated Member of Parliament, appealed for Seking to be excluded on heritage grounds. The appeal was refused: keeping the little island out of the scheme would have cost another S$130 million for an alternative site. In March 1994 Berita Harian ran the headline "Raya terakhir di Pulau Seking" — the last Hari Raya on Pulau Seking. That year the remaining hundred and thirty or so residents left for Housing Board flats on the mainland, and the community, in the words of the record, became dispersed. Saniah Binte Ali, the widow of Mohamed Yatim Bin Akib, Seking's last penghulu — its headman — remembered the exchange plainly: on the island, she said, grandmother was free, could walk here, walk there; in the flat it was like being caged.

## The mainland runs out of ground

Why did Singapore need its sea for a rubbish tip at all? Because the mainland had filled up. The Lorong Halus dumping ground in the north-east, opened in 1970, had at one point taken almost half the country's rubbish, and for years the neighbours complained of its smell, its open fires and its pollution. The Lim Chu Kang ground in the west filled and closed in 1992. Lorong Halus itself closed on 31 March 1999, completely full. Semakau Landfill opened the next morning.

Building it took from 1995 to 1999 and, on the National Environment Agency's own history, about S$610 million. The design was simple to describe and enormous to do: a seven-kilometre wall of rock — a perimeter bund — drawn through the sea between Pulau Semakau and Pulau Seking, enclosing 350 hectares of water. The bund is lined with an impermeable membrane and a layer of marine clay, so that the liquid that drains from buried waste stays inside and is treated on site rather than leaking into the strait. The enclosed sea was divided into cells. And on top of little Pulau Seking itself — the island of the Orang Selat, of Keng's shrine and the Teo brothers' shop — the engineers built the landfill's receiving station, the cavernous hall where the barges dock. The village island is still there, in a sense. It is the front door of the tip.

## What arrives, and how

Almost nothing that Singapore throws away goes to Semakau as rubbish. The country's waste goes first to its waste-to-energy plants, where burning cuts its volume by about ninety per cent. What cannot be burned, and what the burning leaves behind, is what makes the journey. Residues are trucked to the Tuas Marine Transfer Station on the mainland, loaded into covered barges, and pushed by tugs on a 33-kilometre sea journey to Semakau — the barges travel at night, and the whole operation is arranged so that the island takes delivery while Singapore sleeps. At the receiving station, excavators with specially designed grabs lift the ash out of the barges and into 35-tonne off-road dump trucks, which run along a ten-metre-wide road on top of the bund to whichever cell is active, where bulldozers and compactors level and compact the fill. On the agency's 2019 figures the island receives about 2,100 tonnes a day — some 1,500 tonnes of incineration ash and 600 tonnes of non-incinerable waste, about 700,000 tonnes a year. Before the ash is even barged, a plant at Tuas run by REMEX, operating since 2015, pulls tens of thousands of tonnes of metal out of it with magnets and eddy-current separators, cutting its weight by about a tenth. When a cell is full it is covered with earth — and then, disconcertingly for anyone expecting a tip, it turns green. Grass and trees take root over the buried ash, and a filled cell becomes a meadow with a view of the sea.

## The island that stayed alive

The surprise of Semakau — the thing that has drawn journalists to call it a "Garbage of Eden" — is that the landfill was built against a living shore, and the shore is still living. The landfill was anchored to Semakau's eastern side; the western shoreline, with its mangroves, seagrass meadows and reef flats, was left natural. Where construction did take nature away, some of it was put back: building the bund cost 13.6 hectares of mangrove on the eastern shore, and the agency replanted two plots totalling the same 13.6 hectares just outside the bund. The replanted mangroves thrive — and that is not just decoration. Healthy mangroves growing hard against the bund are taken as living evidence that the liners beneath are not leaking. The landfill's first alarm system is a forest.

At low tide the western flats are a roll-call of things that are rare everywhere else in Singapore. The island holds the country's largest visitable bed of tape seagrass, stretching for kilometres, and the only known local stand of another seagrass, *Syringodium isoetifolium*, found nowhere else in Singapore's waters. Knobbly sea stars — large, endangered, and in good numbers here — sit out the tide on the flats. Giant clams, including the endangered fluted giant clam, live on the reef; so do seahorses, turtles and reef sharks, with dolphins and otters in the waters around. A survey in 2005 turned up four plants endangered in Singapore, one of them then recorded nowhere else in the country. The birds include the great-billed heron — one long-term resident was nicknamed Jimmy — Malaysian plovers, brahminy kites and mangrove whistlers. Since 2007 a team of volunteers, TeamSeagrass, has monitored the meadow, and that same year a coral nursery was set up in the sea beside the island — the region's first to raise "corals of opportunity", fragments already broken off by accident, to be grown on and transplanted to tired reefs elsewhere.

![A fluted giant clam with a vivid patterned mantle, half-buried in reef rubble](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/c/cf/Tridacna_squamosa_%28Giant_clam%29_brown_and_blue.jpg)

*The fluted giant clam, endangered in Singapore and still found on Semakau's reef — one was among the creatures rescued from the landfill's own lagoon in 2014. (Nick Hobgood, CC BY-SA 3.0, via Wikimedia Commons; photographed in East Timor)*

The strangest chapter of the living island came in 2014. The landfill's second phase required closing the gap in the bund and filling the southern lagoon — a lagoon that, having been left open to the sea for sixteen years, had quietly become a reef. Before it could be filled, it had to be emptied of its wildlife. A survey team inventoried it; divers then harvested more than seven hundred coral colonies and transplanted them, over the winter of 2014–15, to the Sisters' Islands Marine Park. Among them were rarities: a fluted giant clam, and a Neptune's Cup sponge — a species recorded in Singapore in 1908, long thought extinct, rediscovered at St John's Island in 2011, and found again, improbably, growing in a landfill lagoon. Nature groups then caught and released more than 450 fish from the lagoon before the filling began. They had to rescue a reef from the rubbish island's own lagoon before they could bury it.

For a time, Singaporeans could see all this themselves. On 16 July 2005 the agency opened the landfill to the public for guided intertidal walks, birdwatching, catch-and-release fishing and stargazing — and among the guests of honour at the opening were forty families of former Pulau Seking residents, invited back as guests to the island that had been their home. The Teo brothers, the provision-shop keepers, had made their own return the year before, in 2004, to see what their island had become. Visits ran for years, thousands of people came, and the walks were a fixture of the nature calendar. They are, for now, suspended — the agency's page currently offers visits to its waste-to-energy plants instead — so the island has gone back to being what it was for most Singaporeans: a name, a rumour, a place the rubbish goes.

## The clock

When Semakau was designed, it was meant to last until 2045 — nearly half a century of the nation's ash. That date has been walking backwards ever since: coverage in the mid-2000s said 2040; when the second phase was completed in July 2015 — closing the lagoon into a single giant cell and adding 16.7 million cubic metres of room — the agency's own line was "2035 and beyond". At current filling rates, the expectation now is that Semakau will be full by about 2035. Singapore's Zero Waste Masterplan sets the task of cutting what is sent to Semakau by thirty per cent by 2030, but the arithmetic underneath is blunt: a city that burns its rubbish still makes ash, the ash has to go somewhere, and there is exactly one somewhere. Which is why the island's newest chapter is not about burying the ash better. It is about not burying it at all.

## Building with ash

The idea is older than the problem's urgency. As far back as 2001, the environment ministry was studying whether incineration bottom ash — the heavy residue left in the grate after burning, roughly a fifth of what goes in — could be treated and used as a building material. (The other residue, fly ash, is hazardous; it is stabilised and sealed away, and is not part of this story.) In 2009 a road trial using a stabilisation technique got treated bottom ash written into the Land Transport Authority's roadworks specifications as a possible material. The agency gave the material a brand deliberately echoing NEWater: NEWSand — treated ash, washed and chemically stabilised so that contaminants cannot leach out, made to environmental standards the agency wrote for it, for use in place of sand and fine aggregate in road bases and non-structural concrete. A parallel track gasifies waste at up to 1,600 degrees into a glass-like slag, which is made into NEWSand of another kind.

Some of it is already under Singaporeans' feet. Slag from the national waste-to-energy research facility at Tuas was cast into a concrete plaza in front of the Environment Building on Scotts Road, and into a 105-metre footpath linking Our Tampines Hub to the Darul Ghufran Mosque. The serious test came at Tanah Merah Coast Road: from September 2020, about 3,000 tonnes of treated bottom ash, processed by three companies, went into the road base and sub-base of a real road, with environmental monitoring before, during and after construction. The road sections opened to traffic in April 2022, and the ash has performed comparably to conventional material. But the honest status is this: proven in a real road, not yet a routine material. The standards review the trial feeds is still the gate, and research now running — including a university project examining the ash's behaviour at fifteen possible reuse sites, and whether it might one day serve in structural concrete or coastal protection — is still working to finalise the rules.

Behind the trials stands a funding machine built for exactly this problem. The Closing the Waste Loop initiative of 2017 put S$45 million into research on diverting waste — ash included — away from the landfill; its successor, Closing the Resource Loop, added S$80 million in 2022 with an emphasis on getting laboratory results built in the real world. Under the first programme, Republic Polytechnic researchers developed a way to turn bottom ash into "green aggregates", a technology since licensed to industry. And under the same programme, a Nanyang Technological University team spent three years, from 2021 to 2024, on a project with the splendid name "Breaking the Ground": a study of what, exactly, is inside Semakau's filled cells — some fifteen million tonnes of mixed material in the first phase alone — and whether it can be dug back up and used. The study is complete. No excavation has been announced. But the question has been asked, with money and engineers behind it, and it changes what the landfill is: not a final resting place, necessarily, but a stockpile awaiting a use.

The island is already a laboratory in another way. Since 2014 it has hosted REIDS, a renewable-energy testbed run by the university — a small, working model of the microgrids that Southeast Asia's off-grid islands need, mixing solar panels, Singapore's largest wind turbine, batteries, diesel and a hydrogen system, and supplying the landfill's own facilities with a few hundred kilowatt-hours a day. A rubbish island that generates its own power, monitors its own mangroves, and is studied as a mine.

In June 2026 the newest institution arrived: TREASURES, a S$35 million national research centre for residue and toxic waste, set up by the environment agency and the university, with a whole division devoted to the landfill's transformation — recovering, treating and repurposing what has been sent to Semakau, and what is already stored there. Its co-director, Professor Chu Jian, put the ambition plainly: to turn Semakau from a disposal site into a "transit storage and processing hub", and perhaps extend its life by decades. Nothing has been built yet; the centre's first grants were called in the middle of 2026. But the direction of travel is set, and it runs opposite to the barges: for twenty-seven years the island has taken what Singapore discards. The plan, now, is for it to start giving some of it back.

## The people, again

The island's people, meanwhile, have been rebuilding their community where they can — which is not on the island. In 2020 Firdaus Sani, a fourth-generation Orang Laut descendant, began a page of family photographs and stories. His maternal grandparents had lived on Semakau until 1977, and the family, moved to Telok Blangah, had chosen the estate partly to stay near open water. The page became Orang Laut SG, a community organisation running research, education, coastal tours and communal feasts cooked from his grandmother's recipes — the food, he says, is a way of staying connected to where the family came from. In June 2025 the organisation held the first Hari Orang Pulau, Islanders' Day, at West Coast Park, reuniting the families of islands most Singaporeans have never heard of. The carnival the southern islands once took turns hosting, with its swimming races and greasy pole, has a mainland descendant after all.

**Where it fits in the bigger story:** Singapore has closed many worlds to build its present — the river trade, the attap villages, the mainland dumps — but Semakau is the rare case where the replacement is still visibly arguing with what it replaced. A landfill that had to rescue a reef from its own lagoon. A village island underneath the receiving station, whose shopkeepers came back as tourists. An island of ash that the country now hopes to mine. Minah and Rani, who gave away their flat and went home to a beach hut, were early with the island's essential fact, the one every plan since has had to reckon with: people were attached to this place. "Our hearts are attached to the island." The rubbish came later. The attachment, in one form or another, is still there — and so, now, is a research programme betting that even the rubbish is not finished yet.
