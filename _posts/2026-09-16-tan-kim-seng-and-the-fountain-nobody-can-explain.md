At Esplanade Park, an ornate Victorian iron fountain has stood for well over a century, its three tiers ringed with classical figures and its basin fed by four spouts shaped like Poseidon's face — or it would be fed, except several of those spouts had been sealed shut for years, quietly turning a monument to a water donation into a fountain that couldn't actually run water. Thousands of joggers and tourists pass it every week. Almost none stop to read the inscription that explains why it's there, or who Tan Kim Seng was.

Tan Kim Seng was born in 1805 in Dutch Malacca, a Straits-born Peranakan of Hokkien descent. He founded the trading house Kim Seng & Company in 1840 and built one of the largest fortunes in early Singapore — by the time he died he was estimated to hold roughly half the island's private land. His standing in both the Chinese and colonial worlds made him one of the few merchants the government actually listened to: he was appointed a Justice of the Peace in 1850, and in 1857 became the first Asian member of Singapore's Municipal Commission. Three years earlier, in May 1854, he'd used exactly that standing to help end the Hokkien-Teochew riots — ten days of fighting between rival secret societies, sparked by a dispute so small it's remembered as the "Five Catties of Rice Riots," that killed an estimated 500 people and destroyed some 300 homes before it burned out. He also founded Chong Wen Ge, one of Singapore's earliest Chinese schools, in 1849, and gave generously to Tan Tock Seng Hospital, a cause the philanthropist Tan Tock Seng had championed a decade before him.

None of that is what put his name on a fountain, though. On 18 November 1857, Tan offered the government $13,000 toward Singapore's first proper waterworks — piped water carried down from a reservoir near Bukit Timah, on condition that it stay free to the public once built.

The government took the money and then spent the next two decades bungling what it was meant to pay for. Contractors laid earthenware pipes at Kandang Kerbau that turned out to be useless for the job; sections of pipe sat unused at the harbour; at one point an engineer drew up a scheme that would have required water to run uphill. The waterworks weren't finished until 1877, opening the following year — thirteen years after Tan had died in Malacca in 1864, without ever seeing a drop of the water his gift was meant to supply.

The Municipal Commissioners built the fountain in 1882 partly to make amends for the fiasco. A Victorian, three-tiered, cast-iron structure by the English firm Andrew Handyside & Co., it was unveiled at Fullerton Square on 18 May 1882, its inscription crediting Tan's donation "towards the cost of the Singapore Water Works." It stayed there until Fullerton Square itself was redeveloped in the 1920s to make way for the Fullerton Building. A Straits Budget report from May 1929 describes the fountain's removal as still under way after "controversy lasting nearly eight years" over where it should go — it was eventually dismantled and rebuilt at its current site in Esplanade Park, where it has stood ever since. It was gazetted a national monument in 2010, and has been substantially restored twice: once in 1994, for $1.12 million, and again as recently as 2023-24, in what conservators called the most extensive intervention yet, with 257 separate components taken apart, cleaned and recoated.

Tan Kim Seng's own philanthropy didn't end with him, either. His son, Tan Beng Swee, carried on the family's giving in Malacca, funding a bridge and a burial ground that still bear the family name there. His grandson, Tan Jiak Kim, went further still: in October 1904 he launched the fundraising campaign that founded the King Edward VII College of Medicine, personally donating $12,000 toward what would eventually grow into the medical faculty of the National University of Singapore. Three generations of one family — a water donation the government couldn't get right, a bridge in Malacca, and, eventually, a medical school — and almost none of it is what the fountain most people photograph is actually remembered for.

**Why it matters today:** the fountain most people photograph on a walk through Esplanade Park doesn't really commemorate a gift of water — it commemorates a colonial government's twenty-year failure to deliver one. The lasting result of Tan Kim Seng's money wasn't the waterworks at all; it was three generations of a family that kept building the civic institutions the government wouldn't fund on its own, from a hospital and a school to, eventually, Singapore's first medical college. Actual water security took another century and a half to solve — that's the story Marina Barrage and Linggiu Reservoir finished telling.
