In late January 1947, ten days into a strike by Singapore's municipal labourers, the drains were choking in the monsoon rain and the men sent out to clear them were Japanese soldiers. They belonged to the army that had run the island until eighteen months earlier. The Straits Times ran a photograph of them at work under a caption that could not quite settle on what to call them: "Dismal Days For The PoWs," the word set in inverted commas. Officially they were not prisoners of war, and that distinction was the whole point.

The category was called Japanese Surrendered Personnel, and it was Japan's own idea. In the last weeks of the war, with defeat certain, Tokyo proposed that its troops in Allied hands be classed not as prisoners of war but as "surrendered personnel," a status with no basis in international law. The Allies, and Britain in particular, accepted it. A prisoner of war was covered by the 1929 Geneva Convention: to be repatriated with the least possible delay once fighting stopped, and not to be put to work that was dangerous, degrading, or connected to military operations. A surrendered person was covered by none of that. Britain came out of the war acutely short of manpower and shipping, with its Asian colonies to put back together, and several hundred thousand disarmed Japanese troops across South East Asia — held under a label that carried no repatriation clock and few limits on labour — were the way it proposed to do some of that work.

Around 35,000 Japanese troops were moved out of Singapore into holding camps in Johor soon after the British came back in September 1945, but tens of thousands stayed in the region as a labour pool, and Singapore drew on them heavily. The city they had occupied was in poor shape. Allied aircraft had bombed the docks and Keppel Harbour, and water, electricity and telephone services were unreliable. Japanese Surrendered Personnel were set to repairing the wharves and the Seletar naval base, rebuilding roads and railway bridges — among them the Connaught bridge on the Klang line, destroyed in the 1941–42 campaign — clearing rubble and refuse, and exhuming and reburying the dead from the occupation's mass graves. When repatriation was announced in early 1947, the Singapore Municipal President, Mr Rayman, objected in the press: the municipality had been short of labour since the reoccupation and could not easily replace them.

![Japanese prisoners of war being marched at the double to clear up Singapore, 1945](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/e/e5/British_Reoccupation_of_Singapore%2C_1945_IND4826.jpg)

*Japanese prisoners of war marched at the double through Singapore to clean-up work, 1945. (Photo: No. 9 Army Film & Photographic Unit / Imperial War Museums / Wikimedia Commons, public domain)*

They were organised as army labour battalions, under guard, and the conditions were not gentle. A photograph from the labour camp near Seletar shows Japanese medical orderlies in face masks preparing, in the caption's words, to deal with "the sick and dying." In April 1947 a man from the camp at RAF Tengah was remanded on a charge of killing another with an axe. That July the army narrowed the rules: from the first of August no Japanese on the island was to be used for "non-essential" work — the banned jobs included service as domestic grooms — and all of them were to be confined to basic reconstruction. The same month, an English missionary who spoke Japanese was reported to be several months into welfare work among the men in the camps.

Repatriation ran in batches through 1947. The first group of 132 left Singapore on the troopship Dilwara in March; from April the departures were on Japanese-manned ships sailing from the Seletar naval base, and by that autumn the men still in Malaya were being counted in the thousands. The last Japanese Surrendered Personnel held anywhere under South East Asia Command went home in October 1947. Historians of the period have noted that the Allies took some care not to draw attention to how much of the immediate postwar recovery had run on Japanese labour. [See more photographs related to this post →](/gallery/japanese-surrendered-personnel-singapore-cleanup-1945/)

**Where it fits in the bigger story:** a real portion of the work that got Singapore functioning again after 1945 — the cleared sites, the repaired quays, the drains unblocked in the rain during a strike — was done by the army that had spent three and a half years running the place into the ground. It was done under a name, settled on jointly by the defeated and the victors, that seems to have been chosen so that neither side would have to describe the arrangement as what it was.
