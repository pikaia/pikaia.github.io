When the Thian Hock Keng temple was built on Telok Ayer Street between 1839 and 1842, it stood on the beach. Sailors landing from China came up from the water to give thanks to Mazu, the goddess of the sea, for a safe crossing. By the end of the century it was four streets from the sea, and today the water is further away still. The land in front of it was made by the colonial government, which levelled hills, built seawalls and filled in the bay.

## The first fill, 1822

<div style="float: left; max-width: 280px; width: 42%; margin: 0.25em 1.5em 1em 0;">
<img src="https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/8/81/KITLV_-_103742_-_Lambert_%26_Co._-_Thian_Hock_Keng_Temple_in_Singapore_-_circa_1890.tif/lossy-page1-1280px-KITLV_-_103742_-_Lambert_%26_Co._-_Thian_Hock_Keng_Temple_in_Singapore_-_circa_1890.tif.jpg" alt="The Thian Hock Keng temple around 1890, a Chinese temple with a curved, ornamented roof behind a low wall, with a rickshaw in the street" style="width: 100%; display: block; border-radius: 4px;">
<em style="display: block; font-size: 0.8em; margin-top: 0.5em;">The Thian Hock Keng temple on Telok Ayer Street around 1890. When it was built, the street ran along the beach. (Photo: Lambert & Co., KITLV/Leiden University Library, public domain, via Wikimedia Commons)</em>
</div>

Singapore's reclamation began three years after the British arrived. The south bank of the Singapore River was low and swampy, and in 1822 Stamford Raffles had a small hill near today's Battery Road levelled and its earth used to fill the swamp. About 300 labourers did the work in roughly four months. The new ground became Boat Quay along the river and Commercial Square, later Raffles Place, the centre of the town's trade.

Along the coast to the south, Telok Ayer Street, whose Malay name means "water bay", followed the shoreline. The Nagore Dargah shrine, built between 1828 and 1830, and the Al-Abrar Mosque, which began as a thatched hut in 1827, faced the sea along with Thian Hock Keng.

## A seawall for the merchants, 1864

The next stretch was the waterfront east of Commercial Square, where the merchants' offices and warehouses backed onto the sea. From the late 1850s a seawall was built from Fort Fullerton south towards the Telok Ayer Market, and the ground behind it was filled to make a road along the water. The work was done by [Indian convict labour](/2026/09/28/the-indian-convicts-who-built-colonial-singapore-1825-1873/), and it went slowly. According to the National Library Board's history of reclamation, the foundations could only be laid at the lowest tides, which came about once a fortnight, so the wall took three years and the road another year. It was finished in 1864 and named Collyer Quay after George Chancellor Collyer, the Chief Engineer of the Straits Settlements.

The largest project of the century came next. Between 1879 and 1897 the Public Works Department reclaimed a 42-acre strip of the bay in front of Telok Ayer Street, using earth from Mount Wallich and part of Mount Palmer, two hills to the south that were blasted away for the purpose. The first stretch, as far as Cecil Street, was finished by 1886, and on the new land the department laid out Cecil Street, Robinson Road and Raffles Quay along the new seafront. The roads linked the commercial district with the [docks at Tanjong Pagar](/2026/09/25/the-private-company-that-ran-singapores-harbour-1864-1905/). In April 1881, the Singapore Daily Times wrote that beside the Telok Ayer works, "the Collyer Quay reclamation sinks into utter insignificance".

The old Telok Ayer Market, which had stood on the shore at the end of Market Street since the 1820s, was pulled down in 1879 as the reclamation began. Its replacement, designed by the municipal engineer James MacRitchie with cast-iron work made in Glasgow, was built on the new land between 1890 and 1894. That building survives today as Lau Pa Sat.

## Did it pay?

<div style="float: left; max-width: 280px; width: 42%; margin: 0.25em 1.5em 1em 0;">
<img src="/assets/images/singapore-free-press-1896-11-17-page-309.jpg" alt="Page 309 of the weekly Singapore Free Press, 17 November 1896, a page of dense columns of small type" style="width: 100%; display: block; border-radius: 4px;">
<em style="display: block; font-size: 0.8em; margin-top: 0.5em;">Page 309 of the weekly Singapore Free Press of 17 November 1896, which printed the cost of the Telok Ayer reclamation and the money raised by selling the land. (Singapore Free Press and Mercantile Advertiser, via NewspaperSG, National Library Board Singapore, public domain)</em>
</div>

In November 1896, at the request of a member of the Legislative Council, the government published what the Telok Ayer reclamation had cost and what it had earned. According to the statement, printed in the Singapore Free Press, $917,243 had been spent on the work between 1879 and 1896, and it had produced 1,808,028 square feet of land. About 88,000 square feet had gone to Finlayson Green and the new market. The government had sold 653,028 square feet at an average of $1.13 a square foot, bringing in $739,481, and more than a million square feet were still available for sale or held in reserve. The statement put the cost of reclamation at 51 cents a square foot, so the land was selling for more than twice what it had cost to make.

## The basin that sank, 1893–1932

<div style="float: left; max-width: 260px; width: 42%; margin: 0.25em 1.5em 1em 0;">
<img src="https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/2/21/Telok_Ayer_Singapore_1913.png" alt="A 1913 map of the Telok Ayer waterfront, showing the town's street grid, and offshore a long seawall with a gap in it" style="width: 100%; display: block; border-radius: 4px;">
<em style="display: block; font-size: 0.8em; margin-top: 0.5em;">The Telok Ayer waterfront on a 1913 map, with the unfinished seawall of the basin reclamation offshore. (Image: Fraser and Neave, 1913, public domain, via Wikimedia Commons)</em>
</div>

Even before the Telok Ayer works were finished, a second, larger scheme began further out. Started in 1893, it was meant to reclaim 88 acres behind a 5,000-foot seawall. In 1910 engineers found that the seawall was sinking, and work was suspended with 65 acres reclaimed and 4,120 feet of wall built, leaving a gap of 880 feet. The unfinished section was turned into a tidal basin for small vessels, the Telok Ayer Basin. Work resumed in 1930 and was completed in 1932, by which time the cost had risen from 2.5 million to 15 million Straits dollars. A road built along the new land, Raffles Way, was renamed Shenton Way in 1951.

According to the National Library Board's account, all the reclamation of the colonial period added about 3 square kilometres to Singapore. Between 1965 and 2015, the country reclaimed about 138 square kilometres more, at Changi, along the East Coast, on Jurong Island, at Tuas and around the river mouth. The Telok Ayer Basin itself was filled in between 1974 and 1977, and the land beyond the old waterfront became Marina Bay. According to the Singapore Land Authority, the country's land area grew from 581.5 square kilometres in 1960 to 744.3 square kilometres at the end of 2025.

## What comes next

Two confirmed projects will move the shoreline again. At Tuas, the Maritime and Port Authority is building a new port in four phases on reclaimed land. The first phase of reclamation was completed in 2021, and when all four phases are finished in the 2040s the port will cover about 1,337 hectares and handle up to 65 million twenty-foot container units a year.

Off the East Coast, the Urban Redevelopment Authority is planning "Long Island", which could reclaim more than 800 hectares, about twice the size of Marina Bay, to protect the low-lying coast from rising sea levels, create a new reservoir and provide land for future development. As of its update in March 2026, the plans were still being worked out through technical studies, and reclamation will begin only after those studies are complete.

**Where it fits in the bigger story:** Much of downtown Singapore stands on ground that was once the bay. The shoreline has been moving since 1822, first with hills carried to the sea and seawalls built at low tide, and the old waterfront streets like Telok Ayer Street still trace where the beach used to be.
