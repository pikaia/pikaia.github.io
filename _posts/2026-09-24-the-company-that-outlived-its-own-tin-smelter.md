By 1912, a company most Singaporeans today have never heard of was smelting roughly a third of the world's tin, on a small island just off the harbour. Its ingots, stamped "Straits Tin," were internationally recognised as the purest grade money could buy. The smelter that made that possible is gone, the island it stood on is a naval base, and the company itself doesn't smelt anything in Singapore anymore. It is, remarkably, still around, still listed, still making money, more than a century after the furnaces on Pulau Brani first lit.

## Two foreigners and a smelter

The company was called the Straits Trading Company, and it was incorporated in Singapore on 8 November 1887 with $150,000 in capital, the work of an unlikely pairing: Herman Muhlinghaus, a German entrepreneur, and James Sword, a Scottish businessman from the merchant house Gilfillan, Wood &amp; Co. They started modestly, with five small coastal vessels running trade routes rather than any grand industrial ambition. The pivot came in 1890, when they opened their first smelting plant on Pulau Brani, a small island just off Singapore's harbour, built on the site of an old, unused dry dock.

The idea was straightforward but not obvious at the time: instead of leaving tin ore to be smelted in small batches by traditional Chinese-run furnaces scattered across the Malay Peninsula, centralise it into one large, coal-fired, efficient works. It worked. Pulau Brani's furnaces could process ore at a scale and consistency the old scattered smelters simply couldn't match.

## The purest tin in the world

A second plant followed in 1902, at Butterworth in Province Wellesley, on the mainland opposite Penang. Between the two works, the company's tin, branded "Straits Tin" and shipped as 100-pound ingots stamped with the company's own name, became internationally recognised by the 1900s as the purest grade of tin in the world. By 1912, Malaya was producing roughly half the world's supply of tin, and two-thirds of that output was refined by the Straits Trading Company alone. A single firm, working through two coal-fired plants a strait apart, had become one of the most important players in a global industrial supply chain.

## Scorched earth, then rebuilt

The Pulau Brani smelter's biggest disruption didn't come from a market crash. It came from the British themselves. In 1941, with the Japanese invasion of Malaya already underway, the smelting plant on Pulau Brani was deliberately destroyed, denying it to the advancing enemy just before Singapore fell in February 1942. It was scorched-earth policy applied to a tin smelter.

Staff began returning to the ruined works in December 1945, and rebuilding started under the company's long-serving chairman, Sir John Bagnall, who had led the Straits Trading Company since 1923 and would stay on until 1947. Smelting resumed at Pulau Brani within roughly a year of the staff's return, even as the postwar tin market itself faced a slower, structural threat: cheap synthetic alternatives, aluminium chief among them, had begun eating into demand for tin worldwide.

## Leaving the island

Pulau Brani's smelter outlasted the war by only two more decades. In the late 1960s, the plant moved off the island entirely, making way for what became Singapore's first naval base, officially opened on Pulau Brani in 1974. The furnaces that had once made Singapore synonymous with the world's purest tin were gone from the island for good, after more than seventy years there.

The Butterworth plant on the mainland kept going longer. In 1982, it was folded into the Malaysia Smelting Corporation Berhad, a separate company that took over the group's actual smelting operations going forward. Tin smelting, the business the Straits Trading Company had been built around in 1887, was no longer something the company did directly in Singapore at all.

## A conglomerate now

What replaced tin, gradually, was everything else. Through the late twentieth century the company diversified into property management and financial investment, and in 1997 it launched a hospitality arm under the Rendezvous Hotels name, still running today. In 2008, the Tecity Group acquired a majority stake, and the group that emerged looks nothing like the smelting operation Muhlinghaus and Sword founded. Today the Straits Trading Company describes itself as a conglomerate across three divisions: Resources, meaning a 73 percent stake in the Malaysia Smelting Corporation, now listed in its own right on Bursa Malaysia; Property, spanning commercial and retail real estate across Asia and Europe; and Hospitality, the Rendezvous Hotels chain. The company remains listed on the Singapore Exchange, reporting a net income of S$218.4 million in 2025, under Executive Chairman Chew Gek Khim.

**Where it fits in the bigger story:** Singapore's founding fortunes are usually told through trade and entrepot commerce, not heavy industry, but for a few decades the Straits Trading Company ran one of the most important industrial monopolies of the colonial world from a small island most Singaporeans today couldn't place on a map. It survived a self-inflicted wartime demolition, lost the island itself to the navy, spun its own core business off into a separate company, and turned into something almost unrecognisable, a property-and-hotels conglomerate that happens to still hold a stake in tin. Few nineteenth-century Singapore firms can say they're still trading in 2026 at all, let alone after changing this completely.
