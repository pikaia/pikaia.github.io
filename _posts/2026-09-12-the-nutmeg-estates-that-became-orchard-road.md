Orchard Road today is glass towers, luxury malls and a mile of shop windows, one of the most expensive stretches of retail real estate in Asia. Its name is a leftover clue to something else entirely: in the 1830s and 40s this same ground was thick with actual orchards, planted by colonial speculators chasing a spice fortune that lasted barely a generation. Nutmeg was the biggest bet of all, and while the plantations themselves have vanished without a trace, the men who planted them are still on the street signs — Oxley, Cairnhill, Cuppage, Claymore, Scotts, Killiney. Singapore's most expensive shopping street is, underneath its signage, a map of a spice that failed.

## A gamble on the world's most valuable spice

For centuries, nutmeg had been one of the most valuable commodities on earth, grown almost nowhere else on the planet outside a handful of small islands in the Dutch East Indies' Banda Sea, where the Dutch had spent two hundred years enforcing a brutal monopoly on it. Breaking that monopoly was one of Stamford Raffles's ambitions from the moment he founded Singapore: within months of his second visit in 1819, he had nutmeg seeds and trees shipped in from the East India Company's outpost at Bencoolen, on Sumatra, and planted as an experiment on the slopes of what was then still called Government Hill — Fort Canning.

The experiment itself was modest, but it set a pattern that Singapore's merchants and colonial officials followed with real enthusiasm through the late 1830s and 1840s, when existing nutmeg plantations were returning very good profits. It became, in the words of one contemporary account, an "unthinking mania": planters carved country estates out of the low hills just inland from the town — around what is now Tanglin, Cairnhill, Claymore and Emerald Hill — and the hills were fashionably renamed after whoever owned the biggest bungalow on top of them.

For a couple of decades it looked like a very good bet. Land was cheap, prices were high, and a nutmeg estate promised the kind of return that had already made fortunes for the Dutch further east. Merchants, surgeons and harbour officials alike put their savings into trees.

## The men who named the hills

Singapore's street map still carries the names of the men who planted it. William Cuppage, a postal clerk who rose to become acting Postmaster General in the 1840s, leased land on what became Emerald Hill from 1837 and secured the freehold for his nutmeg plantation in 1845; his name survives in Cuppage Road and Cuppage Terrace. Dr Thomas Oxley, a colonial surgeon, bought some 173 acres bounded by what are now Orchard, Grange and River Valley Roads, planted it with nutmeg as Killiney Estate, and built a house on top of it in 1842 — commemorated today in both Oxley Road and Killiney Road, with Grange Road recalling a second house, Grange House, on the same estate.

A little further along, Charles Carnie's house, built around 1840 on what was then called Carnie Hill, gave the area its modern name once the spelling drifted to Cairnhill; by 1848 his 68-acre estate held 4,370 nutmeg trees. Captain William Scott, Singapore's Harbour Master and Postmaster, ran an even bigger operation nearby, Claymore Estate — the third-largest nutmeg plantation in Singapore, with about 5,200 trees by the same year, remembered now in both Claymore Road and Scotts Road.

None of these men left behind a portrait that has survived, and little personal detail about most of them is recorded beyond the plantation ledgers and the street signs. What is left is simpler, and in its way more telling: an entire grid of roads in the middle of modern Singapore, still named for a crop that has not grown there in a century and a half.

## Canker

The nutmeg mania collapsed almost as fast as it had begun. Sometime in the 1840s a disease the planters called "nutmeg canker" appeared, causing the fruit to wither and drop before it ripened. Nobody at the time understood the cause: an 1865 writer blamed careless cultivation by inexperienced amateur planters, others suspected an insect, and it would be more than three decades before the botanist H. N. Ridley confirmed a beetle was to blame. Whatever the cause, the effect was total. The blight spread from estate to estate through the 1850s until, as one account from the time put it, there was "ruin staring the proprietors of the plantation in the face." By 1862, commercial nutmeg cultivation in Singapore had effectively ceased, and the big estates were broken up into smaller lots and sold off.

The scale of the wipeout is easy to underestimate at this distance. Even Singapore's own Botanic Gardens began on the wreckage of it: when the Agri-Horticultural Society acquired 23 hectares of land at Tanglin in November 1859 to found the gardens, what it bought was an abandoned nutmeg plantation. The Scottish planter Lawrence Niven was hired the following year to develop the site, and the bungalow built for him there, completed in 1868 and known today as Burkill Hall, is described on its own heritage marker as the last Anglo-Malay plantation house left in Singapore — built on ground that had been nutmeg orchard.

## From plantation to Peranakan terrace

With the nutmeg gone, the big estates were subdivided and passed from owner to owner, and through the second half of the century the area filled in as a quiet suburb of bungalows and private houses, still known by the planters' old names but no longer agricultural. Cuppage himself moved onto his Emerald Hill land in the early 1850s, built two houses there, and lived out his life on the estate until his death in 1872. It then passed to his daughters, and in 1890 to a son-in-law, the lawyer Edwin Koek, whose own attempt to run it as an orchard also failed; the property changed hands again in 1891, and by the turn of the century belonged to two Straits Chinese businessmen, Seah Boon Kang and Seah Boon Kiat.

In 1901 the Seah brothers subdivided the estate into 38 building lots, keeping ten and selling the rest; among the buyers was the prominent Peranakan physician and community leader Lim Boon Keng, who took three. The first house went up in 1902, roughly a third of the street was built by 1918, and by the 1930s a clear majority of Emerald Hill Road's houses — at least 59 of 112 — were owned by Peranakan, or Straits Chinese, families, drawn by a fashionable new street close to town. The result, largely designed by the architect R. T. Rajoo, is one of Singapore's best-preserved runs of Peranakan terrace housing: two- and three-storey homes that pair Georgian shuttered windows and columns with brightly coloured ceramic tiles and Chinese Baroque plasterwork, a style now simply called Peranakan architecture.

A short walk from Emerald Hill, on Oxley Road — Dr Oxley's old Killiney Estate — is where Singapore's founding prime minister, Lee Kuan Yew, made his home, and his wife, Kwa Geok Choo, came from a Peranakan family herself, very much in keeping with the neighbourhood's character. It is a small, pleasant coincidence of geography rather than anything more: the street where the country's first leader lived carries the name of the surgeon who tried, and failed, to grow nutmeg there a century before.

## From suburb to shopping belt

Well into the 20th century the stretch now known as Orchard Road stayed largely residential — bungalows, mission schools, a scattering of small shops, a quiet lane a short ride from the commercial docks and offices of the old town. Its turn into a shopping district came later, and gradually: department stores and cinemas began opening along the road from the 1950s and 60s, and the pace picked up sharply from the 1970s, as land was cleared and rebuilt for the shopping centres, hotels and office towers aimed at a growing, increasingly affluent population and a rising tide of tourists. By the 1980s, Orchard Road had become what it still is today — Singapore's premier shopping street — with almost nothing of its 19th-century suburban character left standing above ground.

Almost nothing, but not quite. Outside ION Orchard, at the very centre of the modern shopping belt, stands a two-tonne bronze sculpture by the artist Kumari Nahappan: an opened nutmeg seed nearly five metres long, its crimson mace unfurled, unveiled in 2009 as a deliberate nod to the plantations that once stood on that exact ground. It is easy to walk past without noticing what it is a picture of.

One actual nutmeg tree still stands in Singapore today, a few kilometres from Orchard Road: a specimen behind the Garage Building at the Singapore Botanic Gardens, protected under the National Parks Board's Heritage Tree scheme as a living link to Raffles's original 1819 experiment. It is the exception that proves the rule — one tree, carefully conserved as a curiosity, on ground where thousands once grew as a business.

**Why it matters today:** Orchard Road's route from failed cash crop, through a Peranakan suburb, to global shopping street is a reminder that the ground under Singapore's most familiar places has usually been something else first, and that a street name can be the only monument a vanished industry ever gets. Nutmeg itself never disappeared from the region — head up the Malay Peninsula to Penang today, and the trees still bear fruit around Balik Pulau, sold everywhere as dried nutmeg, pickles, juice, balm and oil, a living trade that Singapore's own plantations never got the chance to become.
