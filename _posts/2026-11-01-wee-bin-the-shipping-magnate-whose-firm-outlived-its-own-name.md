In March 1911, the Straits Times announced the end of one of Singapore's oldest Chinese businesses. "Steps are in progress," it reported, "to wind up the well-known and long-established business of Messrs. Wee Bin and Co., of 106 Market St." Three weeks later the firm's steamers were sold by public auction on the waterfront they had served for half a century.

The strange part of the story is the date on the founder's grave. Wee Bin had died in 1868, forty-three years before the auction. In between, his firm — at its height the largest Chinese shipping business in Singapore, with a fleet of more than twenty vessels — was carried on by his son, who died at thirty-six, by his grandson, who died at twenty-six, and above all by a son-in-law who had joined the firm as a boy helping its cook buy ingredients.

Wee Bin was a Hokkien, born in 1823 in Longxi county, in the Zhangzhou prefecture of Fujian. No record found for this post says when he arrived in Singapore. By 1856, aged thirty-three, he was established enough to found his own house: Wee Bin & Co., trading under the chop, or trade name, Hong Guan, in Market Street, a short walk from the river godowns. He ran it, unusually for its time and place, on Western business practices.

*The port of Singapore about 1890, in the decades when Wee Bin & Co.'s steamers were among the harbour's familiar sights. (G. R. Lambert & Co., KITLV, public domain, via Wikimedia Commons)*

The firm's first connections were with the Dutch East Indies. It built up trade with houses in Bali until it had become the greatest importer of products from that island. It traded in all kinds of earthenware, it manufactured sago, it acted as merchant bankers, and above all it bought ships. By the end of Wee Bin's life the firm had built up a fleet of over twenty vessels for the China and Dutch Indies trade, with branches in the Indies to serve them. In an 1866 survey, fifty-six of the a hundred and seventy-eight vessels registered in Singapore were Chinese-owned, and Wee Bin & Co. stood at the head of that world.

![A steamship in dry dock, its hull exposed, workers dwarfed beneath it](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/c/c1/KITLV_-_29164_-_The_steamship_Brisbane_in_dry_dock_in_Singapore_-_1860.tif/lossy-page1-1280px-KITLV_-_29164_-_The_steamship_Brisbane_in_dry_dock_in_Singapore_-_1860.tif.jpg)

*The steamship Brisbane in dry dock at Singapore in 1860 — steam shipping in Wee Bin's own decade, when his firm was building its fleet. (KITLV, public domain, via Wikimedia Commons)*

Something of the man's standing can be seen in the small print of the newspapers: in 1865, a decade after founding his firm, Wee Bin appears as sole executor to the estate of a fellow merchant — the kind of trust merchants placed only in one of their own number whose word was good. He married twice. One of his wives was a daughter of Kiong Kong Tuan, the Penang merchant who held the colony's opium and spirit farms; Wee Bin's own road to wealth was ships, not farms, though he briefly went into partnership with his brother-in-law Kiong Seok Wee in a ship-chandlery and a general trading firm alongside his own house.

He died in 1868, aged forty-five, leaving a son, Wee Boon Teck, still in his teens, and a daughter. The surprise of the story is that the firm did not die with him. It prospered for another forty-three years.

![Studio portrait of a young Chinese merchant in formal dress](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/e/ee/Wee_Bin.jpg)

*Wee Bin (1823–1868), founder of Wee Bin & Co. (Photo: Song Ong Siang, One Hundred Years' History of the Chinese in Singapore, 1923, CC BY-SA 4.0, via Wikimedia Commons)*

Boon Teck grew into the business and, in Song Ong Siang's words, "materially improved and strengthened the position of the firm". He sat on the committees of the Tan Tock Seng Hospital and the Po Leung Kuk, the society that protected women and girls, and he was remembered for his charity: a gift of four thousand dollars to the Tan Tock Seng Hospital was held by the government for some twenty years and then spent on a ward in the hospital's Moulmein Road building that bore his name. He died on the 22nd of September 1888, aged thirty-six — the second towkay of Hong Guan to die young.

His son, Wee Siang Tat, became a partner in January 1900, and the succession thinned further. Siang Tat had no head for business; he was a music lover, one of a dozen young Straits Chinese who met regularly to practise the violin at Siam House, the North Bridge Road home of the merchant Tan Kim Ching. He died fourteen months after becoming a partner, on the 14th of March 1901, aged twenty-six.

By then the firm's survival rested on the son-in-law. Lim Ho Puah had been born in Amoy in 1841 and came to Singapore young, entering Wee Bin & Co. in the humblest capacity in the house: as an apprentice who helped the firm's cook buy ingredients. His head for accounts carried him up to bookkeeper, and his industry so impressed the towkay that Wee Bin gave him his only daughter in marriage. After Boon Teck's death in 1888, Lim, as executor, took over the management of the shipping fleet. The kitchen boy had become the firm's senior partner.

![Studio portrait of an older Chinese merchant with a queue, in formal dress](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/2/21/Lim_Ho_Puah.png)

*Lim Ho Puah (1841–1914), who joined Wee Bin & Co. as a kitchen apprentice and ended as its sole surviving partner. (Photo: Song Ong Siang, 1923, CC BY-SA 4.0, via Wikimedia Commons)*

Under Lim the firm reached beyond the Indies trade into the China passenger business. It entered that trade in 1891. In 1894 Lim bought a new steamer and renamed her Hong Leong. In 1896 he bought the Glen Line steamer Glenfalloch, then the largest ship in the trade between the Straits and China. In 1901 he took delivery of the Hong Moh, a two-funnelled steamer of nearly four thousand tons. With their sister ships they ran a weekly service — Rangoon, Penang, Singapore, Hong Kong, Swatow, Amoy — and a missionary who knew the harbour described the firm's house flag, a blue flag with a red cross, "flying from the mastheads of quite a fleet of coasting steamers". Lim's standing outside the firm matched it: a director of the Tanjong Pagar Dock Company, a member of the Chinese Advisory Board, a committee man of the Po Leung Kuk, and a Justice of the Peace.

![Godowns and craft along a crowded quay on the Singapore River](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/f/fe/Binnenhaven_van_Singapore_The_boat_quay_Singapore_%28titel_op_object%29%2C_RP-F-00-5018-39.jpg)

*Boat Quay on the Singapore River — the working waterfront a short walk from the firm's Market Street offices. (Rijksmuseum, CC0, via Wikimedia Commons)*

When Wee Siang Tat died in 1901, Lim Ho Puah became the sole surviving partner of Wee Bin & Co. The firm traded on for another decade, but its ownership was quietly stuck. Siang Tat's widow, Ho Shiok Chui Neo, was an heir to the estate; while she remained a widow, the old partnership could rest undisturbed. In March 1909 she remarried, and the estate had to be divided. In March 1910 the family's property — ninety-seven lots of it — went under the hammer and realised almost four hundred thousand dollars. What remained was to wind up the firm itself.

It is worth being clear about what the 1911 liquidation was not. It was not a bankruptcy, and it was not a failure. It was a seventy-year-old man settling a family estate that the law of partnership would no longer let him carry. The Straits Times said as much in its own way, calling the concern "well-known and long-established" as it announced the end.

On the 28th of March 1911, Messrs. Powell & Co. sold the steamers by public auction. The Pinang Gazette printed the prices: the Glenfalloch at sixty thousand dollars, the Hong Wan I at eighty-five thousand. Two of the smaller ships went to the agent of the Java sugar king Oei Tiong Ham; one or two others went to third parties. And the large steamers at the heart of the fleet were bought by the man who had the strongest claim to them — Lim Peng Siang, Lim Ho Puah's son and Wee Bin's own grandson.

![Studio portrait of a Chinese businessman in Western dress](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/1/1c/Lim_Peng_Siang.png)

*Lim Peng Siang (1872–1944), Wee Bin's grandson, who bought the firm's steamships at the 1911 auction. (Photo: Song Ong Siang, 1923, CC BY-SA 4.0, via Wikimedia Commons)*

Readers of our post on Lim Peng Siang will know the rest of his story. In 1904, on a loan of fifty thousand dollars from his father, he and his brother Lim Peng Mau had founded their own firm, Ho Hong Co., and gone into rice milling and coconut oil. The name they chose deserves a moment's attention. Ho Hong joins "Ho", from Lim Ho Puah, to "Hong", from Hong Guan, Wee Bin's chop — the successor firm carried the old firm's name inside its own. The steamers bought in 1911 became Ho Hong's shipping department, and on the 27th of May 1913 they were formalised as the Ho Hong Steamship Co. Limited, with five million dollars in nominal capital. The Glenfalloch, the Hong Bee and the Hong Wan I were transferred into its ownership within days, the Hong Moh followed in January 1914, and by 1926 the fleet ran to fourteen vessels, some of them named, fittingly, after Singapore places.

![A pier crowded with passengers and cargo, steamers alongside](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/5/5c/KITLV_-_50211_-_Lambert_%26_Co.%2C_G.R._-_Singapore_-_Johnston%27s_Pier_Singapore_-_circa_1900.jpg)

*Johnston's Pier about 1900, Singapore's landing place before Clifford Pier — the waterfront from which, in March 1914, Lim Ho Puah's coffin was embarked for China. (G. R. Lambert & Co., KITLV, public domain, via Wikimedia Commons)*

Within a year of that last transfer, the man who had held the old firm together for four decades was dead. Lim Ho Puah died in February 1914. His funeral procession, on Sunday the 15th of March 1914, was reported as the longest ever seen in Singapore, as his coffin was carried to the water to be embarked for burial in China. It did not pass without incident: the silken cover draped over the coffin was stolen along the way, and a man was later convicted in court of dishonestly keeping it. The Singapore Free Press titled its report "The Coffin Cover — an incident of Singapore's longest funeral", and that small theft is, in its way, the last newspaper trace of the house of Hong Guan.

Nothing on Market Street today marks the firm. The name Wee Bin survives in one portrait, in Song Ong Siang's pages, and in the shipping intelligence of old newspapers. But the trade itself never stopped: it passed, ships, routes and all, into a grandson's company whose name still held a syllable of the old chop — and through Ho Hong's bank, in time, into the ancestry of OCBC. The firm outlived its founder by forty-three years. Then, in everything but name, it outlived itself.

**Where it fits in the bigger story:** Wee Bin & Co. was one of the first great Chinese-owned shipping houses of Singapore, built in the founder's twelve working years and then sustained for four decades by family succession of a kind that rarely survives one generation, let alone three. Its end was not collapse but settlement — and its absorption into Ho Hong connects the age of the Market Street godowns to the industrial groups, and the bank merger, that shaped the Singapore of the twentieth century. It is also the family prequel to our post on the founder's grandson, [Lim Peng Siang, the man Singapore called its greatest magnate](/2026/09/22/lim-peng-siang-the-man-singapore-called-its-greatest-magnate/).
