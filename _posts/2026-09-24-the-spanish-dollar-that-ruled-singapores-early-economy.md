When the East India Company finished buying Singapore in 1824, the bill was written in a coin that belonged to neither Britain nor Singapore. Sultan Hussein received 33,200 Spanish dollars and a monthly allowance of 1,300 for life, and the Temenggong received 26,800 and 700 a month. The money that priced the island's cession had been struck in Mexico and Peru in the name of the King of Spain, and it stayed Singapore's everyday money for another eighty years.

## A coin from another empire

The Spanish dollar, also called the piece of eight or the eight-real coin, had been struck since 1497 to a fixed standard of about 25.6 grams of fine silver. Its strength was uniformity: it was produced in huge volume and to a remarkably consistent standard, so a dollar minted in the Americas could be trusted far from where it was struck. The Manila galleons carried it across the Pacific in bulk to the Philippines and China, and it is often called the first global currency, accepted across Europe, Asia and the Americas alike. Chinese merchants stamped small chopmarks into the coins they handled to vouch for their authenticity.

By the time the British arrived in 1819, Singapore's traders were already using it. A mix of currencies was in circulation at first, including Indian rupees and Dutch guilders, but the Spanish silver dollar quickly took over, and the Resident, John Crawfurd, is credited with formalising it as the settlement's official currency in 1823. The Monetary Authority of Singapore's own history describes the silver dollar as the main currency of trade, with copper coins used mainly at the bazaar for small change.

## Rupee versus dollar

The Company's own preference was different. After Singapore became part of the Straits Settlements in 1826, the administration pushed for the Indian rupee, which was the currency of the Company's accounts. The market did not follow. The Spanish dollar, by then increasingly called the Mexican dollar, stayed popular with merchants, so the colony ran on a split arrangement: official books were kept in rupees, while daily transactions were in dollars. The first banknote to carry the word "Singapore" was not government paper at all but a note issued in 1840 by the Singapore branch of the Union Bank of Calcutta.

The arrangement lasted until London took over the Straits Settlements in 1867. The rupee then lost its legal-tender status, and Mexican, Hong Kong, Spanish, Peruvian and Bolivian silver dollars were all recognised at once. A colony's money was, in effect, whatever silver coins its merchants trusted.

## When silver stopped being dependable

<em style="display: block; font-size: 0.8em; margin-top: 0.5em;">A Straits Settlements one dollar coin of 1904, the year the foreign dollars were demonetised, made of silver but no longer Spanish or Mexican. (Photo: 5snake5, CC0, via Wikimedia Commons)</em>
</div>

That worked as long as the supply of good silver dollars held up. As it dwindled in the 1890s, the government began issuing its own British trade dollars in 1895, created a Board of Commissioners of Currency in 1897, and issued its first banknotes soon afterwards. In 1903 the board issued the Straits dollar, valued at par with the trade dollar, and on 31 August 1904 the British, Mexican and Hong Kong dollars ceased to be legal tender in the colony.

A coin whose value was its silver could not be steadier than the silver price, and the colony's exchange rate with sterling moved with it. In 1906 the Straits dollar was therefore tied to gold instead, fixed at two shillings and four pence, with the British gold sovereign made legal tender for the first time. The peg answered silver's instability, but silver itself kept moving: as its price rose, the coin risked becoming worth more melted than spent, and a new, smaller dollar with less silver in it was issued in 1907. The Straits dollar moved onto a sterling exchange standard when Britain left the gold standard in 1931, and in 1939 it was replaced at par by the Malayan dollar.

## The baton of world money

Singapore's story is a small window onto a larger one: the role of the world's money has passed from one currency to another, and the island happened to sit under several of them. The Spanish dollar was the first true global reserve currency, recognised from the sixteenth to the nineteenth centuries across Europe, Asia and the Americas. As commerce shifted north in the seventeenth century, the Dutch guilder became the main currency between Europe and the Dutch colonial empire, through the eighteenth century. In the nineteenth century the pound sterling took over, and by the late 1800s more than sixty percent of world trade was invoiced in pounds. At the Bretton Woods conference in 1944 the US dollar was only the second currency in global reserves, but it soon became the dominant one. By the 1950s 55 percent of global reserves were still held in sterling, though the share was about 10 percent lower within twenty years. The US dollar even inherited its shape from the coin in this story: when the United States set its own dollar in 1792, it based it on the Spanish dollar's weight, and the Spanish coin stayed legal tender there until 1857.

**Where it fits in the bigger story:** Singapore has never issued the world's reserve currency, but for two centuries its economy has run on whichever one was current, and its own money was repeatedly redefined to match. The Spanish dollar that paid for the island in 1824 was, at that moment, the closest thing the world had to a common currency; by the time it disappeared from Singapore's pockets in 1904, that role belonged to the pound, and within another forty years to the American dollar.
