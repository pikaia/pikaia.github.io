I was born in 1960 on Jalan Payoh Lai — as best I can recall, anyway, memory that old gets slippery — in what was then the Kangkar area of Hougang, and for years afterward I walked to school and to church. The walk from the Upper Serangoon Road junction took about twenty minutes, and my favourite shortcut ran along a backlane to the left of Montfort School, called Holy Innocents' Lane. I only learned recently, digging into this, that the name traces back to the cluster of Catholic schools that once shared that stretch of Upper Serangoon Road — Montfort among them. Today, neither the lane nor the cemetery that once stood beside it appears on any map at all.

Sundays meant the same walk in the same direction, except to Mass instead of school, cutting through whatever backlane shaved the most time off the trip. The church at the end of it, the Nativity of the Blessed Virgin Mary, was older than anything else I knew as a child, though I had no idea how much older. A French missionary priest, Fr. Ambroise Maistre, bought the land in 1857 for the Teochew Catholic farmers and fishermen of Aukang — converts, many of them, from Shantou in China. The attap chapel that first stood there gave way to the Neo-Gothic building still standing today, blessed in December 1901 and expanded again in 1933. It's been a national monument since 2005, and it still holds a Teochew-language Mass, a tradition instituted in 1971 that is, as far as anyone can confirm, the last regular Teochew Catholic Mass left in Singapore.

Montfort School, next door, turned out to be part of that same tangle. It was founded in 1916 — not as Montfort at all, but as Holy Innocents' English School, established beside the church by the parish priest of the time, and the Brothers of St Gabriel took it over in 1936. But it wasn't the only "Holy Innocents" on that stretch of Upper Serangoon Road: Holy Innocents' Boys School and Holy Innocents' High School (today an entirely separate school, tracing back further to 1892) grew out of the same church grounds, alongside what's now CHIJ Our Lady of the Nativity. The names collided so often that in 1958 Holy Innocents' English School was renamed Montfort, after St Louis Marie Grignion de Montfort, the order's founder, specifically to end the confusion. Which of those schools Holy Innocents' Lane was actually named for, I can't say for certain — only that Montfort, my own school, was one of at least three that could have claimed it. Montfort moved to Hougang Avenue 8 in 1992, and at some point after that, the lane, and the cemetery next to it, were cleared away entirely.

"Hougang" is the Mandarin reading of the Hokkien and Teochew "Au Kang" — the end of the river — and Kangkar itself meant "foot of the stream," the settlement clustered wherever Teochew planters ran their kangchu system of jungle-clearing plantations along Sungei Serangoon. There were roughly twenty such kangkars across the island by an 1849 newspaper count, and by 1986 more than nine in ten Kangkar villagers were still Teochew, the dialect still the area's everyday language. The Kangkar I half-remember was cleared in 1984, its families resettled into three- and four-room HDB flats, its land folded into what's now Punggol Park.

<div style="clear: both;"></div>

Punggol End, further along, was a different kind of village entirely — Kampong Wak Sumang, a Malay fishing settlement at Tanjong Punggol founded, by one descendant's account, before 1819. It got piped water, electricity and proper roads only in the 1960s, and became briefly well known afterward for its seafood restaurants and boatels, before the government announced reclamation in 1983 and told residents to vacate by 1986. The fishing port and wholesale market that had outlasted the kampong itself finally closed in 1997, to make way for Punggol New Town.

![HDB flats in Hougang, photographed in 2007](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/1/14/Hougang_HDB.JPG)

*HDB flats in Hougang, photographed in 2007 — the kind of housing that replaced the Kangkar villages after 1984. (Photo: Terence Ong / Wikimedia Commons, CC BY-SA 3.0)*

Hougang New Town itself was announced in 1979 — 460 hectares, planned for 120,000 residents — with its first neighbourhoods finished by 1983 and the rest by 1992, the same year Montfort left its original site for good. [See five more historical photos related to this post →](/gallery/jalan-payoh-lai-kangkar-montfort-nativity-church/) One thing about the area didn't get redeveloped along with everything else: Hougang has been held by the same opposition party, the Workers' Party, in every general election since 1991 — a small, quietly remarkable fact in its own right, less a story about any one politician than a marker of how far Singapore's electoral landscape has matured since the kampong days.

I can't reconstruct the Kangkar of my childhood in any real detail anymore — the specific smells, the sounds, the neighbours' faces are mostly gone, the way the lane and the cemetery are gone. What's left is the walk itself: twenty minutes each way, a shortcut past a school that used to have a different name, to a church that's outlasted every version of the neighbourhood around it.

**Why it matters today:** Most of what made up the Hougang I grew up in — the lane, the cemetery, the kampong itself — exists now only in scattered memories like mine and in records like the ones that let me write this. Montfort School and Nativity Church are the rare fixed points that survived the clearing, which is probably the only reason I can still find my way back to any of it at all.
