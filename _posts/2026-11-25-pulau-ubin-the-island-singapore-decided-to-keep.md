Ten minutes by bumboat from Changi Point, Singapore becomes another country. There are no traffic lights on Pulau Ubin, no shopping malls, no housing blocks — just a village of bicycle shops, kampong houses among the coconut palms, flooded granite quarries turned into lakes, and a few dozen residents living much as their grandparents did. Every other island around Singapore has been remade — merged, built on, connected, closed. Ubin alone was left as it was, and in the end that was a decision, not an accident. This is the story of the island Singapore decided to keep.

## The stone island

Ubin's original Malay name says what the island is made of: Pulau Batu Jubin, Granite Stone Island. *Ubin* itself comes from *jubin*, the squared granite tiles cut from its seven hills; in Hokkien the island is Chioh-sua, Stone Hill. What looks like one island was once five, separated by tidal rivers and joined by bunds thrown up for prawn farming — 1,020 hectares of granite, rubber and mangrove, the second-largest of Singapore's offshore islands.

The stone was the point. From the nineteenth century, Ubin's quarries fed the building of Singapore itself. Granite blocks quarried and shaped on the island by Indian convict labour went into the Horsburgh Lighthouse in 1851; Ubin stone built the Istana in 1869 and went, with Bukit Timah granite, into the Singapore–Johore Causeway in 1923. The National Parks Board counts six quarries on the island — Balai, Kekek, Ketam, Pekan, Petai and Ubin — some dug 40 metres below sea level.

![British military map of Pulau Ubin, 1945](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/d/dc/P_Ubin_1945_map.jpg)

*Pulau Ubin in 1945: the quarries, the kampongs and the estates all marked, with Changi across the harbour. (British military survey map, Public domain, via Wikimedia Commons)*

The last and biggest was Ketam Quarry, worked by the Aik Hwa company from 1964. It employed more than a hundred men, most of them islanders, and in the 1990s it was still supplying 160 to 180 tons of granite a month — some 30 to 40 percent of Singapore's whole demand. When Ketam closed in May 1999, quarrying on Ubin ended for good. The pits filled with rain, and the holes the island's stone was taken from became still, dark lakes, now among its most beautiful sights.

## The kampong island

People came for the stone, and for the land. The island's first inhabitants were Orang Laut and Malays of Bugis and Javanese origin; a European planter, Thomas Heslop Hill, opened a coffee estate in 1879 — coffee was so important that the island had a Coffee Hill. In the 1880s came the founding migration, a move of Malay settlers from the Kallang River led, it is believed, by Encik Endut Senin (the name is also spelled Endun Senin). Chinese quarry workers followed — Hakka first, then Hokkien and Teochew — and the island filled with kampongs named for their founders and their features: Kampung Melayu, Kampung Jelutong, Kampung Noordin, and more.

At its height the island held real institutions. Bin Kiang School, established in 1952, was the island's first school, and a maternity and child health clinic followed in 1957. The Fo Shan Teng Tua Pek Kong Temple still keeps one of the last three Chinese opera stages in Singapore, and its festival still runs for a week. Islanders grew coffee, rubber, pineapple, coconut and durian, farmed prawns and fished.

Then the century turned the other way. Resettlement to the mainland and the closing of the quarries emptied the island, decade by decade. The figures are stark: 2,028 people at the 1970 census; about 1,000 by 1987; 139 in 2001; and by 2012, just 38 residents in 24 households. Bin Kiang School closed in 1985 and was demolished in 2000. Those who remain are known by name — Ahmad Kassim, whose family's wooden house in Kampung Melayu was built by his father during the war, and Ong Ang Kui, the "Ah Ma" of the famous Ah Ma Drink Stall, who sells drinks and fruit from the more than ninety durian trees her late husband planted.

## The German girl who never was

On a hill path inside what is now the Ketam Mountain Bike Park stands a small shrine, and behind it one of Singapore's most repeated ghost stories. The legend says that when the First World War broke out in 1914, British soldiers came to Ubin to intern the German family who ran a coffee plantation there. The family's daughter — eighteen years old, in most tellings — fled into the jungle, fell from a cliff or a quarry edge, and died. Workers found her body days later, covered in ants, and buried her on the hill; her spirit was kept in an urn with a crucifix and coins, and gamblers began coming to pray to her for lucky numbers.

It is a wonderful story. It is also, on the evidence, not true — and the unravelling is the better tale. In 2021 the historian William Gibson searched the archives for the National Library Board's BiblioAsia magazine. The coffee estate was real, but its German owners, the firm of D. Brandt and Company, had sold it in 1907, seven years before the war. In 1914 the estate's manager was an Anglo-Irishman, and no German family appears in the island's records. There was no internment raid on Ubin, and no newspaper of the time reports a missing girl or parents searching for her. The whole story traces back to a single informant, an islander born in 1928 — more than a decade after the girl was said to have died — who had it from another man who had not seen it either.

The shrine itself is genuine, and older than the legend. It began as a datuk keramat, a local spirit shrine, on a granite hill that has since been quarried away entirely — the spot is now the Ketam Quarry pond — and one of its traditions says the "grave" was a termite mound, which across the region often become shrines. When the quarrying came close, it was the Aik Hwa quarry company itself that moved the shrine and built it a yellow hut by the sea, out of respect. The shrine was rebuilt in brick in 2015, with a statue of the girl holding coffee berries and a sign reading "Berlin Heiligtum", dated 1896 — a year that is itself a mistake, since the First World War began in 1914. Worshippers still come, and the offerings now include Barbie dolls and nail polish. The island kept the story, and kept the shrine, long after the facts had quietly left both behind.

## A feint, and a school for toughening boys

Ubin's war, when it came, was brief and strange. On the evening of 7 February 1942 the Japanese occupied the island, and the next day their guns on Ubin bombarded Changi, drawing the fortress artillery's reply. It was a diversion. That night, while Singapore's defences looked east, the real invasion crossed the Johore Strait in the west.

Twenty-five years later the island found its most famous institution. In 1967 the People's Association established the Outward Bound School of Singapore on Pulau Ubin, set up by Dr Goh Keng Swee. The British Army had run a holiday adventure camp on the island since the late 1950s, and British officers ran the school's first courses; the first intake included a fifteen-year-old named Lee Hsien Loong, who remembered, fifty years on, an island of prawn ponds, granite quarries, a few kampongs and old rubber plantations, and training built on canoes, sailing dinghies, compasses and rope courses. The Defence Ministry ran the school from 1970 to 1991, toughening teenage boys before National Service; it then returned to the People's Association as Outward Bound Singapore, and its new campus — which it describes as the first purpose-built modern Outward Bound centre in the world — opened in 1997. Today, under a national outdoor education masterplan, every Secondary 3 student in Singapore goes through a five-day Outward Bound expedition programme, and Ubin remains its home.

I know this part of the island's story at first hand, because I saw it as a boy. A friend of mine — from home, from Holy Innocents Lane in Aukang, where our families' roots ran deep — had become an instructor at the school, and I visited him on the island. To a boy it seemed an adventurer's life: the camp, the boats, the jungle, the whole island as a workplace. I slept in the trainees' bunk one night and was woken by loud bangs going off around us; my friend explained, quite matter-of-factly, that they were thunderflashes, which the instructors set off around their trainees. He talked, too, about the school's tradition of the solo night — a trainee sent out alone overnight, a rite of passing. Thousands of Singaporean boys passed through that world. For most of them, Ubin was not a day trip. It was where they were sent to be toughened.

## The shore that was saved

At the island's eastern tip lies Chek Jawa, a hundred hectares where six habitats meet — sandy beach, rocky shore, seagrass lagoon, coral rubble, mangroves and coastal forest. It was slated for reclamation, and was saved at the very end of 2001, days before the work was due to begin, when a survey revealed how much life the shore held and the public took up its cause. That story has its own post on this blog — [Chek Jawa: The Shore That Was Saved by a Survey](/2026/10/22/chek-jawa-the-shore-that-was-saved-by-a-survey/) — and since 2007 a boardwalk, a viewing tower and a visitor centre in a 1930s Tudor-style house have let visitors see the shore without trampling it.

## The island that made its own power

For all its nearness to one of the most wired cities on earth, Pulau Ubin has never been connected to the national power grid. For generations its people drew water from wells and ran their lights and fans off diesel generators — noisy, and expensive. As recently as 2025, the Government told Parliament that the island has around sixty residential and business electricity consumers, and that connecting it to the mainland grid is not cost-effective.

So Ubin became an experiment instead. On 10 October 2013 the Energy Market Authority inaugurated the Pulau Ubin Micro-Grid Test-Bed, built by a consortium of Daily Life Renewable Energy and OKH Holdings. Around thirty homes and businesses near the jetty were connected to a small grid of their own, powered by solar panels and biodiesel generators, at 80 cents a unit against about $1.20 for home-generated diesel electricity. Newly able to run chillers and air-conditioners, the village shops changed what they could sell. In 2023 the system was upgraded by EDP Renewables, with 328 kilowatts of solar panels and a megawatt-hour flow battery.

The honest coda is in the parliamentary record. In a written reply in March 2026, the Government reported that in 2025 solar supplied only about 10 percent of the micro-grid's electricity and diesel the remaining 90 percent, and that there were thirteen power trips that year — a long way from the operator's publicity about an island running on sunshine. A better battery is due later in 2026, and the tariff is fixed until 2034. The experiment continues, which is perhaps the right way to see it: Ubin was never the grid's success story. It is the place Singapore tries things out, at village scale, where the results are measured in one community's lights.

## The island today

In 2016 the National Parks Board became the island's single managing agency — it had previously been split among twelve. The Ubin Project, launched in 2014 with the island's community, carries a promise worth quoting exactly: "We are committed to retain Ubin in its rustic state for as long as possible." Its next phase, launched at the project's tenth anniversary, runs to 2034 and includes a programme to restore kampong houses and an Ubin School initiative. About 300,000 visitors a year make the crossing now. The bumboats leave Changi Point Ferry Terminal when about a dozen passengers have gathered, charge $4 a person each way, and take ten minutes. On the island, the bicycle shops by the jetty — the village's main economy — hire out bikes for the day, and the roads lead past the quarries to the Ketam Mountain Bike Park, Singapore's first, built around the old Ketam Quarry.

**Where it fits in the bigger story:** Singapore's other islands were remade for industry, housing or leisure, each in its turn. Ubin's distinction is that the remaking stopped — first because the quarries closed and the people drifted away, and then, deliberately, because the country decided that one island should stay as a reminder of what all of them once were. The last villagers, the schoolboys on their solo nights, and a grid that runs an experiment at village scale are all part of the same decision: to keep one place where Singapore can still see its own beginning.
