Thomson Road, Upper Thomson, Thomson Plaza and the Thomson–East Coast Line all carry the name of a man few Singaporeans could say much about. John Turnbull Thomson arrived in 1841 as the settlement's Government Surveyor, aged twenty, and for the next twelve years he measured, mapped and built much of the settlement while also sketching and painting what he saw. His pictures of the market on the shore, the public buildings and the people at work are some of the few images of 1840s Singapore made by someone who lived there, and twenty years later he wrote a book about the place and the people he had known.

Thomson was born on 10 August 1821 at Glororum in Northumberland, in the north of England. He studied mathematics at Aberdeen and served an engineering apprenticeship in Newcastle upon Tyne, and in 1838, still a teenager, he went out to the Straits of Malacca to work as a surveyor in Penang. He spent about three years there, mostly in the countryside, and later wrote that he had come to know the villagers well enough to share their small complaints and worries.

On 25 October 1841 the East India Company appointed him Government Surveyor for the Straits Settlements, based in Singapore. In 1844 he also took over as Superintendent of Public Works from George Drumgoole Coleman, the architect who had laid out much of the early town and was leaving for home. For the next nine years one young man was responsible for measuring the settlement's land, charting its waters and designing and building most of its public works.

## The town he found

Thomson described Singapore on his arrival as a town of 30,000 people, with "nearly every nation of the earth" represented but the Chinese and Malays the largest groups. The European quarter was a district of handsome houses for merchants and officials. The Chinese town was more closely built and busy with trade, and the Malays lived in villages of wooden houses with leaf-thatched roofs on the edges of town. Fires broke out often in the closely packed quarters and could leave hundreds of families homeless. The harbour, he wrote, was crowded with European, Chinese and Malay ships.

<img src="https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/6/6a/John_Turnbull_Thomson_-_Singapore_Town_from_Pearl%27s_Hill_Looking_East.jpg" alt="A black and white drawing looking down from a hill over rows of long low buildings, a river with a bridge and hills in the distance" style="width: 100%; display: block; border-radius: 4px;">
<em style="display: block; font-size: 0.8em; margin-top: 0.5em;">Singapore town from Pearl's Hill, looking east, drawn by Thomson in about 1845. (John Turnbull Thomson, public domain, via Wikimedia Commons)</em>
</div>

Only about two hundred Europeans lived on shore. In the evenings they met at Scandal Point, on the shore near the Esplanade, at six o'clock to exchange the news, while married couples drove past in their carriages and stopped to talk. There were no banks yet, so each firm kept its own iron safe, and Thomson remembered a merchant's word as being as good as his bond.

He also recorded a harder side of the town. Gang robberies happened almost nightly in the poorer districts, and he blamed much of the crime on secret societies and on a government that paid its senior officials well but its police very little. He wrote that in his first four years he came across the bodies of twenty murdered men on the public roads within a few miles of town. The Company's government put a full-time officer in charge of the police only in 1843, and Thomson described this as long overdue.

## Mapping the island and the strait

Thomson's main job was measurement. He carried out the allotment survey of the town, which fixed the boundaries of plots of land, and a topographical survey of the island and its smaller islands. In 1846 he published the result as *Plan of Singapore Town and Adjoining Districts from Actual Survey*. The map shows the town along the river, Kampong Glam and the roads running out through the plantations, with property boundaries and the names of the country districts. A copy is held by the National Library of Singapore.

<img src="https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/3/32/Admiralty_Chart_No_2404_Strait_of_Singapore_Sheet_3_Surveyed_by_J.T._Thomson_Esq._Government_Survr._1846-54%2C_Published_1855%2C_Corrections_to_1866.jpg/1280px-Admiralty_Chart_No_2404_Strait_of_Singapore_Sheet_3_Surveyed_by_J.T._Thomson_Esq._Government_Survr._1846-54%2C_Published_1855%2C_Corrections_to_1866.jpg" alt="A pale nautical chart of the Strait of Singapore showing the coast of Singapore island, the islands to the south and many small depth soundings in the water" style="width: 100%; display: block; border-radius: 4px;">

*The British Admiralty's chart of the Strait of Singapore, "surveyed by J. T. Thomson, Esq., Government Surveyor, 1846-54", published in 1855. (United Kingdom Hydrographic Office, public domain, via Wikimedia Commons)*

He also worked at sea. Between 1846 and the early 1850s he made a marine survey of the Singapore Strait and of the east coast of Johor up to Pulau Tioman. The British Admiralty published his work as navigation charts and kept issuing corrected editions of them for decades. The charts still carry his name in their titles.

## Singapore in his pictures

Thomson had no formal training as an artist, but he drew and painted throughout his time in Singapore. Many of them were later collected and published in books about him, and they show buildings and scenes that have long since gone.

<img src="https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/d/df/Public_Offices%2C_Singapore_by_John_Turnbull_Thomson_1846.jpg" alt="A sepia drawing of a large two-storey colonial building with columns and a portico, with three uniformed sepoys standing in front" style="width: 100%; display: block; border-radius: 4px;">

*The Public Offices, designed by George Drumgoole Coleman, in a drawing Thomson made in 1846, with three uniformed guards in front. (John Turnbull Thomson, public domain, via Wikimedia Commons)*

His drawing of the Public Offices shows the government's own building as an orderly classical block. The view from Pearl's Hill shows the town from above, with the low roofs of the Chinese town, the river and its bridges and the hills beyond. His drawing of Telok Ayer Market shows the octagonal market hall standing on stilts over the sea before the land in front of it was reclaimed later in the century.

<div style="float: left; max-width: 240px; width: 38%; margin: 0.25em 1.5em 1em 0;">
<img src="https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/3/35/ChineseKayTribeStonecutters-Singapore.jpg" alt="A pencil sketch showing several small figures of Chinese stonecutters in different working poses, squatting and hammering at blocks of stone" style="width: 100%; display: block; border-radius: 4px;">
<em style="display: block; font-size: 0.8em; margin-top: 0.5em;">Hakka stonecutters on Pulau Ubin, sketched by Thomson. (John Turnbull Thomson, public domain, via Wikimedia Commons)</em>
</div>

He also drew people at work. One page of his sketches shows Hakka stonecutters on Pulau Ubin at their trade, squatting over blocks of granite with hammers and chisels. Stonecutters like these quarried and dressed the granite for his largest project, the lighthouse on Pedra Branca, which was first lit in October 1851. That story is told in [an earlier post on Horsburgh Lighthouse](/2026/10/15/horsburgh-lighthouse-building-the-light-on-pedra-branca/).

Thomson's interest in the interior took him up the Kallang River by canoe with a few friends and five Malay boatmen. He described tall trees shading the stream, snakes on the branches overhead and Chinese gambier and pepper plantations in the jungle, where the planters gave the visitors tea. Near a great swamp the ground was covered in tiger footprints. Tigers were a real danger to plantation workers at the time, and he described how a tiger caught in a pit trap became an occasion for the European community to gather. The government paid a reward of 100 Spanish dollars for each tiger's head. [An earlier post covers the years when Singapore had wild tigers](/2026/09/18/when-singapore-had-wild-tigers/).

<div style="float: right; max-width: 240px; width: 38%; margin: 0.25em 0 1em 1.5em;">
<img src="https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/7/7b/The_Hon._Hoh-Ah-Kay_Whampoa%2C_C.M.G.%2C_M.L.C.%2C_and_Consul_for_Wellcome_V0037527.jpg/1280px-The_Hon._Hoh-Ah-Kay_Whampoa%2C_C.M.G.%2C_M.L.C.%2C_and_Consul_for_Wellcome_V0037527.jpg" alt="An oval photographic portrait of an older Chinese man in a dark jacket and cap, wearing medals on his chest" style="width: 100%; display: block; border-radius: 4px;">
<em style="display: block; font-size: 0.8em; margin-top: 0.5em;">Hoo Ah Kay, known as Whampoa, photographed by J. Taylor in about 1881. (Wellcome Collection, CC BY 4.0, via Wikimedia Commons)</em>
</div>

One of the friends Thomson wrote about most warmly was Hoo Ah Kay, the merchant known as Whampoa. Thomson first knew him as a boy helping his father in a shop in the Chinese town, supplying ships with beef, bread and vegetables. Later Thomson was a regular guest at Whampoa's orange garden at Toa Payoh and at his dinners, where naval officers and merchants mixed. Whampoa once looked around Thomson's dining room, which was hung with oil paintings, and said he wished he could "scratch" as well as Thomson. Thomson teased him about the difference between "scratch" and "sketch", but when Whampoa asked him to paint a portrait of his father before the old man left for China, Thomson did so. He later saw it kept with care in Whampoa's house. Whampoa's own story is told in [an earlier post on the merchant who brought Singapore its first ice](/2026/09/15/whampoa-the-merchant-who-brought-singapores-first-ice/).

Thomson also admired Abdullah bin Abdul Kadir, the Malay teacher and writer known as Munshi Abdullah, and quoted his account of the early town at length. In 1874 Thomson published an English translation of parts of the *Hikayat Abdullah*, which helped bring the book to English readers. [An earlier post covers Munshi Abdullah's life](/2026/10/10/munshi-abdullah-the-scribe-who-watched-singapore-begin/).

## What he built

As Superintendent of Public Works, Thomson designed or built a wide range of the settlement's public works in the 1840s. The best known is the hospital for poor Chinese patients on Pearl's Hill, paid for by Tan Tock Seng, whose foundation stone was laid in 1844. He built the first bridge across the Kallang River, known as Thomson Bridge, and repaired Coleman Bridge over the Singapore River. He added a spire to St Andrew's Church, and he is credited with the Dalhousie Obelisk, put up in 1850 to mark the visit of the Governor-General of India. He also laid out roads running from the town into the interior, including the road that now bears his name.

<img src="https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/c/c3/Tan_Tock_Seng_Hospital_circa_1844-1856.jpg" alt="An old photograph of a long single-storey building with a classical front and arched windows, standing on open ground" style="width: 100%; display: block; border-radius: 4px;">

*The Chinese pauper hospital on Pearl's Hill, now known as the first Tan Tock Seng Hospital, which Thomson designed. Building began in 1844, and patients moved in in 1849. (Unknown author, public domain, via Wikimedia Commons)*

Much of this work was done by convict labourers from India, as was common practice in the Straits Settlements at the time. In 1852 he also wrote a report on how the town could be supplied with clean water, an issue that later governments took up.

## After Singapore

Thomson's health broke down after years of fieldwork in the tropics, and in August 1853 he left Singapore on sick leave and did not return. That month, the Singapore Free Press reported, leading residents held a well-attended meeting in his honour at the News Room in Raffles Place. He spent two years in Britain and travelled in Europe, and early in 1856 he emigrated to New Zealand.

<img src="https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/4/41/John_Turnbull_Thomson_Dunedin.jpg/1280px-John_Turnbull_Thomson_Dunedin.jpg" alt="An oil painting of a wide harbour with low green hills, a few houses along the shore and small boats on the water" style="width: 100%; display: block; border-radius: 4px;">

*Dunedin, New Zealand, painted by Thomson in about 1856, soon after he arrived. (John Turnbull Thomson, public domain, via Wikimedia Commons)*

In New Zealand he became Chief Surveyor of the Otago Province and explored and mapped much of the interior of the South Island. He laid out the town of Invercargill and went on painting the landscapes he surveyed. On 1 May 1876 he was appointed the first head of the country's new national Survey Department, as Surveyor-General, and he held the post until 1879. He wrote *Some Glimpses into Life in the Far East* over the long winter evenings, about twenty years after the events it describes; it was published in London in 1864, and a sequel followed in 1865. He died at his home in Invercargill on 16 October 1884, aged 63. A statue of him with a surveyor's theodolite stands at Ranfurly in Central Otago, and a mountain, a waterfall and a park in New Zealand are named after him.

## His name on today's map

<img src="https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/d/d3/Thomson_Road_Singapore_January_1965_02.jpg/1280px-Thomson_Road_Singapore_January_1965_02.jpg" alt="A colour photograph of a row of two-storey shophouses with shuttered windows beside a wide empty road, with plants along the kerb" style="width: 100%; display: block; border-radius: 4px;">

*Shophouses on Thomson Road in January 1965. (Don Christie, CC BY-SA 4.0, via Wikimedia Commons)*

In Singapore his name survives mainly on the map. Thomson Road follows the line of one of the roads he laid out into the interior, and Upper Thomson Road, Old Upper Thomson Road and the surrounding Thomson district all come from it. The name passed on to Thomson Plaza, Thomson Medical Centre and, in 2020, to the Thomson–East Coast Line. His charts, his 1846 plan and his drawings are held by the National Library, the National Museum of Singapore and collections in New Zealand. [See 11 more historical pictures related to this post →](/gallery/john-turnbull-thomson-the-surveyor-who-drew-1840s-singapore/)

**Why it matters today:** Much of what we can picture of 1840s Singapore comes from one man, who was paid to measure the place but also chose to draw it and later to write about its people. His surveys fixed the boundaries of land and roads that still shape parts of the city, and his pictures and memoir record a small, rough and crowded port town in the years before photography reached it. His name is on a road, a district and an MRT line that thousands of people use every day, while his story is much less well known.
