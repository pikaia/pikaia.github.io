Raffles Place, Raffles Hotel, Raffles Institution, a university, an MRT interchange, and a statue at the spot where he supposedly first stepped ashore — Sir Stamford Raffles's name is stamped across Singapore more than almost anyone else's. The man whose actual signature secured the island for Britain, on a treaty that ceded it "in full sovereignty and property... for ever," has a street, a lane and a bridge named after him. All three spell his name wrong.

## A trading post, not a colony

What Raffles actually obtained in February 1819 was permission to "maintain a factory or factories" on the island — a trading post, not territory. The Sultan who signed it, Hussein, was the elder son of the previous ruler but had been passed over for the throne in favour of his younger brother, who held it with Dutch backing; Raffles installed Hussein as a rival Sultan specifically so he'd have someone willing to sign. The Temenggong who co-signed, a separate figure named Abdul Rahman who administered the area around Singapore, was a subordinate noble of contested authority in his own right. The Dutch objected to the whole arrangement immediately, and for good reason: none of it was obviously legal.

The man who actually ran the settlement on that shaky footing was William Farquhar, appointed its first "Resident" — the East India Company's term for the officer who governed a settlement day to day, doubling as magistrate, treasurer and the Company's political liaison to whichever local ruler still nominally held sovereignty. Raffles, based mostly in Bencoolen with the grander title of Lieutenant-Governor, was Farquhar's distant superior and visited only rarely. Farquhar held the Singapore post for four and a half years before Raffles dismissed him in 1823, a story with its own long tail of injustice — [told in full here](/2026/09/17/william-farquhar-the-co-founder-written-out-of-the-story/). What matters for this story is simpler: when Farquhar left, the island's legal status was still exactly as unresolved as it had been in 1819.

## The scholar who took the job

His replacement was an unusual choice for a colonial administrative post. John Crawfurd was born in 1783 on the Scottish island of Islay, trained as a physician at Edinburgh, and joined the East India Company as a surgeon in 1803. Posted to Penang in 1808, he taught himself Malay well enough to become one of the era's serious scholars of the language and the region, and it was there he first met Raffles. He followed Raffles into the 1811 invasion of Java and was appointed Resident at the court of Yogyakarta, where he spent years cultivating relationships with Javanese aristocrats and collecting manuscripts. Returning to Britain in 1816, he wrote the three-volume *History of the Indian Archipelago* — for years the most thorough English-language account of the region available — and was elected a Fellow of the Royal Society.

In 1821 he led a diplomatic mission to Siam and Cochinchina on behalf of the Governor-General in Bengal, a difficult posting that left him experienced in exactly the kind of patient, formal negotiation Singapore's unresolved status now needed. He arrived to take over as the island's second Resident on 9 June 1823 — a physician, an Orientalist and a seasoned diplomat, sent to finish a job Raffles had started and never actually closed.

## The treaty that actually mattered

Two things happened in 1824 that together settled Singapore's status for good. In March, Britain and the Netherlands signed the Anglo-Dutch Treaty of London, dividing the region into separate spheres of influence — the Dutch withdrew every objection to the British holding Singapore, in exchange for Britain handing over its own remaining claims on Sumatra. That resolved the international dispute. It did nothing to resolve the local one: Raffles's 1819 arrangement was still just a trading permission from a Sultan and Temenggong of disputed standing, not a transfer of the island itself.

Crawfurd closed that gap on 2 August 1824. In a formal Treaty of Friendship and Alliance, Sultan Hussein and Temenggong Abdul Rahman did not grant a factory — they ceded, in the treaty's own words, "in full sovereignty and property to the Honourable the English East India Company, their heirs and successors for ever, the Island of Singapore... together with the adjacent seas, straits, and islets, to the extent of ten geographical miles." A separate article abrogated every earlier agreement between the two sides outright. In exchange, the Sultan received 33,200 Spanish dollars up front and a lifetime stipend of 1,300 dollars a month — more than triple his old 1819 annual rate — and the Temenggong received 26,800 dollars and 700 a month. The Governor-General in Bengal, Lord Amherst, ratified it that November.

It's a dry legal document next to Raffles's landing, but it's the one that would have held up if the Dutch had ever pressed the point, and the one every later British claim to the island actually rested on.

## Running what he'd just secured

Crawfurd's instructions were to cut spending, but he spent anyway, on reclamation work along the Singapore River that the settlement's traders had been asking for. He edited and part-financed the *Singapore Chronicle*, the island's first newspaper, whose first issue appeared on 1 January 1824 — seven months before his own treaty. He also moved against slavery within the settlement: the following month, in September 1824, he freed a group of enslaved women who had fled the Sultan's compound complaining of mistreatment, part of a broader push that left Singapore, unusually for a colonial free port of the era, without slavery in any form the local chiefs tried to maintain it under. Trade and population kept climbing through his three years in the post, building on the momentum Farquhar had already established. He left Singapore on 14 August 1826, closing out a tenure of just over three years, brief even by the standards of the men who preceded and followed him.

Crawford Street, Crawford Lane, Crawford Bridge and Crawford Park are all that's left of him on a map — each one spelled with an "o" he never used. He went on to a long second career in Britain as a prolific Orientalist writer and an outspoken advocate for free trade, but it's the treaty that has the better claim on his memory here. **Why it matters today:** Raffles picked the site and struck the first, legally shaky bargain for it. Farquhar spent four and a half years actually building the settlement on that shaky ground. Crawfurd is the one who sat down with the Sultan and Temenggong a second time and got a document that would survive a legal challenge — full sovereignty, in perpetuity, no ambiguity left for the Dutch or anyone else to exploit. Singapore remembers the first two names. The one that closed the deal ended up on a bridge, misspelled.
