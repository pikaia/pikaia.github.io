When Ya'acob Mohamed stepped down from Parliament in December 1980, Prime Minister Lee Kuan Yew paid tribute to him for his part in winning Malay support for the People's Action Party in the early 1960s. Ya'acob had left UMNO for the PAP in 1958, taking 30 of its members with him, and went on to serve as a minister of state and as Singapore's envoy to the Philippines, India and Egypt. He also represented the Southern Islands, whose villages and schools have since disappeared, and today he is far less well known than many of the first-generation leaders he worked with.

## An orphan who wanted to teach

<img src="https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/4/4e/Some_of_the_Brunei_Malay_teachers_%28former_students_of_Sultan_Idris_Training_College%2C_Tanjong_Malim%2C_Perak%2C_from_1932_to_1948%29.jpg/1280px-Some_of_the_Brunei_Malay_teachers_%28former_students_of_Sultan_Idris_Training_College%2C_Tanjong_Malim%2C_Perak%2C_from_1932_to_1948%29.jpg" alt="A black and white group photograph of about twenty men in shirts and ties, some seated and some standing, in front of a building" style="width: 100%; display: block; border-radius: 4px;">

*Former students of the Sultan Idris Training College at Tanjong Malim, the Malay teachers' college that turned Ya'acob down, in a photograph from between 1932 and 1948. (Government of Brunei, public domain, via Wikimedia Commons)*

Ya'acob bin Mohamed was born on 25 January 1925 at Sungai Pisang in Kelantan, and his family moved to Johor when he was still an infant. His mother died when he was eight and his father when he was twelve. Several teachers helped him to stay in school, and one of them eventually adopted him. He often came top of his class.

He wanted to be a teacher, and he came first in the examination for teachers in Johor. His application to the Malay teachers' college at Tanjong Malim, in Perak, was still turned down, because he had not been born in Johor. Deeply disappointed, he moved to Singapore, where he lived with relatives and studied at the Madrasah Khairiyah, a religious school.

## From nationalist to UMNO

The rejection added to his unhappiness with British rule. In Johor he joined the left-wing Malay Nationalist Party and its youth wing, Angkatan Pemuda Insaf, known as API. When the British banned these groups in the late 1940s, he returned to Singapore, joined the United Malays National Organisation (UMNO) in 1949 and became head of its Bukit Panjang branch.

The PAP was founded in 1954, and Ya'acob was invited to join it that year. He declined. At the time he believed that UMNO's talks with the British over Malaya's independence would also lead to independence for Singapore, through a merger of the two.

## The switch to the PAP

He changed his mind when UMNO indicated that independence for Singapore was not one of its priorities. In 1958 he joined the PAP, and 30 other UMNO members followed him. The Founders' Memorial describes 31 Singapore UMNO leaders switching overnight, and dates the move to early 1959. UMNO condemned the defectors as traitors to the Malay community. Ya'acob, a devout Muslim and a forceful speaker, quickly won the trust of the PAP's leaders, and within months he was chairman of the party's Bukit Panjang branch.

*The Old Parliament House, where the Legislative Assembly elected in 1959 met, photographed in 2006. (User:Sengkang, copyrighted free use, via Wikimedia Commons)*

In the general election of May 1959 he stood for Bukit Timah, a mainly Chinese constituency. He won 6,174 votes, or 61.1 per cent, against 24.4 per cent for the Liberal Socialist candidate and 14.5 per cent for an independent. The PAP won 43 of the 51 seats in the Legislative Assembly and formed Singapore's first fully elected government.

## The Southern Islands

<img src="https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/f/f0/Singapore%3B_a_view_across_part_of_Blakang_Mati_Island._Waterc_Wellcome_V0037528.jpg/1280px-Singapore%3B_a_view_across_part_of_Blakang_Mati_Island._Waterc_Wellcome_V0037528.jpg" alt="A watercolour of a calm bay with small green islands, a few low buildings with red roofs on the shore and palm trees in the foreground" style="width: 100%; display: block; border-radius: 4px;">

*A view across part of Blakang Mati towards the smaller southern islands, painted in 1880. (J. Taylor, Wellcome Collection, CC BY 4.0, via Wikimedia Commons)*

In September 1963, days after Singapore joined Malaysia, Ya'acob moved to the Southern Islands constituency, which took in Blakang Mati (now Sentosa), Pulau Brani and the smaller islands south of the harbour. The seat had been held since 1959 by Ahmad Jabri bin Akib of UMNO, who stood again for the Singapore Alliance. Ya'acob won it with 2,764 votes, or 55.4 per cent.

The islanders lived mostly in kampongs of fishermen, boatmen and workers for the British military. Blakang Mati alone had about 1,000 residents by 1959, in two villages on its northern shore, one mainly Malay and one mainly Hokkien. Until 1959 they drew their water from wells and springs or bought it from military reservoirs, and in that year the British army laid a pipeline to the island from Keppel Harbour.

As their assemblyman, Ya'acob worked to improve living conditions on the islands. By 1963 there were three primary schools on Blakang Mati and Pulau Brani, with about 750 pupils, as well as Islamic classes for Muslim children. In 1964 the government built the Blakang Mati Integrated Primary School, which brought the Malay, English and Chinese streams together under one roof at a cost of $175,000, and in July Ya'acob officially opened a new school on the island. That year he also opened a mosque on Pulau Sebarok for about 40 Muslim workers of the oil company Esso.

*The Malay village on Pulau Brani in about 1900, one of the island communities that later formed part of the Southern Islands constituency. (G. R. Lambert & Co., public domain, via Wikimedia Commons)*

The island communities did not last. In the early 1970s Blakang Mati was redeveloped as the leisure island of Sentosa, and its villagers were moved to the mainland, many of them to flats in Telok Blangah. Some of the integrated school's staff and pupils went to Telok Blangah Integrated School, and the old school site became Sentosa's Maritime Museum. Other island villages were cleared over the following decades for industry and, in the case of Pulau Seking, the last of them, for the Semakau landfill. Its settlement was demolished in 1993.

## Merger and separation

Ya'acob's most important years in politics came during the merger. In 1990 he was awarded the Distinguished Service Order, after his death, for his vital role in Singapore's political development, particularly from 1961 to 1965. These were years in which Malay leaders in the PAP were often branded traitors by their opponents.

On 16 July 1964 the Straits Times reported his appeal to Singapore's Malays to "have patience". Five days later, on 21 July, race riots broke out in Singapore.

Singapore left Malaysia on 9 August 1965. Ya'acob was deeply disappointed, and he later summed up the moment with a Malay saying: "The rice has turned into porridge", meaning that what is done cannot be undone. On 15 December 1965 he told the new Parliament that Singapore's Malays would not migrate to Malaysia. "We Malays have never migrated," he said. At Lee Kuan Yew's request the speech was broadcast on the radio three times.

He served as Parliamentary Secretary and then Minister of State in the Prime Minister's Office, and later as Senior Minister of State. In 1968 he became the first MP for the new Kampong Ubi constituency, winning 81.9 per cent of the vote. He held the seat in 1972, with 56.9 per cent against candidates from the Singapore Malay National Organisation (PKMS) and the United National Front, and in 1976, with 68.5 per cent against PKMS.

## A Malay school of its own

Ya'acob was a strong advocate for the Malay community. He argued for recognition of the special position of the Malays and for the promotion of the Malay language, held that Singapore's first head of state should be Malay, and lobbied for Malay-medium secondary education. He said that he helped the Malays because they were the community "least advanced in Singapore", not simply because they were Malays, and he remained a firm supporter of multiracialism.

He backed Sang Nila Utama Secondary School, Singapore's first Malay-medium secondary school. It was opened on Upper Aljunied Road on 14 October 1961 by the Minister for Education, Yong Nyuk Lin, with 477 pupils and 18 teachers. Malay-medium pre-university classes followed in 1965, and English-stream classes were added in 1968. Enrolment fell as English became the main language of schooling. The pre-university classes ended in 1979, when the Ministry of Education phased out pre-university centres that did not teach in English, only two Malay-stream secondary classes were left by 1984, and the school closed in 1988. Its buildings were demolished for the Bidadari housing estate, and in 2019 a road there was named Sang Nila Utama Road at the Malay community's request.

## Envoy abroad

Ya'acob served as Singapore's first ambassador to the Philippines from 1969 to 1971, and President Ferdinand Marcos decorated him with the Order of Sikatuna. He was High Commissioner to India from 1977 to 1984, and in 1984 he became ambassador to Egypt. In Cairo he drew attention to the living conditions of Singapore students at Al-Azhar University and started a fund-raising drive for a hostel for them. He resigned in April 1986 because of poor health.

He had a long link with Egypt. In 1962 President Gamal Abdel Nasser awarded him the Order of the United Arab Republic. In 1981 the National University of Singapore gave him an honorary Doctor of Laws for his contribution to a harmonious multiracial nation.

## The politician who never retired

After he left Parliament, Ya'acob kept speaking at public forums and writing letters to Malay-language newspapers. He kept good relations with the government, but he believed that citizens had the right to offer constructive criticism of its policies without fear. "To compromise is not to give in," he said in a 1986 interview.

He also served as deputy president of the Asian Sepak Takraw Federation and president of the Singapore Amateur Sepak Takraw Association, was involved in the Singapore Silat Federation, and helped to set up the Lembaga Biasiswa Kenangan Maulud, a scholarship fund for needy Malay students.

In August 1989 he suffered a heart attack in Kuala Lumpur. He died of leukaemia at the Singapore General Hospital on 11 October 1989, aged 64, and was buried in the Muslim cemetery at Jalan Bahar. He left his wife, Hasmah binti Awang, three sons and five daughters. A biography in Malay by Sulaiman Jeem and Abdul Ghani Hamid followed in 1990, and in 1992 a scholarship and bursary in his name were set up for needy students in Kampong Ubi.

**Why it matters today:** Ya'acob Mohamed spent his political life arguing that standing up for the Malay community and building a multiracial Singapore could go together. His move from UMNO in 1958 helped the PAP win Malay support at a decisive time, and he spoke for the Malays in Parliament through merger and separation. The Malay-medium school he championed and the island villages he represented have both disappeared, which may be one reason his name is less familiar than those of his contemporaries.
