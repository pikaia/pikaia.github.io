For more than a century, the Singapore River was Singapore's main cargo terminal, and it never had a proper dock. Ocean-going ships anchored out in the roads while flat-bottomed lighters — bumboats, twakows, tongkangs — ferried every sack, crate and barrel between them and the godowns lining the riverbank, load by load, by hand.

## A river with no dock

Singapore's harbour was deep enough for ocean-going ships, but the river itself was shallow and narrow, too tight for anything bigger than a lighter to get past its mouth. So ships stayed out at anchor, and cargo travelled the last stretch — ship to godown, godown to ship — in small boats built for exactly that job. Early on, the trade belonged to Chulia lightermen from South India, using a rounded, double-ended boat called a tongkang. By the early 20th century, Chinese crews — Teochews first, Hokkiens close behind — had taken over with a different design, the twakow: a wide, nearly flat-bottomed hull, built to carry as much weight as possible through water barely deep enough to float it.

## The work itself

A twakow crew was small — two to four men, usually sharing a dialect, a surname or a hometown, working for a towkay who fronted them advances in hard times and expected loyalty in return. The work was exactly as hard as it looked: cases of dried goods that could top 200 kilograms, holds that were "unbearably hot and humid," and a boat that would tip if a load went in wrong. Lightermen were known to work more than 24 hours straight when a ship needed clearing, sleeping in snatches between loads. By 1983, close to 800 lighters were working the Singapore River and Kallang Basin between them — a whole floating economy that most of the office towers now standing on the riverbank have no memory of at all.

I never worked on the river myself, but I do have a secondhand link to the buildings it fed. I once worked as an FX trader for Bank of America in Singapore — one of the founding banks of Singapore's offshore US-dollar market — and an older colleague once showed me a photograph, his own hair still long in it, of a godown the bank had once managed. I couldn't say for certain it was one of these riverside godowns rather than one elsewhere in the harbour, but I recall the river being visible in the shot. He'd shown it to me simply to make a point: the bank's roots here went back further than most people, himself included, ever gave it credit for.

## Why it had to be cleaned

By the 1970s the Singapore River and the connected Kallang Basin were catching the runoff of a fifth of the island's land area — and most of what flowed into them had nowhere else to go. A government count from the period puts a number on almost everything that ended up in the water: 27,000 squatter families discharging sullage straight into the river; 3,600 backyard industries and 430 motor-repair shops; 610 pig farms and 480 duck farms; 4,900 street hawkers washing down beside open drains; 390 fruit and vegetable wholesalers dumping spoiled produce; 21,000 premises with no sewer connection at all, some still on nightsoil buckets emptied by hand. On top of all of that sat close to 800 bumboats and 64 boatyards, working cargo and repairs directly over water that had nowhere to flush to. The river was, by any practical measure, an open drain that boats happened to use.

## Ten years, on the record

On 27 February 1977, at the opening of an addition to the Upper Peirce Reservoir, Prime Minister Lee Kuan Yew set a public target: "It should be a way of life to keep the water clean, to keep every stream, every culvert, every rivulet, free from unnecessary pollution. In ten years let us have fishing in the Singapore River and fishing in the Kallang River. It can be done." The Straits Times covered the same speech the next morning, though its own headline led with reservoirs and water rationing rather than the line that would end up remembered.

What followed was a genuinely multi-agency campaign, run over exactly the ten years Lee had named. Squatters, backyard trades and pig and duck farmers were resettled into public housing and proper industrial estates — more than 26,000 farming families alone. Street hawkers were moved into purpose-built food centres with running water and sewer connections. Vegetable wholesalers went to a new site at Pasir Panjang. Boatyards and the charcoal trade were phased out or resited to Lorong Halus. And the bumboats — all 800 of them — were moved wholesale out of the Singapore River and Kallang Basin to a new lighterage anchorage at Pasir Panjang, ending, for good, the trade that had run on this river for over a century. By September 1983, traditional lighterage on the Singapore River had effectively ceased. The nightsoil bucket system, one of the last holdouts, was phased out in January 1987.

## What the river got back

In 1987, Lee marked the campaign's completion in terms that read almost like disbelief at what had actually been pulled off: "Only ten years ago our rivers were like sewers, smelly, dirty and devoid of fish or any aquatic life... It was a triumph to have diverted all toilet and sullage water into sewers, and to have resettled hawkers, boatmen, pig and duck farmers. We now have pleasant riverscapes. We can walk along the river sides and fish or boat, ski and swim, all unthinkable only a few years ago." Fish returned; so did rowing regattas, a river carnival, dragon boat races, and eventually the bars and restaurants that now line both banks.

I worked in one of those CBD towers myself for a stretch, at HSBC, in the years after the clean-up was long finished. I used to buy mee pok from a small food centre by the water on my lunch break. One day an expat trader took up a bet with a colleague to swim across the river. Everyone around us knew, as a matter of public record, that the water had been cleaned — and still felt something close to revulsion at the idea of actually getting into it. He made it across unharmed. The campaign had succeeded on paper years before it fully succeeded in anyone's head.

**Why it matters today:** the Singapore River's clean-up gets less attention than the housing programme or the port, maybe because there's no single building to point to — just water that looks ordinary now, exactly the way "ordinary" was the entire point. But it is one of the clearest examples of a Singapore pattern: a hard, unglamorous, decade-long administrative effort, aimed at a target that sounded almost naive when it was announced, that actually landed on schedule. The bumboats that made the river worth polluting in the first place are gone from it entirely — surviving only as tourist boats, a couple of bronze statues, and the name "Boat Quay" itself, now advertising restaurants instead of the trade it was named for.
