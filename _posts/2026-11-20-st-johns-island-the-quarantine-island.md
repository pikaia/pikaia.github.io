For more than a century, almost every stranger who came to Singapore by sea began their Singapore story on the same small island, six and a half kilometres south of the mainland: St John's Island. Migrants, pilgrims, traders and troops were landed there first, examined, disinfected, vaccinated and held — sometimes for two days, sometimes for weeks — before they were allowed to become part of the port city. At its height the island's quarantine station was the largest in the British Empire. Today the same island holds a holiday lodge, two marine laboratories and a small, ageing colony of cats, and most Singaporeans know it, if at all, as a quiet place for a picnic. This is the story of the island that stood guard at Singapore's door.

  function splitLongSentence(text, offset, duration) {

## The first anchorage

St John's was Singapore's doorstep before it was anything else. On 28 January 1819, Stamford Raffles' fleet anchored off the island, and representatives of the Temenggong of Johor came out to the ships and assured the British that there were no Dutch settlers on the main island. Only the next day, 29 January, did Raffles land on Singapore itself and begin the founding of the port.

For its first decades the island served the new port as an outer signal post. Raffles' instructions of February 1819 ordered a European stationed on St John's with a boat and a small crew, to board square-sailed vessels passing through the Straits — in effect, heralds announcing the new port to passing captains. A signal flagstaff, moved from Pulau Tambakul (today's Kusu Island) in 1823, stood on the island until 1833, and the flag survives in the island's Malay name: Pulau Sekijang Bendera, *bendera* being the Malay word for a flag. Its neighbour, Lazarus Island, is Pulau Sekijang Pelepah, after its palm fronds; both began as "Sekijang", barking-deer island, a name the European charts gradually bent into "St John".

When the signal station was abandoned in 1834, the island went quiet. A visiting naturalist that year found a single old Malay inhabitant among coconut, orange, guava and plantain trees. It would not stay quiet for long.

## Cholera makes an island necessary

The legal groundwork came in 1868, when the Quarantine Ordinance gave the port its first real powers: a ship arriving with cholera, smallpox or fever aboard had to anchor outside the harbour limits, hoist the yellow quarantine flag, and let nobody board or leave until it was medically cleared. Merchants grumbled for years that the rules interfered with trade. Then, in 1873, cholera arrived from Bangkok despite an order quarantining vessels from Siam, and between July and September there were 857 cases and, by the National Library Board's account, 448 deaths. Shipboard orders, it turned out, were not enough. Singapore needed a place to put the people.

The plan came from the Acting Master Attendant, Henry Ellis: a lazaretto — a quarantine station — on St John's Island, with a hospital, a patrol boat and a burial ground on Peak Island, today's Kusu. It was approved and announced in the Governor's speech to the Legislative Council on 21 March 1874. The station opened that November, barely finished, and was tested at once: its first intake was the passengers of the cholera-stricken S.S. *Milton*, out of Swatow and bound for Penang — over a thousand Chinese passengers landed on an island that had, months before, held almost nobody.

One part of Ellis's plan did not survive contact with the community. Burials began on Kusu, but in 1875 the community leader Cheang Hong Lim objected in writing that the island had been used for over thirty years for Chinese and Malay religious observances, and had been desecrated by the interments. From the early twentieth century, passengers who died on arrival or soon after were buried instead on Lazarus Island, which only officials could visit.

## How quarantine worked

The routine hardened into a system. A ship classed as "infected" — a case of disease within twelve days before arrival — or merely "suspected" sent its passengers to the island. Steerage passengers landed at the jetty and passed through muster sheds, where they changed while their clothes and belongings were steamed in boiler houses; by 1930 they were also sprayed with Izal disinfectant in purpose-built disinfection buildings. The ships themselves were fumigated with sulphur, stored loose on the island. Then came the camps: rows of accommodation with their own kitchens, a vaccination for everyone, and a wait — two or three days for most deck passengers, a week or more for migrants from China — until the doctors cleared the batch for the mainland.

Quarantine, though, ran on a class system. First- and second-class cabin passengers simply presented themselves for clearance and walked ashore; it was the steerage passengers, the ones who had shared a deck or a hold, who were ferried to the island. The station served others besides immigrants: returning Haj pilgrims were routinely processed there too, and in 1890 the pilgrims of the steamer *Queen Margaret*, homeward bound from Mecca, were quarantined on the Deputy Health Officer's instruction because cholera deaths had occurred aboard during the voyage — even though no cases remained when she arrived.

The diseases that kept the island busy changed with the decades: cholera first, then smallpox — the *Singapore Free Press* noted in 1926 that smallpox appeared on two out of every four immigrant ships — and bubonic plague, which prompted a plague hospital on the island after the 1894 Hong Kong outbreak; a plague case was caught there in March 1896. Chickenpox, measles and beri-beri all passed through as well.

## The largest in the Empire

What began in 1874 as a cluster of attap huts grew steadily: a hospital in 1894, more than $300,000 of redevelopment from 1903, and by 1908 a station that covered the whole island. An inventory published in *The Straits Times* in 1935 gives the measure of the place: hospitals for smallpox, cholera, plague and chickenpox; muster sheds and disinfection buildings; sulphur storehouses; a dispensary; a police station, a Coroner's court and a lock-up; barracks for fifteen Sikh policemen, with a temple; a mosque; quarters for gardeners, coolies and workmen; a post office; and telephone wires tying it all to the mainland.

By the mid-1920s the station could hold 6,000 people at any one time, organised by 1935 into 22 camps. Between 1903 and 1923, by the *Singapore Free Press*'s account, it inspected some 8 million arrivals and quarantined about 300,000 of them. A model of the station was exhibited in the Malaya Pavilion at the British Empire Exhibition at Wembley in 1924. The National Library Board describes it as the largest quarantine station in the British Empire; *The Straits Times* in 1935 ranked it the largest in the world after New York's Ellis Island and El Tor in Egypt.

## The voices of the quarantined

The official view was sunny. The *Free Press* in 1926 assured its readers that immigrants "arrive hungry, dirty and miserable after a deck passage through the China Sea and they spend five blissful days… with nothing to do, wholesome food to eat and the beaches of the island on which to lounge". The migrants' own recorded memories, preserved in National Archives oral histories, are more mixed.

Teo Choon Hong, who arrived from Amoy in 1937, remembered the class divide bitterly: "Only those on the lower deck were quarantined. Those who stayed in the cabins did not have to go. There was a class distinction… We were bossed around like chickens and ducks." He was held two days, cooking his own rations in the camp. Teong Eng Siong, arriving from Foochow in 1948, remembered the same system mildly: three to five days, three meals a day — two small slices of bread and milk tea for breakfast, eggs, vegetables and fish later — two showers a day and a big shared hall to sleep in. And Saravana Perumal, who came from Jaffna in 1947, recalled being "locked up in the camp" with rations, firewood and pots, and being frightened by the centipedes and cockroaches. Perumal's story has a second act: in 1955 he returned to St John's as a member of staff, transferred to help set up the island's next institution.

## An island of detentions

Quarantine was never the island's only use. In the First World War, most German men in Singapore were interned on St John's; by 1916, 296 enemy nationals from the island had been shipped on to internment in Australia. In the Second World War the pattern repeated: enemy nationals — some of them refugees who had fled Nazi Germany — were interned there in 1940, Japanese women and children were held on the island from late 1941, and Allied prisoners of war were detained there after the invasion. A Chinese-style moon gate built by German internees still stands on the island's western shore.

When the Malayan Emergency was declared in 1948, the island was fitted out as a detention centre: the first batch, 200 political prisoners from Johor Bahru, arrived on 20 September 1948, and within two years more than 600 people were held there. Among the detainees of the 1950s was C. V. Devan Nair, held for anti-colonial activities, who called the island "St John's University" for the reading he did there; a young lawyer named Lee Kuan Yew visited him on the island in 1952. In Operation Coldstore, on 2 February 1963, 113 left-wing politicians and trade unionists were arrested, and 99 of them were moved to St John's. Nair, released and detained again over the years, went on to become Singapore's third President.

## The other cure

The island's last great institution treated a different kind of arrival. Post-war Singapore counted more than 16,000 opium addicts; opium had been banned in October 1945, yet over 1,400 opium dens were still operating in 1949. In February 1955 the colonial government opened the Opium Treatment Centre on St John's — its first attempt at rehabilitation instead of imprisonment — run, strikingly, by the Prisons Department.

The regime ran in phases: withdrawal over two to four weeks in the prison hospital, the drug tapered rather than stopped outright; then six months to a year of rehabilitation on the island, learning carpentry, canework, tailoring and gardening; then follow-up ashore. By 1966 more than 4,000 addicts had been treated there. In 1973 the centre was converted into Singapore's first Drug Rehabilitation Centre, for addicts of all drugs, and in 1975 it closed, the island earmarked for redevelopment. That same year, from May to October, the emptied island took in South Vietnamese refugees — its last intake of strangers in transit.

## The long goodbye

Quarantine itself died by stages, killed by the aeroplane and the health certificate. Deck passengers from China were exempted in 1971 if they held valid health certificates; compulsory quarantine for arrivals from India ended in 1973. On 14 January 1976, just over a century after the S.S. *Milton*, the quarantine station officially closed. The islanders — staff families and villagers across St John's, Lazarus and Seringat, more than 400 people in the early 1960s — were relocated to the mainland in 1976 and 1977, and the island's little primary school, which had taught the staff's children since the 1950s, closed. A handful of residents stayed on for decades; the last permanent islanders left only in 2017. In 1998 and 1999 a temporary detention centre was built on the island for an expected influx of illegal immigrants and refugees that never came; it stood unused, a fence in search of a purpose, and the island settled into quiet.

## The island today

![The St John's Island jetty from above, with a ferry alongside the pontoon](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/0/0d/St_John%27s_Island_jetty.jpg)

*The island's jetty from above in 2022, a ferry alongside — the modern version of the landing every arrival once made. (Bob Tan, CC BY-SA 4.0, via Wikimedia Commons)*

The afterlife began the same year quarantine ended: in 1976 a subsidised holiday camp opened on the island, and grander resort schemes — restaurants, a golf course, even a casino — came to nothing. Today the overnight option is the St John's Island Lodge, run by the Singapore Land Authority in the island's old fabric: the former holiday bungalow and, under new names, the old camps themselves. Day visitors come on the ferries from Marina South Pier, about half an hour out; there are no shops, and everyone leaves on the last boat. (At the time of writing, the island's own jetty is temporarily closed and ferries land at Seringat jetty, a half-hour's walk away.)

In 2006, reclamation and causeways completed the linking of St John's to Lazarus and Seringat, and the quarantine island became a beach island: you can now walk from the old station site across to Lazarus Island's lagoons.

![Pier Beach on St John's Island: sand, palms and still water](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/a/a6/St._John%27s_Island%2C_Singapore%2C_2023_1.jpg)

*Pier Beach today — the landing shore as a picnic beach, photographed in 2023. (Denis Barthel, CC BY-SA 4.0, via Wikimedia Commons)*

Science moved into the quiet. The Tropical Marine Science Institute opened its island laboratory in 2002; upgraded with national funding, it was launched in 2016 as the St John's Island National Marine Laboratory, Singapore's only offshore marine research facility, open to researchers from all local institutions. Next door, the Marine Aquaculture Centre, established in 2003, works on breeding food fish such as Asian seabass.

![The path through the island's centre, with the Marine Aquaculture Centre and the National Marine Laboratory beyond the trees](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/5/5d/St._John%27s_Island%2C_Singapore%2C_2023_4.jpg)

*The island's centre in 2023: the Marine Aquaculture Centre (right) and the National Marine Laboratory (left) behind the trees. (Denis Barthel, CC BY-SA 4.0, via Wikimedia Commons)*

Since 2014 the island's western reefs have lain inside the Sisters' Islands Marine Park, Singapore's first, and a second marine park has been announced for the waters off Lazarus and Kusu — announced, as of this writing, but not yet gazetted.

And then there are the cats. St John's became locally famous as a cat island: in 2014 it held over a hundred cats in five or six colonies. The SPCA had begun a sterilisation programme in 2011, and the population has since aged and thinned — about sixteen cats by 2018 — cared for now by volunteer feeders who raise money for the old animals' vet bills. The island that once processed eight million arrivals ends its story, for now, as the charge of a few dozen researchers, some weekend picnickers and a small pride of elderly cats.

**Where it fits in the bigger story:** Singapore's island posts usually face outward — Semakau the rubbish island, Bukom the refinery. St John's faced inward: it was the filter through which the port's people had to pass, and for a century the first Singapore that newcomers ever saw was a jetty, a disinfection shed and a camp bed on a small island offshore. The door has closed; the doorstep remains.
