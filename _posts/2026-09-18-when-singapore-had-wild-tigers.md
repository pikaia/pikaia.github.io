By the middle of the 19th century, tigers in Singapore were rumoured to kill a person every day. Pressed on the subject in the House of Commons, the Governor of the Straits Settlements put the real figure at a still-alarming two hundred deaths a year, in a population of only fifty thousand. The last of those tigers was shot in 1930, at the edge of what is now Choa Chu Kang New Town. Wild animals never really left Singapore after that — they just got smaller, and mostly harmless. An otter family crossing the road in the middle of the financial district now gets a road sign of its own.

## A menace worth a bounty

Singapore's tigers were never native in the sense of having nowhere else to go. They swam across the Strait of Johor from Malaya — strong enough swimmers that some were caught in the fishing stakes set along the shore — and found a virgin jungle full of pigs and deer to hunt. The earliest newspaper report of an attack, in the *Singapore Chronicle* on 8 September 1831, described a Chinese man killed, with the same tiger probably responsible for a second death shortly after. In May 1839 two labourers were carried off near what is today Serangoon Road, then known as Rangong Road.

The real danger began with gambier and pepper. When cultivation of the two crops took off from the 1840s, plantations pushed out of the town and into the jungle tigers hunted in; by the late 1840s there were some six hundred of them. Chinese plantation coolies made easy, isolated targets, and reports of encounters rose through the 1830s and 1840s. By the middle of the century tigers were rumoured to kill someone every day. William Butterworth, the Governor of the Straits Settlements, questioned on the subject in the House of Commons, gave a still-alarming estimate of two hundred deaths a year. A village near Bukit Timah was abandoned altogether in 1859 after repeated attacks. Officially, only seven deaths were reported to the police in 1857, against a believed toll of three hundred; plantation owners often kept quiet about the deaths, unwilling to scare off the next batch of workers.

The government's answer was money. A reward of twenty dollars a tiger climbed to fifty, then a hundred, then a hundred and fifty as the toll grew, and hunters could earn more again selling the skin and other parts. Pits four to four and a half metres deep were dug and camouflaged; a tiger caught alive went into a rattan basket too strong to bite through, and one caught in a pit was often simply shot where it lay. Hunting tigers became something between a public service and a sport, lucrative enough that at least two Europeans made a living at it. In 1859 the Superintendent of Convicts, J. F. A. McNair, put convict labourers on regular patrol through Bukit Timah, Serangoon, Changi and Choa Chu Kang; within a year the patrols had killed half a dozen tigers.

## The last tiger

The bounty and the shrinking jungle did their work. A man was killed by a tiger on Thomson Road in 1890, and two more tigers were shot at Bukit Timah in 1896, but sightings after that grew rare. In 1902 a tiger did turn up in the middle of town, hiding under the elevated Bar & Billiard Room of the Raffles Hotel — but it was not one of the wild survivors. It had escaped from a travelling circus on Beach Road and was shot by Charles McGowan Phillips, the principal of nearby Raffles Institution. It's the story most people know; the wild tiger it's usually mistaken for had, by then, almost nowhere left to hide.

The real last tiger was killed on 26 October 1930, at eleven in the morning, on the fringe of Choa Chu Kang village. It weighed 127.8 kilograms. The hunter credited with the shot was Ong Kim Hong; Tan Tian Quee and Low Peng Hoe of the Straits Hunting Party were there too, and a photograph of the wider hunting party — several dozen men and dogs gathered under the village's roadside sign — survives in the National Library's collection.

My wife's family has a thread in this story of its own. One of her relatives, by family account, was part of the hunting party that tracked down that last tiger — though he never claimed to have fired the shot himself, only to have been there.

## From tiger to otter

A century later, Singapore has wild animals again, just not the kind that used to kill a person a day. Smooth-coated otters, extinct here for decades, began turning up in the 1990s and have since colonised the island's waterways so thoroughly that a family swimming past Robertson Quay or Sungei Serangoon is now closer to routine than remarkable — routine enough that the road sign pictured above shares a signpost with an ordinary traffic-slowing notice, not a special warning of its own.

![A family of five smooth-coated otters swimming in Sungei Serangoon, Singapore](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/b/bf/Smooth-coated_otters_%28Lutrogale_perspicillata%29%2C_Sungei_Serangoon%2C_Singapore_-_20130518.jpg)

*A family of smooth-coated otters in Sungei Serangoon (Serangoon Reservoir). (Photo: Kokhuitan / Wikimedia Commons, CC BY-SA 4.0)*

The Oriental Pied Hornbill tells a similar story: locally extinct for decades, it recolonised Singapore from the 1990s onward and is now seen in parks and reserves across the island, from Sungei Buloh to the Botanic Gardens.

![An Oriental Pied Hornbill calling from a tree branch at Sungei Buloh Wetland Reserve, Singapore](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/2/2c/Anthracoceros_albirostris_%28Oriential_Pied_Hornbill%29.jpg)

*An Oriental Pied Hornbill at Sungei Buloh Wetland Reserve. (Photo: Tan Pek Nan / Wikimedia Commons, CC BY 4.0)*

Not every comeback is welcome. Wild boars from the forests of Pulau Ubin and the Central Catchment area have pushed into nearby housing estates often enough that, in a 2020 survey, nearly 40 per cent of Pasir Ris residents said they would rather the animals — along with the neighbourhood's stray dogs — were removed or relocated altogether. The National Parks Board's usual first move is to relocate an animal; it culls only when an animal is judged a genuine danger, as with a boar that attacked a woman and bit an officer in Punggol in February 2021, after residents had been feeding it.

![A group of wild boars, including piglets, on a forest path at Pulau Ubin, Singapore](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/a/ab/Singapore_Wildschweine_auf_Pulau_Ubin_1.jpg)

*Wild boars on a forest path at Pulau Ubin. (Photo: Zairon / Wikimedia Commons, CC BY-SA 4.0)*

Saltwater crocodiles get the same case-by-case treatment. When a nearly three-metre crocodile turned up near Marina East Drive in October 2023, NParks first looked at relocating it to Sungei Buloh Wetland Reserve — already home to twenty crocodiles and with no room for another — and only put it down once that option was closed off. A smaller crocodile spotted off Sentosa Cove in January 2026 was simply kept under watch for a week, with beaches reopened once it moved on; another spotted off the same stretch that May was caught and euthanised in turn.

![Eco-Link@BKE, a vegetated bridge over the Bukit Timah Expressway connecting two nature reserves](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/d/d8/Eco-link_%40_BKE_%2823337731653%29.jpg)

*Eco-Link@BKE, opened 2012, the first bridge of its kind in the Asia-Pacific. (Photo: Jnzl's Photos / Flickr / Wikimedia Commons, CC BY 2.0)*

The clearest sign of how far the management philosophy has shifted sits over the Bukit Timah Expressway, on almost the same ridge that killed two tigers in 1896. The Eco-Link@BKE, a 75-metre vegetated bridge, was built for no other reason than to let animals move between the Bukit Timah and Central Catchment nature reserves without having to cross the highway at all.

**Where it fits in the bigger story:** Singapore spent a century clearing the jungle that made tigers a genuine daily danger, and now spends real effort managing what has grown back in its place — a boar, a crocodile, a hornbill, a family of otters. The forest patches that are left, Bukit Timah and the Central Catchment chief among them, have become some of the few places on the island where the built-up city recedes, which may be exactly why they draw a following among Singapore's own seniors, many of whom now treat a hike through them as a regular part of retirement — a quiet, present-day echo of an island that was once almost entirely theirs.
