In June 1850 the Straits Times reported, in a few lines, that a ship from Calcutta had brought two State prisoners to Singapore. One of them was Bhai Maharaj Singh, a Sikh religious leader who had opposed British rule in Punjab, and he died in the jail on Outram Road six years later. The place where he was cremated became a shrine that people of several faiths visited for decades, and it has since moved twice to reach the Silat Road gurdwara, where it stands today.

## Who he was

<div style="float: left; max-width: 240px; width: 38%; margin: 0.25em 1.5em 1em 0;">
<img src="https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/f/f8/Maharaja_Duleep_Singh_and_Governor-General_Lord_Dalhousie%2C_Lahore%2C_Punjab%2C_ca.1849%E2%80%9350.jpg" alt="A painting of the young Maharaja Duleep Singh in a jewelled turban seated beside Lord Dalhousie in a dark coat, on chairs under an arch" style="width: 100%; display: block; border-radius: 4px;">
<em style="display: block; font-size: 0.8em; margin-top: 0.5em;">Maharaja Duleep Singh, the last ruler of the Sikh kingdom, and the Governor-General, Lord Dalhousie, in a painting from Lahore of about 1849&ndash;50, inscribed in Persian with both their names. (Image: unknown artist, Toor Collection, public domain, via Wikimedia Commons)</em>
</div>

Bhai Maharaj Singh was the head of a Sikh religious order at Naurangabad, in Punjab. After the death of Maharaja Ranjit Singh in 1839, the Sikh kingdom was weakened by disputes over the succession, and in the 1840s it fought two wars against the British East India Company. Sikh accounts describe Maharaj Singh as a leader of the resistance to British rule; the British treated him as an outlaw and put a reward of 10,000 rupees on him. He was arrested near Adampur on 28 December 1849, soon after Punjab was annexed. The Governor-General, Lord Dalhousie, decided that he should be held far from Punjab, and he was sent to Singapore.

<div style="clear: both;"></div>

## Six years at Outram Road

<div style="float: left; max-width: 280px; width: 42%; margin: 0.25em 1.5em 1em 0;">
<img src="/assets/images/straits-times-1850-06-18-page-4.jpg" alt="Page 4 of The Straits Times, 18 June 1850, a page of dense columns of small type" style="width: 100%; display: block; border-radius: 4px;">
<em style="display: block; font-size: 0.8em; margin-top: 0.5em;">Page 4 of The Straits Times, 18 June 1850, which carried the short report of the prisoners' arrival. (Singapore Press Holdings, via NewspaperSG, National Library Board Singapore, public domain)</em>
</div>

The Straits Times of 18 June 1850 reported that the ship *Mahomed Shaw* had arrived from Calcutta "having on board two State prisoners," Maharaj Singh, whom it called the Gooroo, and his companion Khurruck Singh. The paper described them as the instigators of the killing of two British officers at Multan in 1848, a charge that reflected the British view of the events, and added that both were "closely watched, under a strict military guard, in one of the rooms of Her Majesty's gaol."

According to the Central Sikh Gurdwara Board's history, Dalhousie had instructed the authorities in Singapore that the prisoner should not be treated with unnecessary harshness and should be held somewhere clean but secure. He was nevertheless kept in a cell on the upper floor of the new jail, about 14 by 15 feet, whose windows had been walled up. His health failed. He was almost blind within three years, suffered from rheumatic pain in his feet and ankles, and developed a swelling of the tongue and neck that left him unable to eat properly.

<div style="clear: both;"></div>

<div style="float: left; max-width: 240px; width: 42%; margin: 0.25em 1.5em 1em 0;">
<img src="https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/8/83/Bhai_Maharaj_Singh_and_Companion_%28Khurruck_Singh%29_in_a_Prison_Cell.jpg" alt="A drawing of two Sikh men in a vaulted stone cell, one standing in a white robe and turban and the other seated on the floor" style="width: 100%; display: block; border-radius: 4px;">
<em style="display: block; font-size: 0.8em; margin-top: 0.5em;">Bhai Maharaj Singh and Khurruck Singh in a prison cell, a drawing by Colesworthy Grant dated 1850. (Image: Colesworthy Grant, public domain, via Wikimedia Commons)</em>
</div>

He died in the jail on 5 July 1856. The Straits Times of 8 July reported that "the State prisoner at Singapore, better known as 'The Gooroo'" had died "at one o'clock on Saturday last," and suggested that his companion might now be allowed to return to India. Khurruck Singh was later moved to Penang, where he also died. According to the gurdwara board, it was Khurruck Singh who performed the cremation, near the prison.

<div style="clear: both;"></div>

## The shrine

<div style="float: left; max-width: 280px; width: 42%; margin: 0.25em 1.5em 1em 0;">
<img src="/assets/images/straits-times-1856-07-08-page-4.jpg" alt="Page 4 of The Straits Times, 8 July 1856, a page of dense columns of small type" style="width: 100%; display: block; border-radius: 4px;">
<em style="display: block; font-size: 0.8em; margin-top: 0.5em;">Page 4 of The Straits Times, 8 July 1856, which reported the death of the Sikh prisoner. (Singapore Press Holdings, via NewspaperSG, National Library Board Singapore, public domain)</em>
</div>

The cremation site did not stay anonymous. According to accounts collected by the Sikh community, Tamils were the first to mark it with stones and leave flowers there. Muslims later put up green flags around it and treated it as a *keramat*, a holy grave, and Sikhs came as well. Many people in Singapore came to know the man buried there not as Maharaj Singh but as "Baba Karam Singh," a name that may have come from an epithet meaning a worker of wonders.

<div style="clear: both;"></div>

## Moving twice

In the 1940s the shrine was moved from the prison area to a small field on the grounds of Singapore General Hospital. In 1961 a Sikh police officer installed a copy of the Guru Granth Sahib there, and the number of visitors grew until the crowds were getting in the way of the hospital's ambulances. The Sikh community and the government agreed to move the shrine again, and from 12 October 1966 it was transferred to the grounds of the Silat Road gurdwara, the temple that the [Sikh Police Contingent](/2026/09/26/the-sikh-police-who-came-in-1881-and-the-community-they-left-behind/) had helped to build in 1924.

A memorial building dedicated to Bhai Maharaj Singh was opened there on 23 October 1995, and on 14 November 1999 the National Heritage Board declared the site a historic site.

**Where it fits in the bigger story:** The Outram Road prison is gone, and the few lines the Straits Times gave to its State prisoners in 1850 and 1856 are easy to miss. What kept this story alive in Singapore was not the official record but the people who marked a cremation site with stones, flags and flowers without always knowing whose it was, and a community that carried the shrine with it twice as the city was rebuilt around it.
