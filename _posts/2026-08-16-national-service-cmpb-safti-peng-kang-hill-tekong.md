I did my National Service the way a lot of Singaporean men my age did: reported to CMPB, got sent to SAFTI for Basic Military Training, was housed there, and trained on the slopes of Peng Kang Hill. Recruits today go through more or less the same rite of passage, but almost none of it happens in the places I remember — BMT has been on Pulau Tekong for decades, and even CMPB itself has since moved.

*A Basic Military Training passing-out parade at The Float @ Marina Bay, photographed in January 2015. (Photo: Seloloving / Wikimedia Commons, CC BY-SA 4.0)*

National Service began with the National Service (Amendment) Act, passed on 14 March 1967, after Britain announced it would withdraw its forces from east of Suez. Registration ran from 28 March to 18 April that year, with roughly 9,000 men born between January and June 1949 called up by pink reminder cards to register at CMPB and a handful of district offices. The top 10 per cent went straight into two years of full-time service with the newly formed 3rd and 4th Singapore Infantry Regiments at Taman Jurong Camp, with training beginning that September. CMPB has been the system's registration hub ever since, historically located at Depot Road — until a brand-new CMPB opened at Bukit Gombak, opposite Cashew MRT station, on 14 October 2025. The new building consolidates medical classification and health screening under one roof, adds a digital queue and notification system, a sheltered all-weather IPPT track, and a 700-seat food court — a considerably more comfortable first stop than the one earlier generations queued through.

SAFTI itself was established on 14 February 1966 at Pasir Laba, officially opened by Dr Goh Keng Swee that June, and became the institution that trained the SAF's earliest instructors, NCOs and officer cadets — its first commissioning parade produced 117 new officers in July 1967. Peng Kang Hill, where I trained, was one of several training hills at the SAFTI Live Firing Area at Pasir Laba, alongside others nicknamed FOFO Hill, Bunker Hill and Elephant Hill. MINDEF's own account of it, marking National Service's 55th anniversary, put it simply: soldiers used to charge up and down its steep slopes repeatedly, and the hill remains in use for training even today.

![Commissioning parade at SAFTI Military Institute, Singapore, 2002](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/8/89/SAFTI_MI_commissioning_parade.jpg)

*A commissioning parade at SAFTI Military Institute, photographed in 2002. (Photo: Rifleman 82 / Wikimedia Commons, public domain)*

What happened next is really two separate stories that are easy to mix up. General Basic Military Training, for every recruit rather than just officer candidates, was moved off the Pasir Laba mainland site to a new Basic Military Training Centre on Pulau Tekong over the course of the late 1980s and into the 1990s, as the old site ran out of room and the increasingly built-up western part of Singapore left less space for live-firing ranges. Separately, SAFTI itself was reconstituted as a unified tri-service institute — a plan Prime Minister Lee Kuan Yew proposed in 1982 — with construction of a new campus at Upper Jurong Road breaking ground on 9 June 1990 and finishing in 1995; SAFTI Military Institute officially opened on 25 August 1995, and today trains officers and specialists rather than housing general BMT. ![Aerial view of the Basic Military Training Centre at Pulau Tekong, Singapore, 2008](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/d/dd/Pulau_Tekong_BMTC.JPG)

*An aerial view of the Basic Military Training Centre at Pulau Tekong, photographed from a passing aircraft in 2008. (Photo: Tunafish1990 / Wikimedia Commons, CC BY-SA 3.0)*

Pulau Tekong itself has its own history apart from BMT. Land reclamation between 1981 and 1985 added roughly 540 hectares to the island, and MINDEF formally acquired it for military use in 1986. Its last civilian residents — a community that had numbered around 8,000 at its peak, mostly Malay with some Teochew and Hakka families — left in 1987, and the island's two halves, Tekong Besar and Tekong Kechil, were merged in the mid-1990s as land was filled in between them. Some of the old kampong names, like Selabin, Permatang and Sanyongkong, survive today as the names of training areas on the very ground where those villages used to stand.

The men going through BMT today salute the same flag, sing the same pledge, and march to more or less the same drill I once did. But the building where they first report, the island where they train, and even the institute that once housed both are now three different places from the ones I knew — proof that an institution can hold its shape for nearly sixty years while almost nothing physical about it stays put.

**Why it matters today:** National Service is one of the few Singaporean institutions old enough to have completely outgrown its own original geography — CMPB has moved buildings, SAFTI split into a BMT centre on an island and an officer institute in Jurong, and the mainland training ground I once ran up and down is now, for most recruits, somewhere they've never set foot.
