On the 2nd of December 1941, two of the most powerful warships in the world came up the Johore Strait and anchored at Singapore, and the island thrilled to see them. They were the first battle fleet ever based there, and they had been sent halfway round the world for one purpose: to frighten Japan into leaving Malaya alone. Eight days later both ships were on the seabed off Kuantan, sunk by aircraft in under two hours, and 840 of their men were dead. No shot from Singapore's famous guns had been fired in their defence, because the guns faced the sea, and the blow had come from the air. This is the story of the deterrent that deterred nothing — and of the two wrecks, war graves both, that are still being fought over today.

The gesture came first, and it was a politician's gesture as much as a sailor's. In the autumn of 1941, with Japan pressing south through Indochina, Winston Churchill and his cabinet settled on sending a small, modern striking force to Singapore — a "fleet in being", whose mere presence, they reasoned, would make Tokyo hesitate. The force was to be built around the new battleship HMS *Prince of Wales*, the battlecruiser HMS *Repulse*, and the aircraft carrier HMS *Indomitable*, whose planes would be the fleet's eyes and its shield. *Prince of Wales* was barely a year old and already famous: in May 1941 she had fought the *Bismarck* in the action in which HMS *Hood* was lost, and in August she had carried Churchill across the Atlantic to meet President Roosevelt. *Repulse* was her opposite in almost everything — a ship of the First World War, launched in 1916, fast and handsome and thin-skinned, a greyhound among battleships.

![HMS Repulse under way in her prime](https://s3-ap-southeast-2.amazonaws.com/awm-media/collection/044777/screen/4086207.JPG)

*HMS Repulse at sea. A First World War battlecruiser — fast, elegant, and armoured for a war of guns, not of aircraft. (Australian War Memorial 044777, public domain)*

Then the plan lost its shield before it had even sailed. On the 3rd of November 1941, entering Kingston harbour in Jamaica on her way east, *Indomitable* ran aground. She needed twelve days of repairs in an American dry dock, and she never caught up. Force Z would go to Singapore with no carrier — a fact known in London, and accepted there, because the gesture was the point: the ships had been publicly announced, and their arrival was itself the message. Tokyo read the message and answered it in kind. When Churchill let it be known that the two capital ships were coming, Japan reinforced its air groups in Indochina with dozens more bombers, and trained their crews, for the first time in the war, specifically in killing fast ships under way at sea.

The arrival, when it came on the 2nd of December, was everything the gesture had promised. The fleet — *Prince of Wales*, *Repulse* and four destroyers — came up the eastern channel of the Johore Strait to the naval base at Sembawang, and General Percival, commanding the army in Malaya, never forgot it: "I can remember now the thrill it gave us all as we watched those majestic ships steaming up the eastern channel of the Johore Straits and coming to anchor at the Naval Base." Singapore's newspapers put the battleship on the front page. For a few days, the island felt protected.

![HMS Prince of Wales arriving at Singapore, December 1941](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/7/7f/HMS_PRINCE_OF_WALES_arrives_at_Singapore%2C_4_December_1941._A6784.jpg)

*HMS Prince of Wales arrives at Singapore, December 1941 — the first battle fleet ever based on the island, and the reassurance made visible. (Lt H. J. Abrahams, Royal Navy official photographer, public domain, via Wikimedia Commons)*

The man who took command of the fleet was Admiral Sir Tom Phillips, appointed Commander-in-Chief, Eastern Fleet, in the very days of the arrival. Phillips was an unlikely sea dog: a staff officer of genius, a "desk admiral" who had not held a major sea command in years, and a small man — the fleet called him Tom Thumb. He was also, by every account, brave, clever and utterly certain of his own judgement. He knew the two captains socially; Percival dined with all three men that week. Captain John Leach of *Prince of Wales* he remembered as "one of the finest of men"; Captain William Tennant of *Repulse* would prove, within days, to be one of the finest ship-handlers in the navy.

![Admiral Sir Tom Phillips and Rear-Admiral Arthur Palliser at Singapore Naval Base, December 1941](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/7/78/IWM_FE_487_Admirals_Phillips_and_Palliser.jpg)

*Admiral Sir Tom Phillips (right) with his chief of staff, Rear-Admiral Arthur Palliser, at the Singapore Naval Base in the fleet's few days ashore. Palliser stayed in Singapore when the fleet sailed; the false report that diverted Force Z on its last night passed through his hands. (Public domain, via Wikimedia Commons)*

War came to Singapore before the fleet could go looking for it. In the early hours of the 8th of December, local time, Japanese bombers raided the island and Japanese troops landed at Kota Bharu, in the north of Malaya; *Prince of Wales* and *Repulse* fired their anti-aircraft guns at the raiders from their berths. The news from Hawaii that day killed the last assumption the plan had rested on — that the American fleet might come to Singapore's aid. Phillips' two ships could not fight the Japanese navy, and everyone senior knew it. But Malaya was being invaded, the invasion convoys were at sea, and a fleet that stayed in harbour while the army fought alone was no deterrent at all. That evening, Force Z sailed: the two great ships and the destroyers *Electra*, *Express*, *Vampire* and *Tenedos*, steering north to strike the invasion shipping off Singora.

![HMS Repulse leaving Singapore, 8 December 1941](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/a/a5/HMS_Repulse_leaving_Singapore.jpg)

*HMS Repulse leaving Singapore on the evening of 8 December 1941, on Force Z's one and only operation. She had been in the Far East six days. (Capt. W. L. G. Adams, HMS Corinthian, public domain, via Wikimedia Commons)*

They sailed handicapped in ways that never made the communiqués. The flagship's anti-aircraft fire-control radars had failed in the tropical heat and humidity, and RAF technicians ashore said the repairs would take a week the fleet did not have; her two-pounder ammunition had deteriorated in the same climate. Phillips asked about fighter cover for the operation and was told it could not be guaranteed. He sailed anyway, in strict radio silence, counting on surprise and on weather — a curtain of rain and cloud under which a fleet might move unseen.

For a day, the curtain held. Then, at two o'clock on the afternoon of the 9th, the Japanese submarine *I-65* found them in a gap in the clouds, and shadowed them for five hours, reporting their position; towards sunset, Japanese cruiser floatplanes picked up the trail. Phillips did not know he had been seen — but he knew the arithmetic of surprise, and that evening he gave the operation up and turned for home. In the darkness the two fleets, his and the Japanese covering force, passed within about five miles of each other, neither seeing a thing.

Then, fifty minutes past midnight, came the message that killed them. A report reached Phillips — passed on from Singapore by Palliser — that the Japanese were landing at Kuantan, halfway down the coast towards home. It was exactly the target his force existed to strike, and he turned north to strike it, still in radio silence, telling no one ashore where he was going. The report was false. At dawn his destroyer *Express* went in and looked at Kuantan and found the beaches empty and the town asleep. On the way north, at twenty to four in the morning, the submarine *I-58* had fired five torpedoes at the force and missed with all of them; the British never even knew the attack had happened. At a quarter past ten that morning, a Japanese scout plane found the fleet in clear sunshine and broadcast its position to the bomber bases in Indochina. The bombers were already on their way.

What followed, from 11:13 on the morning of the 10th of December 1941, has been studied by navies ever since, because it changed what a warship was. The first wave — eight level bombers — attacked *Repulse* alone and put one bomb through her hangar into the marines' mess deck; remarkably, she shrugged it off and fought on at twenty-five knots. The torpedo bombers came next, in waves, the specially trained ship-killers of three air groups flying from bases hundreds of miles away. *Repulse*, superbly handled by Tennant, combed the tracks of nineteen torpedoes and dodged them all. *Prince of Wales* was not so lucky. One torpedo struck her aft, on the port side, exactly where the outer propeller shaft left the hull — the single most destructive hit of the day. The still-spinning shaft tore its watertight glands apart, the sea poured into engine and boiler rooms, and within minutes the great ship was listing, her steering and pumps and much of her electrical power gone. A Japanese officer watching from above recorded his astonishment at the empty sky: "Much to our surprise, not a single enemy plane was in sight. This was all the more amazing since the scene of battle was well within the fighting range of the British fighters."

![The first bombs fall on Repulse, 10 December 1941](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/a/a1/Japanese_high-level_bombing_attack_on_HMS_Prince_of_Wales_and_HMS_Repulse_on_10_December_1941_%28NH_60566%29.jpg)

*The attack, photographed from a Japanese bomber: Repulse below, with a bomb bursting close astern, and Prince of Wales above, making smoke. (Japanese official photograph, US Naval History and Heritage Command NH 60566, public domain, via Wikimedia Commons)*

The fighters were at Sembawang, ten Brewster Buffaloes of No. 453 Squadron, Royal Australian Air Force — the squadron designated to defend the fleet. Their commander, Flight Lieutenant Tim Vigors, had proposed keeping aircraft standing over the fleet in daylight; the proposal had been turned down. In the event, no call for help left the flagship at all: Phillips kept his radio silence to the end. It was Tennant, in *Repulse*, who finally broke silence and asked for the fighters — an hour after the first bombs. By then his ship was dying. At about twenty past twelve the last torpedo wave caught *Repulse* in a pincer, aircraft attacking from both sides at once so that dodging one spread of torpedoes turned her into the path of another; four or five struck home within minutes. Tennant ordered his men over the side, and at 12:33 *Repulse* rolled over and went down. *Prince of Wales*, hit by three more torpedoes and then bombed again — one bomb bursting among the wounded gathered in her cinema flat — held on a little longer, the destroyer *Express* alongside her taking off the wounded while the flagship heeled over so far that her bilge keel scraped down the destroyer's side and nearly dragged her under too. At 13:18, the flagship sank. At 13:18, at that very minute, the Buffaloes of 453 Squadron arrived over the oil and wreckage. Vigors flew low over the men in the water, and what he saw went into the official record: they "waved and put up his thumb as I flew over him… like holiday-makers at Brighton waving at a low-flying aircraft. It shook me, for here was something above human nature."

The destroyers did the rest. *Electra* and *Vampire* worked through *Repulse*'s men while *Express* took off the flagship's; *Electra* alone pulled 571 men out of the sea, and *Repulse*'s survivors — fished out of the oil half an hour before — manned *Electra*'s after guns so that her own sailors could stay at the rescue, while *Repulse*'s dentist worked beside *Electra*'s medical teams on the wounded. In all, 2,081 men were saved. Eight hundred and forty were not: 513 from *Repulse* and 327 from *Prince of Wales*, among them Captain Leach, who went down with his ship, and Admiral Phillips, who went down with his. Tennant survived, pulled from the water by *Vampire*, and when the survivors were landed in Singapore that night, the air commander, Air Vice-Marshal Pulford, met him with the words: "My God, I hope you don't blame me for this. I had no idea where you were." The next day, a Japanese pilot, Lieutenant Haruki Iki, flew back alone to the place where the ships had sunk and dropped two wreaths into the sea — one for his own dead of the Kanoya Air Group, and one for the British sailors.

The shock reached London by telephone, at the Prime Minister's bedside, from the First Sea Lord, Sir Dudley Pound: "Prime Minister, I have to report to you that the *Prince of Wales* and the *Repulse* have both been sunk by the Japanese — we think by aircraft. Tom Phillips is drowned." Churchill wrote afterwards: "In all the war, I never received a more direct shock… Across this vast expanse of waters, Japan was supreme, and we everywhere were weak and naked." The two ships were the first capital ships in history, steaming and fighting in the open sea, to be sunk by air power alone — eighty-eight Japanese aircraft had done it, for the loss of three of their own. Singapore, built and boasted of as a naval fortress, was now a land fortress with no fleet, which was the one thing it had never been meant to be. Nine weeks later, on the 15th of February 1942, it surrendered.

The ships are still there. Both wrecks lie upside down in the South China Sea off Kuantan — *Repulse* in 56 metres of water, *Prince of Wales* in 68 — and they are designated war graves, the property of the Crown, with buoys riding over them and White Ensigns on the buoy lines that Royal Navy divers renew. Every Royal Navy ship that passes holds a service of remembrance over the site. But a war grave made of tens of thousands of tons of pre-war steel — steel smelted before the first atomic bombs filled the air with background radiation, and therefore valuable for sensitive scientific and medical instruments — is a prize as well as a shrine. In 2002 an authorised team brought up *Prince of Wales*'s bell, ahead of the looters; it is displayed today at the Merseyside Maritime Museum in Liverpool. In 2014 the wrecks were reported extensively damaged by explosives used by scrap-metal dealers. And in May 2023 the Malaysian authorities detained a Fuzhou-registered salvage vessel anchored illegally off Johor, and found aboard her scrap metal and more than a hundred unexploded artillery shells, believed to have been scavenged from the two wrecks; thirty-two of her crew were detained. Britain's National Museum of the Royal Navy spoke for the dead: "We are distressed and concerned at the apparent vandalism for personal profit of HMS Prince of Wales and HMS Repulse. They are designated war graves."

![The memorial to the Prince of Wales and Repulse at the National Memorial Arboretum](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/e/e3/National_Memorial_to_the_Prince_of_Wales_and_Repulse.jpg)

*The memorial to the two ships at the National Memorial Arboretum, Alrewas, dedicated on the 70th anniversary of the sinking, 10 December 2011, in the presence of surviving crew members. There is a memorial park at Kuantan, near where the ships went down. (Steve Bowen (TenthEagle), CC BY-SA 4.0, via Wikimedia Commons)*

And the remembering goes on, in forms the men of 1941 could not have imagined. In September 2025 the Royal Navy's new aircraft carrier — named, by a coincidence the service does not treat as one, HMS *Prince of Wales* — stopped over the wrecks and held a committal of wreaths. Beside the British officers at the rail stood a young officer of the Japanese destroyer *Akebono*, Sub Lieutenant Takumi Kitamura, helping to honour the same dead: the navies of the two countries that fought over those ships, laying wreaths together in the same water. Eighty-four years after Singapore thrilled to a fleet that was meant to frighten an enemy away, the ships' real garrison turns out to be memory — and unlike the deterrent, it has held.
