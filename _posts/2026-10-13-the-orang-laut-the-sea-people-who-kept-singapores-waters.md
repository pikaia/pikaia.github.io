When Stamford Raffles and William Farquhar came ashore at the mouth of the Singapore River in January 1819, the people living in the boats moored along the bank were so frightened that they all fled. These were orang laut, the sea people, and by most accounts they made up the greater part of the island's thousand or so inhabitants. They had lived in these waters for centuries before the British arrived, and their story runs on long after, to families who were moved off Singapore's southern islands within living memory.

## People of the sea

Orang laut is Malay for "people of the sea". It is not the name of a single people but a broad term for the many communities who lived on boats, or in houses over the water, among the islands and estuaries of the Riau–Johor waters and the Straits of Malacca. The Portuguese called them Çelates, from the Malay selat, "strait". Each group, or suku, had its own home waters and its own speech, closely related to Malay.

Several of these groups lived in and around Singapore in 1819. The Orang Gelam lived around the Singapore River, and the Orang Biduanda Kallang lived in the mangrove swamps at the mouth of the Kallang River. The Orang Seletar roamed the mangroves and rivers of the island's northern shore and the strait between Singapore and Johor. The Orang Selat, the people of the straits, are associated with the southern islands. Raffles recorded about a thousand inhabitants in all, about 200 of them Orang Seletar, and the Biduanda Kallang are thought to have been the largest group.

## Keepers of the straits

<div style="float: right; max-width: 300px; width: 42%; margin: 0.25em 0 1em 1.5em;">
<img src="https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/0/08/Prasasti_Pasir_Panjang.jpg/1280px-Prasasti_Pasir_Panjang.jpg" alt="A flat rock face under a shelter, covered with faint carved lines of large letters" style="width: 100%; display: block; border-radius: 4px;">
<em style="display: block; font-size: 0.8em; margin-top: 0.5em;">The Pasir Panjang inscription on Karimun island, carved in the 9th or 10th century, which may record a chief of the sea people. (Si Gam, CC BY-SA 4.0, via Wikimedia Commons)</em>
</div>

For hundreds of years the sea people were the naval strength of the Malay kingdoms. Both Portuguese and Malay sources say that when Parameswara, the last ruler of Singapura, fled to the island in the 1390s, one of his main sources of support was a group of sea people. According to the Portuguese writer Tomé Pires, who wrote in Malacca in the 1510s, it was sea people who found the site of Malacca and invited him to move there, and the great offices of Bendahara and Laksamana, the chief minister and the admiral, were held by their descendants. Pires found only a few of their villages around Singapore in 1511 and wrote that the channel was "nothing much". The sea people who had come with Parameswara settled not on Singapore but on Karimun, an island at the southern end of the Straits of Malacca, where a cliff carries a Sanskrit inscription of the 9th or 10th century that may be the boast of a sea people's chief.

After Malacca fell to the Portuguese, the sea people served the sultans of Johor. Different suku had different duties. Some rowed and fought in the royal fleets, some carried envoys and letters to foreign rulers, some were blacksmiths who made swords and spears, and others gathered sea produce, sago and agar-agar, or carried water and firewood for the royal kitchens. In return their leaders received titles and gifts. In the 17th century the chief of the sea people of Singapore held the title Raja Negara Selat, king of the country of the straits.

The bond was with the royal line itself. In 1699 Sultan Mahmud Shah II of Johor, the last ruler who could claim descent from the founder of Singapura, was assassinated, and the sea people's loyalty to the new rulers never ran as deep. Over the 18th century they became scattered, and the Bugis took over much of the role they had played.

## When the British came

<div style="float: left; max-width: 300px; width: 42%; margin: 0.25em 1.5em 1em 0;">
<img src="https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/8/81/COLLECTIE_TROPENMUSEUM_Woonschuit_van_een_Oerang-Laoet_familie_Ka._Toengkal_TMnr_10010488.jpg" alt="A black and white photograph of a long, narrow wooden boat with a low thatched shelter, a man sitting in it among baskets" style="width: 100%; display: block; border-radius: 4px;">
<em style="display: block; font-size: 0.8em; margin-top: 0.5em;">A family houseboat of the sea people in the Riau–Jambi waters, photographed in the 1910s or 1920s. Boats like this were home to a whole family. (Unknown photographer, Tropenmuseum, CC BY-SA 3.0, via Wikimedia Commons)</em>
</div>

The early accounts of the sea people were written by outsiders, and not kindly. Munshi Abdullah, the Malay scribe who came to Singapore in 1819, wrote about them with open contempt. The historian Charles Buckley, writing in 1902, described them as people who were "born, lived and died in boats", a whole family often living in a single craft. Abdullah also recorded that the Orang Gelam paid respect to a rock near the mouth of the Singapore River that was shaped like the head of a garfish.

The British town soon pushed them aside. Soon after 1819 the Orang Gelam moved from the river to Pulau Brani, an island off the south coast. As shipping grew on the Singapore River, many families moved from the river to the Kallang between 1823 and 1843, and some built a settlement at Tanjong Rhu. In the 1820s the Temenggong, the Malay chief of the island, resettled most of the Orang Biduanda Kallang on the Pulai River in Johor, and there disaster followed. A smallpox epidemic around 1847 and 1848 reduced about a hundred families to eight. Descendants say a few families stayed behind on the Kallang into the 1930s, and the river is generally thought to take its name from them.

![A photograph of a waterfront village of wooden houses on stilts, with boats drawn up in the shallows in front](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/4/4f/Kampong_in_Singapore%2C_gezien_vanaf_het_water_Campong_Bagoes_Singapore_%28titel_op_object%29%2C_RP-F-00-5018-41.jpg/1280px-Kampong_in_Singapore%2C_gezien_vanaf_het_water_Campong_Bagoes_Singapore_%28titel_op_object%29%2C_RP-F-00-5018-41.jpg)

*A Malay kampong on stilts by the water in Singapore, seen from the sea, about 1890 to 1910. (Rijksmuseum, CC0, via Wikimedia Commons)*

The Orang Seletar kept to the northern shore. In the early years after independence some still lived at the mouths of the Kranji and Kadut rivers and on Seletar Island. As those areas were developed, some families moved across the strait to Johor and others merged into the wider Malay community. After separation in 1965 the Orang Seletar who had become Malaysian citizens could still cross the strait to fish in their old waters until 1987, when that ended after an arrest for alleged smuggling. Today there are nine Orang Seletar villages on the southern coast of Johor, where they fish and farm mussels, and the reservoir, river and aerospace park named Seletar are the main traces of them left in Singapore.

![A green wooden house on stilts over the water, reached by a long plank walkway, with a small boat moored alongside](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/1/1e/Kampung_Orang_Asal_Seletar%2C_Pasir_Gudang_%2811450591134%29.jpg/1280px-Kampung_Orang_Asal_Seletar%2C_Pasir_Gudang_%2811450591134%29.jpg)

*A house in the Orang Seletar village at Pasir Gudang, on the Johor side of the strait, in 2013. (Emran Kassim, CC BY 2.0, via Wikimedia Commons)*

## The island people

<div style="float: right; max-width: 300px; width: 42%; margin: 0.25em 0 1em 1.5em;">
<img src="https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/a/ab/COLLECTIE_TROPENMUSEUM_Woonschuiten_van_de_Orang-Laoets_langs_de_kusten_van_Djambi_en_Riouw_varende_TMnr_10010489.jpg" alt="A black and white photograph of a small boat under sail on calm water, with a dark line of mangroves behind" style="width: 100%; display: block; border-radius: 4px;">
<em style="display: block; font-size: 0.8em; margin-top: 0.5em;">Houseboats of the sea people under sail off the coasts of Jambi and Riau, in the 1910s or 1920s. (Unknown photographer, Tropenmuseum, CC BY-SA 3.0, via Wikimedia Commons)</em>
</div>

Until the late 20th century, many of Singapore's southern islands had villages of their own. Their people called themselves orang pulau, the island people. Some had roots among the sea people, and many had relatives across the water in the Riau Islands. They fished the reefs, built their houses on stilts over the water, and sold their catch on the mainland.

![A long, low wooded island on the horizon across a calm blue sea](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/a/af/Pulau_Sudong_June_2026.jpg/1280px-Pulau_Sudong_June_2026.jpg)

*Pulau Sudong, one of the southern islands, in 2026. Like Semakau and Seking, it once had a village of island people. (ZKang123, CC BY-SA 4.0, via Wikimedia Commons)*

Pulau Semakau, about 8 kilometres south of the mainland, had a Malay village and a small Chinese one, with a school, a mosque, a community centre and a football field. About 600 people lived there. On 29 July 1975 Parliament approved a plan to reclaim the island for a petrochemical complex, and most of the islanders were moved to the mainland by 1977, many of them to flats at Telok Blangah and Bukit Merah. Neighbouring Pulau Seking held out longest. It was the last village community on the southern islands, and the last families left on 24 April 1994. Reclamation then joined the two islands, and the Semakau Landfill, which takes most of the ash from Singapore's incineration plants, opened there in 1999.

![A large modern white building standing at the water's edge, its wide doorway facing the sea](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/c/c9/Pulau_Semakau_32%2C_Nov_06.JPG/1280px-Pulau_Semakau_32%2C_Nov_06.JPG)

*The landfill's receiving station on Pulau Semakau in 2006, built over what had been Pulau Seking. (Sengkang, free use, via Wikimedia Commons)*

Former islanders have spoken about what the move meant. "Life on the island may seem tough," Rohani Rani, who grew up on Pulau Semakau, told an interviewer in 2020. "Yes, we were poor and our life wasn't grand. But my heart was always at ease." Teo Yen Teck, who ran the only Chinese shop on Pulau Seking with his brother and was among the last to leave, put it more simply: "On the island, we were more free."

<div style="clear: both;"></div>

## Remembering the sea people

The descendants have begun to tell the story themselves. Orang Laut SG, started in 2020 by Firdaus Sani, a fourth-generation descendant of the Pulau Semakau islanders, records the memories of former islanders, gathers families for reunions, and sells the island food his family grew up on. The Orang Seletar of Johor have welcomed researchers and writers, and in 2019 a group of them visited Singapore for a play based on their interviews and went to see the places their ancestors had known. The National Library and the National Heritage Board have published accounts of the sea people, and some of the old island communities are remembered in exhibitions and oral history projects.

**Why it matters today:** Singapore's story is usually told as one of migrants who arrived by sea and built a city, but the people who were here first were themselves people of the sea. For centuries they guarded the straits for Singapura, Melaka and Johor, and they were the majority of the island's people when the British came. Within two generations of 1819 most of them had moved away, died out or been absorbed, and the last island villages went in the 1970s, 1980s and 1990s as the southern islands were put to new uses. Much of what is known about them today comes from the families who remember, and the efforts now under way to record those memories are some of the few ways left to keep the island's oldest story from being lost.
