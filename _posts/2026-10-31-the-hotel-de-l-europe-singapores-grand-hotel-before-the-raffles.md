In 1889, a young Rudyard Kipling passed through Singapore and left its hotels with a piece of advice that travellers repeated for decades: "Feed at Raffles and sleep at the Hotel de l'Europe." The Raffles Hotel's managers liked the first half so much that they quietly borrowed it for their own advertising, trimmed to just "Feed at Raffles". The second half pointed across the Padang to the Raffles' great rival — a hotel that was, for much of the nineteenth century, the grandest address in Singapore, and that today has vanished so completely that most people walk over its site without knowing it was ever there.

The site is easy to find. Stand in front of the former Supreme Court building, now part of National Gallery Singapore, on St Andrew's Road facing the Padang. The domed building that houses the Gallery's Supreme Court wing stands where the Hotel de l'Europe stood — the hotel's neighbour in its lifetime, the Municipal Building, is the Gallery's other half, the former City Hall. The hotel faced the Esplanade and the open green of the Padang, in the company of St Andrew's Cathedral a short distance along the road. It was the best seat in the civic district, and the hotel held it for nearly seventy years.

## A hotel that arrived in stages

The Hotel de l'Europe did not begin on that grand site, and it did not begin grandly. Its story on the Esplanade starts with somebody else's hotel. In 1839 the French showman Gaston Dutronquoy established the London Hotel, which by the mid-1840s stood at the corner of High Street and the Esplanade. Dutronquoy was a character in his own right — among other ventures, he ran Singapore's first advertised commercial photographic studio out of the hotel in 1843. After he left Singapore, a Madame Esperanza took the place over in 1858 and renamed it the Hotel de l'Esperance — the Hotel of Hope.

The Europe, meanwhile, began elsewhere. In 1857 another Frenchman, J. Casteleyns, started a modest establishment called the Hotel d'Europe on Hill Street. It moved to Beach Road in 1860, and in 1865 Casteleyns took over the Hotel de l'Esperance premises on the Esplanade and gave them his own hotel's name. The Hotel de l'Europe had arrived on the Padang.

![The Hotel de l'Europe before the rebuild, a long two-storey block with verandahs and a portico, behind a lawn and trees](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/2/2a/KITLV_-_29176_-_Lambert_%26_Co.%2C_G.R._-_Singapore_-_Hotel_de_l%27Europe_at_Singapore_-_1897.tif/lossy-page1-1280px-KITLV_-_29176_-_Lambert_%26_Co.%2C_G.R._-_Singapore_-_Hotel_de_l%27Europe_at_Singapore_-_1897.tif.jpg)

*The old Hotel de l'Europe in 1897, a decade before the rebuild. (G. R. Lambert and Co., public domain, via Wikimedia Commons)*

For its first decades it was comfortable rather than magnificent — about thirty rooms at the start, a cluster of buildings between High Street and Coleman Street with separate quarters for bachelors and families. A visitor in the early 1870s found an American bar-room, a billiard room, and a reading room stocked with newspapers and journals in five languages, from New York, London, Calcutta, Shanghai and Yokohama — a reminder that Singapore's travellers were already citizens of a much wider world. Under proprietors C. A. Hjelmstrom and A. Becker from 1871, and then Becker alone, the hotel grew in standing until, by the 1880s, it was described as the largest and best-known hotel in Singapore, and one of the finest in Asia.

## The colonials' hotel

The Raffles Hotel opened in 1887, and Singapore settled into a two-hotel town with a clear social geography. A saying of the 1920s put it neatly: tourists stayed at the Raffles, while European colonials stayed at the Grand Hotel de l'Europe. The Raffles had the glamour and the travellers; the Europe had the residents — the merchants, officials and planters for whom it was less a hotel than a club with bedrooms.

At some point in those years the hotel was owned by the Alkaff family, the Hadhrami Arab merchants from Tarim, in what is now Yemen, who had arrived in Singapore in 1852 and built one of the town's great property fortunes — the Alkaff Arcade, Alkaff Mansion and the Alkaff Lake Gardens all carried their name. The National Archives records that the Alkaffs owned the hotel at one time before it was sold in the early 1900s, and the historian Maurizio Peleggi places their ownership before the hotel's redevelopment. The records do not give exact years, and the Alkaffs were investors rather than managers — the ground itself belonged to the Catholic Portuguese Mission, which held the freehold throughout the hotel's life. Ownership, lease and day-to-day management were three different things, held by three different parties, which is part of why the hotel's paper trail is so tangled.

By the turn of the century the old buildings were showing their age. In 1904 the Straits Times called the hotel a "barn-like erection jutting into the road-way", an eyesore on the town's finest frontage — even if its bar and billiard room still drew the eye. Something grander was clearly called for.

## Adis and the million-dollar rebuild

The rebuild began as someone else's project. In 1903 a syndicate formed by Sam Tomlinson, a former municipal engineer, and Chye Tian Fook acquired the hotel for redevelopment, and demolition and rebuilding started in 1904, with the hotel partly occupied while the work went on around it. In November 1905 the financing changed hands: Nissim Nassim Adis, one of Singapore's richest self-made men, signed an agreement with the builders and took over responsibility for the cost of construction himself.

Adis's own story is worth a pause. Born in Howrah, India, in 1857, the son of a Calcutta merchant, he was articled to attorneys, then went into exchange and stockbroking — and lost a fortune, some 300,000 rupees, in a Calcutta financial crisis. A second venture in Hong Kong in 1888 crashed as well. He arrived in Singapore in June 1893 with very little, and rebuilt everything: as head of Adis & Co he made his fortune in stockbroking and real estate, built himself Adis Lodge on Mount Sophia — Adis Road still carries his name — and became the first Jew to serve as Master of the Lodge of St Andrew in the Far East. The rebuilt hotel was so identified with him that people called it the Adis Building.

What he paid for was built to impress. Part of the new hotel opened in July 1906, and on 7 August 1907 Adis took possession of the completed premises. The Grand Hotel de l'Europe, as it now styled itself, was a Renaissance-style palace of nearly an acre: two-storey-high Corinthian columns, detached pavilions for dining and recreation, wide verandahs fitted with punkahs, and a ground-floor colonnade of segmental arches between massive rusticated piers, floored with encaustic tiles. Inside were 120 bedrooms, each with its own balcony, dressing room and bathroom — a standard of private comfort few Asian hotels could match — lit by electricity, cooled by electric fans as well as punkahs, and served by a lift. The hotel kept its own horse-drawn carriages to meet ships at Tanjong Pagar. Built and furnished, the whole thing cost a million dollars.

Its dining room was the famous room: nicknamed the "Bird Cage", about a thousand square feet and almost free of columns, advertised as having "the finest view east of Suez". There was a roof garden, and from 1920 a ladies' lounge and tea room. Dinner was accompanied by serious music — the pianist Claude Oliveiro, who played in the hotel's orchestra in its last years, remembered programmes of opera, German waltzes and French waltzes for the diners. A daily rate in 1924, covering room, bath and food, started at twelve Straits dollars.

## Fortress, ballroom, and the long goodbye

The Europe's most dramatic hour came on 15 February 1915, when the sepoys of the 5th Light Infantry mutinied in Singapore. With shooting in the streets, the hotel doubled as a fortress and a sanctuary: women and children took refuge inside it while the revolt was put down. A few days later, crowds were back on its verandahs, watching the parades on the Padang — the hotel resuming its usual role as the grandstand of the civic district.

The end, when it came, was slow and then sudden. Through the late 1920s the hotel fell behind the times and into financial difficulty: the plant was ageing, and the terms of its lease carried an obligation to rebuild all over again. A redevelopment modelled on Hong Kong's new Peninsula Hotel was planned and then abandoned as the Depression bit. From 1913 the hotel had been run by The Europe Hotel Ltd — the "Grand" was dropped from its name by the late 1910s, though some records date the renaming to 1918. In October 1932 the company went into liquidation, and on 30 October 1932 the Straits Times carried the headline that closed the story: "The Europe Hotel Closes."

It had been dancing almost to the last. Five months earlier, in May 1932, the hotel was still advertising a special dinner and dance every Saturday night, an orchestra playing light music on the verandah and during meals every day, and an orchestral concert every Sunday. The building stood empty for a while as hopes of a new modern hotel on the site were raised and failed. The Catholic Portuguese Mission, the landowner, decided on demolition and a block of service flats; the demolition was under way by late 1933. Then the government stepped in, acquired the site — the purchase was completed in 1935 — and chose it for the new Supreme Court.

The Supreme Court building, designed by Frank Dorrington Ward and modelled in part on London's Old Bailey, rose on the hotel's footprint: foundation stone on 1 April 1937, declared open on 3 August 1939. It was the last great classical building of colonial Singapore, home to the Supreme Court until 2005, a National Monument from 1992, and since 24 November 2015 the former Supreme Court wing of National Gallery Singapore.

So the Europe never really left the Padang. The traveller who follows Kipling's advice today can still feed at Raffles — but to sleep at the Hotel de l'Europe, you would have to sleep in an art gallery, on the exact ground where the colonials took their dinners to the sound of waltzes, in the finest view east of Suez.

**Where it fits in the bigger story:** For half a century the Hotel de l'Europe was the other pole of Singapore's social world — the residents' hotel against the travellers' Raffles — and its site has been a seat of power ever since, first of hospitality, then of law, now of art. Its million-dollar rebuild also belongs to the generation of self-made fortunes, Adis's and the Alkaffs', that built so much of the civic district and then vanished with the world that made them.
