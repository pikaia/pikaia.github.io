At the corner of Handy Road and Dhoby Ghaut, a cream Art Deco tower with the word CATHAY running down its face still stands in front of a glass-walled shopping mall. Most people walking under it are on their way to a movie or a meal and have no idea that the mall behind it isn't the original building at all — the tower is the only piece of it left, and it's technically a national monument even though the building it belonged to was torn down.

*The Cathay Building, photographed around 1945. (Photo: Australian War Memorial, public domain, via Wikimedia Commons)*

The building was privately financed, not built by the colonial government: Dato Loke Wan Tho and his mother put up roughly S$1 million of the family's tin and rubber fortune to build it, with construction starting on 11 July 1939 to a design by the British architect Frank W. Brewer. When the Cathay Cinema opened that October, it was the tallest building in Singapore and in South-East Asia, easily clearing the Supreme Court next door, and it stayed the region's tallest until 1954. It was also the island's first fully air-conditioned public building — armchair seating and cool air were novel enough that people came just to experience the cinema itself. The complex grew to include a hotel, serviced apartments, and a broadcasting studio, and the Loke family kept a private penthouse on top with a view over the Straits of Johor. Before the war reached Singapore, the British military had already requisitioned much of the building for its height, installing a radar station to coordinate air defence between Singapore's airbases and housing the Far Eastern Bureau of the Ministry of Economic Warfare.

That height is exactly what made the building useful to two different armies in quick succession. On 15 February 1942, as Singapore's fall became unavoidable, the British were instructed to fly a Japanese flag and a white flag from the top of the Cathay Building for ten minutes — a signal visible across the city, from the tallest structure in it. The Japanese renamed the building Daitoa Gekijo ("Greater East Asian Theatre") and ran Radio Syonan's propaganda broadcasts from inside it for the rest of the Occupation. In October 1943, Subhas Chandra Bose used the building to proclaim the Provisional Government of Free India, calling on Indians to "strike for India's freedom" from the same tower that had signalled Singapore's surrender eighteen months earlier. When Japan itself surrendered in 1945, the building changed hands one more time: Admiral Lord Louis Mountbatten set up his South East Asia Command headquarters there.

Civilian life came back slowly. The Cathay Restaurant reopened in 1948, the complex became a proper hotel in 1954 — the same year the neon-lit nights above are from — and stayed one until 1970, after which most of the building turned into offices. A 1978 renovation covered over much of the original Art Deco detailing, and a 1990 extension added an arthouse cinema, The Picturehouse. By 1999 the Cathay Organisation had decided the ageing complex needed a full rebuild rather than another patch-up, and the original cinema closed for the last time in 2000. Demolition began in 2003 — and that same year, the building was gazetted a National Monument, one of the first in Singapore to be protected under a new scheme that preserves only a building's historic facade rather than the whole structure. Everything behind that Art Deco face on Handy Road today, including the condominium tower above it, is new construction that opened as The Cathay in 2006.

**Why it matters today:** most Singaporeans have walked past that CATHAY sign without knowing it once flew a surrender flag, broadcast Japanese propaganda, and hosted the birth of a foreign independence movement — all a decade before it even became a hotel. The tower's monument status is itself an admission of how thin the line got: Singapore didn't save the building, only its face, and decided that was worth protecting anyway.
