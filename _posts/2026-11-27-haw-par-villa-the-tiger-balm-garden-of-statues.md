On a hill above Pasir Panjang Road stands the strangest garden in Singapore: a terraced hillside crowded with painted concrete statues — gods and demons, monkeys and mermaids, sinners being sawn in half — built by the Tiger Balm fortune as part gift, part advertisement and part moral textbook. It has outlived the mansion it was built around, the company town of theme parks that tried to replace it, and the family that made it. Today it waits behind a partial closure while the state decides what to do with it. This is the garden's own story; the brothers who paid for it, Aw Boon Haw and Aw Boon Par, have [their own post on this blog](/2026/09/02/aw-brothers-tiger-balm-fortune/).

The founding can be told briefly, because the brothers' post tells it in full. In 1935 Aw Boon Haw bought a seafront hill on Pasir Panjang Road, on the rural south-western coast. There the architect Ho Kwang Yew built an Art Deco villa, finished in 1937, which Boon Haw gave to his younger brother Boon Par. The name Haw Par joins the brothers' two names; in Chinese the place is the Tiger Leopard Villa. It was the second Tiger Balm Garden — Boon Haw had built the first behind a house in Hong Kong in 1932 — and like the first, its garden was opened to the public free of charge. Boon Haw made no secret of why. The National Library Board records that he wanted the garden to be an advertisement for Tiger Balm products. It was also, quite deliberately, a school: its statues were meant to teach Chinese cultural values — filial piety, charity, loyalty, resistance to temptation, and what awaited the wicked after death.

## A garden in four tiers

The Singapore garden climbed its hill in four terraces, where the Hong Kong garden had been built up a single steep plane. At ground level came the garage and parking. The second tier held a large fish pond that served as the family's aquarium, stocked with fish and tortoises. The third was the family's own pleasure ground — a lawn, tennis courts and a swimming pool. On the fourth and uppermost tier stood the villa itself, with its gold-gilded domes, looking out over the Singapore Strait.

The garden of 1937 was, by the National Library Board's account, a simple one — some rock structures among gateways, ponds and pavilions in the Chinese manner. There was even a small zoo at first: animals roamed freely, were later caged, and were finally removed altogether — snakes were said to have escaped — and replaced by animal statues, which were easier to keep. The pagoda that still rises over the grounds was originally fifteen storeys tall; Boon Haw later had its top storeys taken down and replaced with a statue of Sakyamuni, leaving the four storeys that stand today.

## The men who made the statues

The statues were made by craftsmen brought from Swatow, in Guangdong, for their knowledge of Chinese folklore. The National Library Board names the first of them: Kwek Hoon Sua, who had worked on the Hong Kong garden and brought over his brother Kwek Choon Sua and a team of apprentices. The Singapore team also included a father and son, Tan See Hua and Tan Seng Hua, and a Mr Liu.

There were no blueprints. Boon Haw directed the work in person, walking the grounds in early-morning rounds of about two hours on his visits every few months, inspecting what the sculptors had made and ordering changes — down, the National Library Board notes, to the colours used. The garden was in that sense drawn at full scale, in concrete, by a man selling ointment: every tableau an advertisement's argument that this was a family firm rooted in Chinese tradition, and every moral a lesson the visitor was meant to carry home along with the name of the balm.

## What the statues taught

The exhibits mixed Buddhist, Taoist and Confucian material with Chinese history and myth. There were the Virtues and Vices tableaux, a lane of moral tales in painted concrete. There was Journey to the West, the Monkey King's story; the Eight Immortals Crossing the Sea; Monkey Mountain. Sculpted plants and animals carried their own meanings — the lotus for purity, the carp for success. The point of it all was instruction by spectacle: virtue and vice set side by side, larger than life, in a garden anyone could walk through free.

## War, and the garden that grew from the ruins

Boon Par enjoyed his villa for only a few years. On the eve of the Japanese Occupation in 1942 he left for Rangoon, and he died there in 1944. The compound suffered extensive damage during the war — the villa was bombed — and after the war the ruined mansion was demolished. No official account gives the demolition a date, and nothing was built in the house's place; the hilltop simply became part of the garden.

What Boon Haw did next is the making of the place as Singapore knows it. Returning from Hong Kong, he rebuilt the garden and greatly enlarged it, adding sculptures in the spaces that had been the family's recreation ground — over the swimming pool, the tennis courts and the lawns. Most of the famous statuary dates from this post-war rebuilding. So, above all, do the Ten Courts of Hell.

## The Ten Courts of Hell

The Courts are a walk-through of the underworld of Chinese Buddhist and Taoist belief, in which the dead are judged court by court by ten Yama kings, each presiding over punishments fitted to particular sins, before their next reincarnation is decided. The idea underneath the gore is cause and effect — one reaps what one sows — and the garden's makers meant it as the moral core of the whole hillside.

The entrance is guarded by Ox-Head and Horse-Face, the escorts of the dead. In the First Court, King Qinguang conducts the preliminary trial. The virtuous cross the Golden Bridge to paradise; those whose good deeds outweigh their sins cross the Silver Bridge; the wicked are shown their own faces in the Mirror of Retribution and sent on to the courts below. The punishments, set out on the courts' own signage, are specific. In the Second Court, those who inflicted injury, and conmen and robbers, are thrown into a volcanic pit; the corrupt, thieves and gamblers are frozen in blocks of ice. In the Third Court, the ungrateful and those who disrespected their elders have their hearts cut out, while drug traffickers and tomb robbers are tied to a red-hot copper pillar and grilled. In the Fifth Court, those who plotted another's death for his property, and moneylenders who charged exorbitant interest, are thrown onto a hill of knives.

Generations of Singaporean schoolchildren were walked through the Courts on school trips and came out the other side promising to be good — which was, of course, the point. The Courts' graphic reputation only grew with retelling, and the garden collected the usual folklore of such places, including the legend that the statues come alive at night.

## Nephews, replicas and a million visitors a year

Boon Haw died in 1954, and the garden passed to his nephew Aw Cheng Chye. Cheng Chye added the International Corners — sculptures representing the cultures of the world, among them a sumo wrestler, a Thai dancer and a replica of the Statue of Liberty — together with replicas of Tiger Brand products, the family's goods joining the gods. After his death in 1971 the family made no major changes, and the garden settled into its long peak as one of Singapore's great free attractions: in the 1970s and 1980s it drew, by the Straits Times figure commonly cited, at least a million visitors a year.

## The state, the dragon, and the free park again

In 1985 the government acquired the grounds under the Land Acquisition Act. The Aw family donated the statues to the nation, on one condition: that the Haw Par name and the family memorials in the grounds be retained. The Singapore Tourist Promotion Board took over the management, and in 1988 leased the park to International Theme Parks Ltd to develop as a theme park.

The result, reopened in 1990, was Haw Par Villa Dragon World — an S$80-million theme park with an amphitheatre, theatres, and the Ten Courts of Hell rebuilt as a boat ride through the inside of a 60-metre-long dragon. It charged admission, and it lost money heavily. In 2001 the operator gave up its rights, the board took the park back, and Haw Par Villa became a free public park again, as it had been in the family's day. The dragon was demolished, and the Courts went back behind stone walls. A museum of the Chinese diaspora, the Hua Song Museum, occupied part of the park from 2005 until it closed in 2012; in 2014 the tourist board marked its own fiftieth anniversary with a festival in the grounds that drew more than 12,000 people over two weekends.

## The museum of death, and a park in limbo

In 2015 the tourist board appointed Journeys Pte Ltd, a heritage operator, to run the park. Journeys closed it in October 2020 for extensive works — conservation of the sculptures including the entrance arch, new night lighting, and the Ten Courts of Hell made fully air-conditioned — and the park reopened on 1 July 2021. That October came the boldest addition since Boon Haw's day: Hell's Museum, billed as the world's first museum dedicated to death and the afterlife — 3,800 square metres on how the world's religions and cultures treat death, with replicas of an altar, a burial crypt, a traditional Chinese grave and a void-deck funeral, wrapped around the Ten Courts, which now sit inside it. The park had by then acquired one more distinction: the Circle Line station beside its gate, opened in 2011, is named Haw Par Villa — the garden had become a place on the map in its own right.

![The entrance to the Ten Courts of Hell today, guarded by Ox-Head and Horse-Face, now the heart of Hell's Museum](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/4/45/Entrance_to_10_Courts_of_Hell.jpg)

*The cave-mouth entrance to the Ten Courts of Hell, guarded by Ox-Head (left) and Horse-Face. Since 2021 the Courts have formed the centrepiece of Hell's Museum. (Jameslee 1993, CC BY-SA 4.0, via Wikimedia Commons)*

And there the story pauses. Journeys' contract ended on 7 December 2025, and from the next day the Singapore Tourism Board closed the park partially, for essential maintenance and repair works, until further notice — saying the works were needed to ensure public safety while preserving the park's cultural and artistic elements, and that it was reviewing the future plans for Haw Par Villa. No reopening date has been announced. The park, unusually for a place so loved, is not gazetted as a national monument. What remains open is Hell's Museum, run by an associate company of the former operator: open daily from 10 in the morning to 6, at S$22 for an adult and S$12 for a child, the Ten Courts at its heart. The Halloween event the museum spawned, Haw Ror Villa, even returned in the autumn of 2026 for a fifth edition, staged in and around the museum while the grounds beyond stayed shut.

**The villa is gone, the ointment empire is gone, and the garden has now outlived its makers, its theme park and its managers — a hillside of painted lessons, built to sell balm, waiting behind its fence for Singapore to decide what it is for.**
