Cross the junction where Orchard Road meets Stamford Road, opposite the National Museum of Singapore, and there is a YMCA building with a red logo on its tower and buses idling outside. Almost nobody waiting there knows that the building on this exact spot was once the Kempeitai's East District Branch, and that a schoolteacher named Elizabeth Choy spent roughly 200 days in a cell in it, tortured for information she never gave up.

## Before the war

Elizabeth Choy was born Yong Su-Moi on 29 November 1910 in Kudat, in British North Borneo, now Sabah. Her father was a civil servant who rose to District Officer, and she was raised in her early years by a Kadazan nanny, picking up Kadazan as her first language. She was sent to her paternal grandfather's village school in Kudat, then to St Monica's, an Anglican boarding school in Sandakan, where a teacher who could not pronounce her Chinese name gave her the English name Elizabeth.

<div style="float: left; max-width: 260px; margin: 0.3em 1.5em 1em 0;">
  <img src="https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/0/03/Elizabeth_Choy%2C_1941.jpg" alt="Elizabeth Choy in her wedding veil and dress, 1941">
  <em>Elizabeth Choy on her wedding day, 16 August 1941. (Family of Elizabeth Choy, public domain, via Wikimedia Commons.)</em>
</div>

In December 1929 she came to Singapore to study at the Convent of the Holy Infant Jesus, lodging with an uncle who ran a music shop on Selegie Road. Her mother died in 1931, and with the Great Depression squeezing the family, Choy gave up a shot at a college scholarship and went to work instead, to help raise her six younger siblings. She began teaching at the Church of England Zenana Mission School (today's St Margaret's School) in 1933, then transferred to St Andrew's School in 1935, by her own account probably the only untrained teacher on the staff at the time. On 16 August 1941 she married Choy Khun Heng, a bookkeeper at the Borneo Company, in a double wedding held together with her brother's.

## The canteen

Singapore fell to Japan in February 1942. Choy and her husband found work running a canteen at the Mental Hospital, renamed Miyako Hospital under the occupation, which had taken in patients transferred from the General Hospital. It gave them a reason to move around the city, and they used it: the Choys secretly carried food, medicine, money, letters and, at real risk to themselves, radio parts to British civilian internees and prisoners of war held at Changi.

![Changi Prison photographed shortly after it was built in 1936, its perimeter wall and watchtowers visible](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/3/31/Changi_Prison_in_1936.jpg/1280px-Changi_Prison_in_1936.jpg)

*Changi Prison, photographed in 1936, a few years before it held the men the Choys were risking their lives to help. (The Straits Times, public domain, via Wikimedia Commons.)*

The end of their smuggling operation traces back to Operation Jaywick, a September 1943 raid in which Allied commandos slipped into Singapore Harbour and sank several Japanese ships. The Kempeitai, unable to believe the raid had been mounted from outside Singapore, concluded it was the work of local sympathisers, and on 10 October 1943 — "Double Tenth" — began a wave of arrests and interrogations across the civilian and internee population. Fifty-seven people were eventually detained; fifteen died under interrogation or in custody. An informant told the Kempeitai the Choys had been smuggling money into Changi. Her husband was arrested on 29 October. Choy went to the Kempeitai's headquarters to ask after him; she was told they knew nothing, then lured back three weeks later and held herself, arrested on 15 November 1943.

## Two hundred days

Choy was interrogated at the Kempeitai's East District Branch, in the old YMCA building on the site described above, and never admitted to being a British sympathiser or named anyone who had helped her. She was starved and repeatedly tortured over roughly 200 days. R. H. Scott, a wartime director at the British Ministry of Information who later testified as a principal witness at Singapore's war crimes trials, said he had seen her stripped and severely beaten on at least one occasion. She credited her Christian faith, and the moral texts she had learned as a schoolgirl, with keeping her intact. Her husband was released some time after her; both survived the war.

## Liberation and England

At the Japanese surrender in September 1945, Lady Mountbatten personally invited Choy to witness the ceremony, and she was escorted to it by the governor, Sir Shenton Thomas, and his wife — the same Lady Thomas to whom Choy had once sent medicine inside Changi.

![Japanese officers being escorted up the steps of the Municipal Building in Singapore for the surrender ceremony, September 1945, with Allied troops and officials lining the route](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/b/ba/Signing_of_the_Japanese_Surrender_at_Singapore%2C_1945_CF719.jpg)

*Japanese officers escorted into the Municipal Building for the surrender ceremony, September 1945 — the ceremony Elizabeth Choy was invited to witness. (Royal Air Force official photographer, public domain, via Wikimedia Commons.)*

The Choys were invited to England to recuperate, and stayed four years. Lady Baden-Powell awarded her the Girl Guides' Bronze Cross, its highest honour for gallantry, and the Rajah of Sarawak gave her the Order of the Star of Sarawak. In June 1946 she and her husband were made Officers of the Order of the British Empire, and on 25 July that year she was given a private audience with Queen Elizabeth, the wife of King George VI, at St James's Palace — a full six years before that Queen's daughter became Elizabeth II. Choy studied domestic science at Northern Polytechnic, taught briefly at a London council school, and — unable to afford art school herself — worked as an artist's model for the sculptor Dora Gordine, who cast her twice, in works titled *Serene Jade* and *Flawless Crystal*.

## Return, politics, and the classroom

Choy came back to Singapore in December 1949. The following year she stood for the West Ward seat in the December 1950 City Council election and lost to the Progressive Party's Soh Ghee Soon. The Governor nominated her to the Legislative Council anyway, where she served a full term from 1951 to 1955 — the only woman on it — speaking often for the poor and for social services. As a Council member she represented Singapore at the coronation of Queen Elizabeth II in June 1953, a second, distinct royal occasion seven years after her private audience with the previous Queen Elizabeth. She stood for election once more, in Queenstown, then left politics, believing she could do more good as a teacher.

She kept teaching at St Andrew's until 1974, apart from a stint from 1956 to 1960 as the first principal of the Singapore School for the Blind. She was known around the school gates for her qipaos and bangles, which earned her the nickname "the Dayak Woman of Singapore," and by the 1950s she had also joined the women's auxiliary of the Singapore Volunteer Corps — where, per a 1953 newspaper headline announcing her enlistment, she picked up a second nickname, "Gunner Choy."

Elizabeth Choy died of pancreatic cancer on 14 September 2006, at her home on MacKenzie Road, aged 95.

**Why it matters today:** the building where Elizabeth Choy was tortured for nearly 200 days without giving up a single name is still standing, gazetted since 1995, directly across the road from one of Singapore's most-visited museums — and almost nobody waiting at the bus stop outside it has any reason to know. Her name survives in a handful of institutions and a Straits Times obituary, not in a street sign, which makes the marker on that corner one of the few physical things left that connects an ordinary building to what was actually done inside it.
