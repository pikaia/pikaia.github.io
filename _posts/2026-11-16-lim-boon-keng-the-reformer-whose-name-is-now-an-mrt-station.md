Every morning, thousands of commuters tap in at Boon Keng station on the North East Line, pass under the name, and think nothing of it. The name is a man's. Lim Boon Keng (1869–1957) was a doctor who treated the poor free of charge, the leading voice against the opium trade, a lawmaker for fourteen years, a co-founder of a girls' school, of three banks and of an insurance company, the personal physician of Sun Yat-sen, and for sixteen years the unsalaried president of a university in China. His contemporaries called him the Grand Old Man of Singapore. Today, almost nobody hurrying through his station could say who he was.

## The scholarship that went to the runner-up

Lim was a third-generation Peranakan, a Straits-born Chinese. His grandfather, Lim Mah Peng, had emigrated from Fujian to Penang in 1839 and married a Straits-born woman; the family moved to Singapore, where Lim Boon Keng was born on 18 October 1869. His childhood was shadowed by loss: his mother died when he was about eight, and his father died during his school years, leaving the family short of money. He entered Raffles Institution in 1879, and when the fees ran out it was the principal, R. W. Hullett, who intervened so the boy could stay on. By the standard accounts, it was those early deaths that turned him towards medicine.

In 1887 Lim sat the examination for the Queen's Scholarship, the colony's great prize for a schoolboy. He came second. First place went to another Raffles boy, Song Ong Siang — who had also come first the year before, and was disqualified both times for being under sixteen, the minimum age. So the scholarship went to the runner-up, and Lim became the first Chinese to win it. Song won it properly in 1888 and read law; the two men stayed collaborators for the rest of their lives, and much of what Lim built, he built with Song beside him.

At the University of Edinburgh, Lim read medicine from 1888 to 1892, graduating with first-class honours in medicine — by BiblioAsia's account, the only one of 204 graduates that year to take a first. He was a Fellow and one-time president of the Royal Medical Society, and went on to Cambridge as a research scholar in pathology. He came home in 1893, aged twenty-four, about as qualified as a young doctor in the colony could be.

Lim set up in private practice on Telok Ayer Street in 1893. In 1896 he went into partnership with T. Murray Robertson, co-owning The Dispensary at Raffles Place; later he founded the Sincere Dispensary on Hill Street, with a branch on Chulia Street. What the National Library Board's profile of him states plainly is that in his practice he "offered free medical treatment to those who could not afford it". From 1907 to 1910 he also taught at the Straits and Federated Malay States Government Medical School — the ancestor of today's medical school — without pay.

## The fight against opium

What opium was doing to the Chinese community troubled him from his first years in practice. He founded the Anti-Opium Society and opened an Opium Refuge, where addicts could get medical help to break the habit, and he wrote against the trade in the newspapers — to the anger, the heritage profile notes, of the colonial authorities and the merchants who profited from it. His 1898 essay "The attitude of the state towards the opium habit" set out the case in print. Opium was the cause he is most identified with, but not the only one: he campaigned as well against the mui tsai system of female child servitude, against foot-binding, and against gambling.

## Fourteen years on the Legislative Council

In 1895, aged twenty-six, Lim was appointed a Chinese member of the Straits Settlements Legislative Council. Governor Sir Charles Mitchell thought him too young and too much of a reformist firebrand, and opposed the appointment; strong public support carried it anyway. He served until 1903, resigned, and was reappointed in 1915 when Tan Jiak Kim gave up his seat, serving this time until he left Singapore in 1921 — fourteen years in all. The causes he pushed were consistent with the rest of his life: laws to relieve poor housing and overcrowding, to improve public sanitation, to mitigate poverty, and to curb the opium trade and gambling. In 1896 he headed a Commission of Inquiry into the sources of poverty in Singapore. Around the Council sat a cluster of other offices, all before he was thirty: municipal commissioner, Chinese Advisory Board member, Justice of the Peace. In 1918 he was appointed OBE for his services as an unofficial member of the Council.

## The magazine, the school and the queue

Lim and Song Ong Siang were the centre of a circle of English-educated Straits Chinese reformers, and the 1890s were their building years. In 1896 Lim had a hand in forming the Chinese Philomathic Society, an intellectual group centred on reformist ideals. In 1897 he and Song founded The Straits Chinese Magazine, an English-language quarterly that became the platform for his essays on opium, on female education and on a revived, modernised Confucianism; he wrote in it under pen names including "Wen Ching" and "Historicus". In 1899 the two men, with other prominent Straits Chinese, founded the Singapore Chinese Girls' School — at a time, the heritage profile says, when education for girls "was considered unnecessary and of no consequence" — and Lim served as the school's president. The same year, he began Mandarin classes at his own home for English-educated Chinese who had never learned the language of their grandparents.

![The Singapore Chinese Girls' School today](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/d/da/%28SGP-Singapore%29_Singapore_Chinese_Girls%27_School_2024-05-03.jpg)

*The Singapore Chinese Girls' School today — founded in 1899, when educating girls was widely thought unnecessary. (S5A-0043, CC BY 4.0, via Wikimedia Commons; photographed 2024)*

His most visible campaign was over hair. Influenced by China's own reform movement, Lim urged Chinese men to cut off their queues — the towchang, the plait imposed under the Qing — as a symbol that the old order could be left behind, and he set out his thinking in his 1901 book The Chinese Crisis from Within, published as "Wen Ching". Around these causes sat institution after institution: a Chinese newspaper in 1898, the Straits Chinese British Association in 1900, and a founding membership of the Singapore Chinese Chamber of Commerce in 1906.

## Rubber, banks, and the money underneath

The reformer had a businessman's eye, and the money ran underneath the reform. Henry Nicholas Ridley, the director of the Botanic Gardens, was preaching rubber to anyone who would listen; Lim listened. He invested early in Tan Chay Yan's planting in Malacca, and in 1909 the two men formed Serangoon Rubber Plantations in Singapore. Those who heeded his call to plant, BiblioAsia records, enjoyed a booming success.

Banking followed. Lim helped to establish all three of the era's Hokkien banks: the Chinese Commercial Bank in 1912, where he was vice-chairman at its founding; the Ho Hong Bank in 1917, with Lim Peng Siang; and the Oversea-Chinese Bank in 1919. Twenty years later, in the depths of the Depression, the three amalgamated — on 31 October 1932 — to form the Oversea-Chinese Banking Corporation, OCBC. Lim himself had no hand in the merger: by 1932 he had been in China for a decade. What he co-founded were the three banks that became it. In 1920 he had also co-founded, with Lim Nee Soon, the Overseas Assurance Corporation, the first locally owned insurance company in Singapore, and served as its founding chairman.

## Sun Yat-sen's physician, Xiamen's president

China ran through Lim's middle years. He knew both Sun Yat-sen and the reformer Kang Youwei, who visited Singapore in the 1900s; he was a founding member of the Singapore branch of Sun's Tongmenghui, the Revolutionary League, set up in 1906, and when it became the Singapore branch of the Kuomintang he served as its president. When the revolution overthrew the Qing in 1911, the appointments followed: Lim was made personal physician and confidential secretary to Sun Yat-sen, and was put in charge of health services for the new republic — medical adviser to the Peking government, inspector-general of its hospitals, president of its Board of Health.

![Tan Kah Kee with Sun Yat-sen in Singapore, December 1911](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/0/03/Tan_Kah_Kee_and_Sun_Yat-sen.jpg)

*Tan Kah Kee (left) with Sun Yat-sen in Singapore on 16 December 1911, as Sun passed through after the revolution. Between them, these two men took Lim to Peking and to Xiamen. (Public domain, via Wikimedia Commons)*

In 1921 came the call that took him away from Singapore for sixteen years. Tan Kah Kee, the rubber and pineapple magnate turned educator, had founded a university at Xiamen — Amoy, in the older spelling — in his home province of Fujian, and he invited Lim to lead it. Lim resigned from the Legislative Council, gave up his Singapore business interests, and served as the university's president from 1921 to 1937, without a salary. His best-known work of those years is a scholar's, not an administrator's: his English translation of Qu Yuan's Li Sao, published in 1929 with prefaces by H. A. Giles and Rabindranath Tagore. In 1937, as the university passed to government control and war approached, he came home.

![The Wenqing Pavilion at Xiamen University](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/f/f5/%E5%8E%A6%E9%97%A8%E5%A4%A7%E5%AD%A6%E6%96%87%E5%BA%86%E4%BA%AD.jpg)

*The Wenqing Pavilion at Xiamen University, with Lim's statue — Wenqing, from his Chinese name Lin Wenqing, is the name the campus still calls him by. (Angbinmarist, CC BY-SA 3.0, via Wikimedia Commons)*

## The occupation: an old man under pressure

Home in 1937, Lim founded the Straits Chinese China Relief Fund Committee to raise money for China's war against Japan — which made him a marked man when Singapore fell in February 1942. The Japanese administration pressured the seventy-two-year-old to head the Oversea Chinese Association, the body charged with raising the 50 million dollars the occupiers demanded from the Chinese community. The record, as the National Library Board tells it, is of an old man coerced: he at first refused; his wife Grace was made to kneel in the sun for hours; he finally accepted a figurehead's role, and then dragged his feet — the heritage profile records that he often feigned drunkenness to avoid being used further as a political tool. When the money fell short, he said so to the authorities' faces. "We never told a lie," he told them, as The Straits Times recalled his words in 1947. "If we are unable to pay, then die we will." Part of the sum was raised; the balance was met by a loan from the Yokohama Specie Bank.

## A kampong of his own, and a name on the railway

From 1937, Lim lived at 1 Paterson Hill, in the Orchard Road district, in an attap-roofed mansion. Over the years the house filled — with relatives, with dependants, with tenants — until, in BiblioAsia's word, it had become his own "kampong", a village of the extended household surviving into the age of the apartment block, on some of the most valuable residential land in Singapore. After the war he retired from public life. His last decade is, in the record, essentially a blank: the Grand Old Man at home in his kampong, while the city he had spent sixty years reforming rebuilt itself around him.

He died at home on 1 January 1957, leaving his wife Grace, six children, thirty grandchildren and six great-grandchildren — among them, a generation further down, the playwright Stella Kon, of Emily of Emerald Hill. He was buried at Bidadari Cemetery; when the cemetery was cleared in the 2000s, his remains were exhumed and his tombstone placed in the Bidadari Memorial Garden at Mount Vernon, among a small company of the notable dead the city chose to keep.

![The tombstone of Lim Boon Keng and Grace at Bidadari Memorial Garden](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/7/77/Gravestone_of_Lim_Boon_Keng_and_Grace_Pek_Ha_Yin%2C_Bidadari_Garden%2C_Singapore_-_20121008.jpg)

*The tombstone of Lim Boon Keng and his wife Grace at the Bidadari Memorial Garden — moved there when Bidadari Cemetery was cleared. (Smuconlaw, CC BY-SA 3.0, via Wikimedia Commons; photographed 2012)*

Posterity has kept his name in exactly two public places. The first is Boon Keng Road. The second is the station beneath it: Boon Keng MRT station, NE9 on the North East Line, which opened on 20 June 2003, forty-six years after his death. In 2004, Senior Minister Lee Kuan Yew held Lim up at an international conference as a role model of biculturalism that Singapore Chinese should emulate — a man at home in two civilisations at once, which is perhaps the shortest summary of the life. The commuters get the name without the life. It is the way of station names; but this one belonged to a man who treated the poor for free, fought the colony's most profitable vice, built a school for girls nobody thought worth educating, and ran a university for nothing.

**Where it fits in the bigger story:** Singapore names its stations after roads, and its roads after men it has half forgotten. Lim Boon Keng's name survives precisely where his life's work was aimed — in the daily movement of ordinary people — attached to a doctor, reformer and educator whom almost none of them could now describe. That gap, between the name everyone uses and the life nobody remembers, is the most lesser-known thing of all.
