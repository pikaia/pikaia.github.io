On Monday, the 2nd of December 1985, the Stock Exchange of Singapore did not open for trading. Neither did the Kuala Lumpur Stock Exchange. Both stayed shut for three days — the only time Singapore's market has ever closed completely. What forced it was a single company, Pan-Electric Industries, a refrigerator maker that had grown into a sprawling conglomerate, and a web of undisclosed contracts that had been propping up its share price. At the centre of it was Tan Koon Swan, a businessman who three weeks earlier had been elected leader of Malaysia's main Chinese political party.

## From refrigerators to a conglomerate

Pan-Electric began in 1956 as Climate Engineering, importing French Frimatic refrigerators into Singapore. Its founder, Ernest Kahlenberg, moved into local assembly under the government's pioneer-industry scheme, renamed the company Pan-Electric Industries in 1961, and opened a factory at Kampong Arang Road in 1965. By 1967 it was billed as the largest refrigerator plant in Southeast Asia, and in August 1968 it listed on the stock exchange.

From the mid-1970s the company diversified quickly and widely: marine salvage through a firm called Selco, property development through Vanguard Realty, a 1981 merger with the electrical-goods maker Acma, stakes in hotels. By 1985 Pan-Electric sat at the head of around sixty subsidiaries. Peter Tham joined the board in 1982; Kahlenberg had left by 1983.

## The man at the centre

Tan Koon Swan was a Malaysian who had made his fortune in Singapore's stock market in the 1970s, with a reputation for a golden touch. He held his interest in Pan-Electric through two companies he controlled, Sigma International and Grand United Holdings. In November 1985 he was elected president of the Malaysian Chinese Association, a senior partner in Malaysia's ruling coalition — a man with money in Singapore's markets and a hand now in Malaysian government.

## The contracts holding it up

The way Pan-Electric's share price was supported was through forward contracts. A shareholder sells a block of shares to a broking firm now, with an agreement to buy them back at a fixed price several months later — in effect a bet that the price will have risen in the meantime. On its own this was an accepted piece of market practice. What made Pan-Electric's version dangerous was that the contracts were large and, for the most part, undisclosed, and that when the market softened through 1985 and the shares fell rather than rose, the parties committed to buying them back were facing heavy losses.

Tan Koon Swan's own forward commitments came to about S$140 million. Across the whole market, brokers' forward exposure to Pan-Electric and related stocks was estimated at close to S$600 million. As the shares kept sliding, Tan and Tham worked to hold the price up, and Tan moved roughly S$29 million out of company accounts to keep the arrangement going.

On 27 September 1985 the company announced a S$64 million rights issue meant to steady its finances. On 18 November it missed a payment on a bank loan. On 21 November it disclosed borrowings of S$453 million from thirty-five banks, most of it unsecured. The banks stopped lending — not only to Pan-Electric but to the broking firms tied to it. On 30 November the company was placed in receivership.

## Three days shut

With Pan-Electric in receivership and its forward contracts about to come due, the danger was a chain reaction. Around half a dozen broking firms were heavily exposed, and because Singapore's and Malaysia's brokers traded with one another, one failure could pull others down with it. On Monday, 2 December, the two exchanges suspended all trading. They stayed closed through Wednesday.

Behind the closed doors, a rescue was put together. On 3 December the Stock Exchange of Singapore asked the four big local banks — DBS, OCBC, OUB and UOB — for a credit line, first reported at S$150 million and settled at S$180 million, to keep the exposed brokers afloat while the forward contracts were unwound in an orderly way rather than through a fire sale. A lawyer named Chan Sek Keong, later Singapore's Chief Justice, helped the Monetary Authority draft the agreement. The Finance Minister, Richard Hu, was clear that the fund was not there to save brokers who had been reckless, only to see that the exchange's own obligations were met, and that anything drawn would be repaid with interest. Even so, about six broking firms did not survive. The market reopened on Friday, 5 December.

## The reckoning

Pan-Electric was wound up in October 1986. Its shares were worthless; thousands of shareholders and a long list of creditors carried the loss.

Tan Koon Swan was arrested in January 1986. He pleaded guilty, under a bargain that cut fifteen charges down to one, to abetting criminal breach of trust, and was sentenced to two years in jail and a fine. He resigned the MCA presidency, and the conviction ended a political career only weeks into its peak. Peter Tham was sentenced to eight years on dozens of forgery charges; Pan-Electric's finance director received a shorter term. Decades later, some in Malaysia — and the case's own lead prosecutor — would argue that Tan had been wrongly convicted, a view that remains contested.

The lasting change was to the rules. The Securities Industry Act of 1986 gave the Monetary Authority far greater control over the securities business and tightened the requirements on forward contracts, on how much capital a broker had to hold, and on what had to be disclosed.

## The long tail

The crisis also exposed how entangled the Singapore and Kuala Lumpur exchanges had become. At the end of 1989 the two of them removed each other's companies from their boards. So that Singaporeans could still trade Malaysian shares, Singapore opened an over-the-counter market called CLOB International in January 1990.

CLOB ran quietly for eight years. Then, in September 1998, in the depths of the Asian financial crisis, Malaysia imposed capital controls and froze it — around S$4 billion of shares belonging to about 172,000 investors, almost all of them Singaporean, locked in place. The freeze lasted more than a year; the shares were released in stages under a deal reached in early 2000. The untangling of the two markets that began with Pan-Electric took fifteen years and a second crisis to finish.

**Where it fits in the bigger story:** the shape of it is familiar to anyone who has watched a financial crisis since. It begins with a scheme that bends convention without breaking the law — here, forward contracts run at a size and secrecy the rules had not anticipated. It runs longer than it should because the person at the centre is trusted, and large positions held in a respected name draw fewer questions than they would from a stranger. Then something unforeseen — a soft market, this time — turns the bet the wrong way, and what was one company's problem becomes everyone's, carried outward along the web of people who had lent it money or stood on the other side of its trades. Pan-Electric was the first time that pattern closed a Singapore exchange. It was not the last time the pattern would run.
