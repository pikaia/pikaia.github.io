From the Sentosa waterfront, across a mile of anchorage, stands an island of tanks and towers that almost no Singaporean has ever set foot on. Pulau Bukom is closed to the public, reached by a workers' ferry, and it has been handling oil longer than any other place in Singapore — since 1892, when what its tanks held was not petrol for cars but kerosene for lamps. It was a storage island first, then a town of five thousand people, then Singapore's first refinery. This is the story of the island that oil built, emptied, and built again.

## Freshwater Island

Bukom lies about five and a half kilometres southwest of the mainland, in the cluster of southern islands. Before oil, it was a mangrove island known to passing ships as a place to take on fresh water — the Dutch called it Zoetwater-eiland, Freshwater Island. Its Malay name is usually traced to *rangkek bukom*, a cone shell wide at one end and tapering to a point, which the island's original shape was said to resemble; another old account derives it instead from *hukum*, judgement, for a ruler said to have tried cases there.

In the nineteenth century the island's owner was Captain Giovanni Gaggino, a trader. What changed its life was a decision taken in London in 1891.

## The first of its kind in the East

In 1891 the London firm M. Samuel & Co. chose Singapore as its base for importing Russian kerosene in bulk and distributing it across Asia — lamp oil, in the age before electric light reached most homes. Samuel appointed the local agency house Syme & Co. to establish a petroleum tank depot. The government refused to allow bulk petroleum storage in town, so the depot went offshore: in September 1891 Syme & Co. bought eight hectares on Bukom from Captain Gaggino for $3,000 and began building.

The depot cost over $60,000, with tank capacity of 4,500 tonnes, a wharf and ship-to-tank pipelines. It opened on 16 September 1892, when the first shipment arrived aboard the S.S. *Murex*. The National Library Board calls it the first depot of its kind in the East, and Singapore's first oil storage facility; by 1902, Bukom had become the oil supply centre for the Far East. The kerosene island was, in effect, the founding site of Singapore's oil trade.

The Samuel business became, in 1897, a separate company called Shell Transport and Trading, and in 1907 it merged with Royal Dutch Petroleum to form Royal Dutch Shell — so the island's tenant grew into one of the largest companies on earth without ever leaving. The connection ran deep into local business life: the Lee family's firm, Lee Chim Tuan, handled stevedoring and materials for Shell at Bukom from the industry's early days, and when Lee Chim Tuan died in 1955, the flag at Shell's signal station on the island was lowered to half-mast in respect.

## Fire, twice over

By 1942 the depot had grown to sixty storage tanks and five wharves. As the Japanese advanced down the peninsula, the British torched forty-two of the tanks to keep the oil from falling into enemy hands. During the Occupation that followed, Bukom served as a prisoner-of-war camp: some three hundred Australian and New Zealand prisoners were put to work salvaging the wrecked plant they had watched burn. After the war, Shell's employees repaired the installation, and by 1950 it was back in working order.

## A town of five thousand

What the post-war island became was not just a tank farm but a town. In the early 1960s as many as five thousand people lived on Bukom, most of them Shell employees and their families. The island had housing, two markets, chapels and a mosque, a Hindu temple, two schools, a clinic, four clubs and an open-air cinema. On Bukom Besar stood the company's quarters — the *rumah putih*, the white houses, for staff families, and the *rumah merah*, the red houses, for officers, engineers and doctors — while the islander kampong stood on Bukom Kechil, the smaller island beside it.

One islander's story carries the whole arc. Ahmadi bin Mukasan, known as Madi, was born and bred on Bukom; his father had come from Banyumas in Java and found work with Shell. In 1961 the family was resettled to a kampong house in Kaki Bukit — "We didn't want to leave initially," he recalled — but Madi came back to the island to work, and lived there again as a Shell fireman, "Fireman 18", facing a major refinery fire in February 1989. The family finally left Bukom in the 1990s.

## Pioneer Certificate No. 1

By 1959 Bukom was already the biggest centre in Southeast Asia for the storage, blending, packing and bunkering of oil. That December, Shell announced something larger: a refinery. The agreement with the Singapore government was signed on 1 March 1960, construction began that June, and on 26 July 1961 the Finance Minister, Goh Keng Swee, officially opened Singapore's first oil refinery. Shell received the government's Pioneer Certificate No. 1 for it — the first award of the scheme that would underwrite Singapore's industrialisation. The plant cost $30 million and could process about twenty thousand barrels of crude a day. Over the next two decades four more refineries were added on the island, and by 1980 Bukom's capacity had reached twenty-five million tonnes a year.

## The island empties

Growth consumed the island it had made. As operations spread onto Bukom Kechil, the inhabitants there — some two thousand people — were resettled around 1970 to make way for crude oil storage; where they all went, the record does not say, and the named moves that survive are individual ones, like Madi's family to Kaki Bukit. A bridge joined the two Bukoms in 1969. Hills on both islands were levelled for tank space in the 1970s, and the reef of Pulau Ular was reclaimed into a thirty-hectare island and linked by another bridge. By 1970 only twelve hundred people still lived on Bukom Besar, and most workers were already what they remain today: ferry commuters.

The last of the island families held on remarkably late. Leng Ng Tai, who joined Shell in 1969, lived on Bukom with his family into the 1990s — by then twenty-eight families remained in two housing blocks, alongside firemen and police families. Doors were never locked; a dance instructor was ferried in every Wednesday. The Lengs moved to a West Coast terrace house in 1997. His son Benny works for the same company, and father and son still make the journey to Bukom together: car to the jetty, then the ferry across.

## The Laju morning

On 31 January 1974, Bukom became the scene of Singapore's first encounter with international terrorism. Four armed men — two from the Japanese Red Army and two from the Popular Front for the Liberation of Palestine — attacked the refinery complex, detonating explosives at three oil tanks and setting one ablaze. The fire was quickly put out and the damage came to some $45,000. The attackers then hijacked the ferryboat *Laju* at the Bukom jetty and held its crew hostage; after seven days of negotiation, conducted for Singapore by a team of officials led by S. R. Nathan, the hijackers released their remaining hostages and were flown out to Kuwait on 8 February. The island went back to work.

## Fire and theft

The modern plant has had two darker chapters, both on the public record. On 28 September 2011, fire broke out in a processing area of pumps and pipelines; Shell evacuated its staff and shut down the entire refinery. The fire took more than thirty hours to extinguish, fought by the SCDF and Shell's own firefighters, and the plant was back to full production only in December.

The second chapter ran quietly for years. From at least 2014, rogue employees siphoned marine gas oil from the plant and sold it at sea; Shell noticed unexplained losses in 2015, and in January 2018 seventeen men were arrested. In court, the scheme was put at more than 350,000 tonnes of gas oil, worth over S$200 million — the largest theft of its kind in Singapore. The first mastermind, Juandi Pungot, was jailed for twenty-nine years in 2022; the second, Abdul Latif Ibrahim, for twenty-five years in 2025, with other former employees drawing long terms between them.

## New owners

In May 2010 the site had taken its modern shape with the opening of the Shell Eastern Petrochemicals Complex — a S$4.1 billion project built around an ethylene cracker on Bukom, tied by subsea pipelines to Jurong Island. But in May 2024 Shell announced the sale of the whole Singapore energy and chemicals park, Bukom and Jurong together, and on 1 April 2025 the sale was completed to CAPGC, a joint venture between Indonesia's Chandra Asri and Glencore. The island now trades as the Aster Energy and Chemicals Park — a refinery of 237,000 barrels a day, on the sale documents' figures, with its 1.1 million-tonne-a-year cracker — and in September 2026 its new owners launched a terminals business around Bukom's thirteen wharves and tank storage. The work never stopped; only the name over the gate changed.

Even the ferry has been renewed. Since 2023 the crossing from Pasir Panjang has been served by fully electric ferries — the first fully electric ferry service in Singapore — beginning with the *Penguin Refresh*, unveiled at Bukom in April that year. One cloud belongs in the record, stated carefully: two oil leaks at the site in 2024 led to Shell Singapore being charged in September 2026 in connection with the discharges and their reporting; the case is before the courts, and concerns the period when Shell still operated the island.

## The next island

Bukom's story may not be finished. At the National Day Rally in August 2026, the Prime Minister announced a long-term plan to merge Pulau Semakau, Pulau Bukom and Pulau Sudong into a new "Western Island", for advanced manufacturing, power generation and defence, linked to Jurong Island — a plan measured in decades, still under study, with marine groups raising attributed concerns for the reefs of the southern islands. If it happens, the kerosene island will be reinvented once more, as it has been roughly once a generation since 1892: depot, refinery, petrochemical park, and now, perhaps, one shore of something larger.

For now it stays what it has been for a century — a place most people know only as a lit shore across the water, and a few thousand know as the far end of a ferry ride to work.

**Where it fits in the bigger story:** Singapore's oil age did not begin with Jurong Island or with the great refineries of the 1970s. It began in 1892, on a freshwater island five kilometres offshore, with tanks of Russian kerosene for the region's lamps — and the same island then hosted the town, the refinery and the petrochemical park that followed. Bukom is the industry's first address, and it has never closed.
