In May 1937, the Bank of China paid $715,000 for one of the most valuable properties in Singapore — a marble-fronted banking palace on Battery Road — and then, for ten years, did not move in. The palace stayed a department store, the bank stayed down the road in Cecil Street, and when the bank finally took possession in 1947 it was only to pull the whole thing down. The building the Bank of China is known by in Singapore is the tower it built on the site afterwards. The palace it bought, and never used, is the forgotten half of the story.

The palace had been built for another bank entirely. In December 1893 the Chartered Bank of India, Australia and China leased a plot at the corner of Battery Road and Flint Street, on a site cleared by a fire that July which had burned the godowns of McAlister & Co. and McKerrow & Co. The architects were Swan & Maclaren — the design seems likely to have been the work of James Meikle, who left Singapore before it was finished — and the building cost about $60,000. The bank moved in on the 6th of February 1895.

It was a palazzo in the full nineteenth-century sense: three storeys and about sixty feet tall, rusticated up to the first-floor cornice on a six-foot granite base, its upper floors divided into five bays by two-storey Corinthian pilasters, with a heavy cornice, a balustraded parapet and a row of ornamental urns along the top. Inside was a banking hall nineteen feet high with a semi-circular counter and an encaustic tile floor, and a fireproof bullion strongroom walled in granite and concrete-and-iron. Readers of our post on the exchange banks of Raffles Place will have met it there — it was the Chartered's grand statement on the waterfront, built to be looked at.

The Chartered itself only stayed twenty-one years. Having outgrown the palazzo, it built Chartered Bank Chambers further along the road and moved out on the 6th of May 1916. The Bank of Taiwan took the ground floor and Mitsui Bussan Kaisha the floor above, and the building settled into a second life as offices. When the Bank of Taiwan left for Raffles Place in May 1931, the palace stood vacant into 1933, was dressed up by the architect Heinrich Rudolf Arbenz, and reopened as "Publicity House". Then, on the 8th of October 1934, the Kuala Lumpur department store Gian Singh & Co. opened a Singapore branch in its basement, run by brothers Hardial Singh Bajaj and Pritam Singh, with the retailer M. S. Ally & Co. as the other main tenant. The Chartered Bank's bullion palace had become a shop.

The Bank of China arrived in Singapore by a different door. Founded in 1912, the bank had set up its overseas branches largely to handle the remittances and foreign exchange of overseas Chinese, and its head office planned a Singapore branch in 1935. The branch opened on the 15th of June 1936 — in the lower two floors of the Great Eastern Life building in Cecil Street, not on Battery Road at all. The bank's own history records an opening of some ceremony: more than a thousand guests, and nine Royal Air Force planes flying three circles over the building.

A year later came the purchase. In May 1937 the Straits Times announced the $715,000 land deal: the bank's Cecil Street rooms were growing too small, and it intended, in time, to pull the old building down and build its own premises on the site. In the meantime the property — by then numbered 4–6 Battery Road — took the name Bank of China Building. It was a name on a deed, nothing more. The tenants' lease had about four years to run, and the palace traded on as a department store under its new owner's name above the door in all but fact.

The war of the lease came first, in 1940. That October the bank applied to the Rent Assessment Board to evict Gian Singh and M. S. Ally so that it could move in itself: its staff had quadrupled since 1936, and its Cecil Street lease would expire on the 31st of May 1941. The applications were refused. The board allowed a fresh application against Gian Singh the following March, but in the event the only tenant put out of the Bank of China Building in 1940 was a cigarette stall.

Then came the larger war. The bank's Singapore branch closed as Japanese forces overran Southeast Asia in 1942 — only its London, New York, Calcutta and Bombay offices stayed open — while back on Battery Road, remarkably, both department stores traded on through the Occupation; by July 1943 a Japanese restaurant, the Mutsumi, had opened in the building. After the war the palace was requisitioned by the British Army, which did not vacate it until the 22nd of November 1946.

What followed was reported in the newspapers as Singapore's "squat" drama. The bank gave both stores until the 15th of January 1947 to leave, meaning to demolish and build. The Rent Board refused an extension; on the day itself the two firms simply stayed put, even after their water and electricity were cut — the supplies were restored on appeal to Lazarus Rayman, the president of the Municipal Commission. Gian Singh's troubles compounded when goods worth $30,000 were stolen from its new premises in a dawn robbery the day before the deadline. The bank sued both firms in February. M. S. Ally moved out by the 10th of February; Gian Singh finally followed once its new Raffles Place store opened on the 15th of March 1947. Demolition of the palazzo was under way by May, under a Malaya Tribune headline that already looked past it: "Work Begins on S'pore Skyscraper".

The bank had lodged its plans the previous November: a fourteen-storey building, 200 feet high, which the Straits Times said would be the tallest building in Malaya if completed. Foundation work began in November 1948 and went slowly, out of care for the foundations of the buildings next door; scientists pronounced the work safe in February 1950, and the foundations were finished that month. Then the building produced its most dated document. With the foundations complete, the branch sought — and, in May 1950, obtained — its head office's approval to build the superstructure. The head office was now in Peking: the Bank of China had come under the new government in China in 1949, and Singapore was among the branches in Hong Kong, London, Penang, Kuala Lumpur and elsewhere that joined the Beijing-headquartered bank. A Singapore building permit, countersigned in a new capital, is the revolution arriving in the colony's paperwork.

Tenders were called in March 1951 and construction began that April, at a cost the Straits Times put at $3 million. The architects were Palmer and Turner of Hong Kong, who were building the Bank of China's Hong Kong premises at the same time. The block was finished in December 1953, and on the 28th of December the Singapore Standard announced: "Bank Of China Moves Into New Building Today." Seventeen years after it opened in Cecil Street, and sixteen after buying the site, the bank was finally on Battery Road.

The Standard described a block designed to look like the bank's premises in Shanghai and Hong Kong: strict symmetry, plaster finish, metal-framed windows, with deliberate Chinese touches in the oriental trimmings and mouldings of the façade. The bank took the lowest three and the top three floors; the basement held safe-deposit boxes and a car park. It was a building of firsts — the first in Singapore to incorporate a mail chute and document lifts, and the first in the country to be centrally air-conditioned, though at the opening only the lower half of the building was served. And at the Battery Road entrance stood the detail that has outlasted every argument about the building: a pair of Chinese lions in pre-cast stone, sculpted by the Italian artist Rodolfo Nolli. They were among his last works in Singapore, and they are still on guard at the door today.

About the tower's height, the record needs one careful paragraph, because the claim most often repeated about it does not survive its own footnotes. The building stands 200 feet (61 metres), and the National Heritage Board calls it, fairly, "one of the first modern skyscrapers in Singapore"; on the low-rise Battery Road waterfront it towered over everything, and a period postcard could describe it without embarrassment as the tallest building of its time. But it was never the tallest building in Singapore. The Cathay Building, finished in 1939, already stood taller at 70 metres, and within two years of the bank's opening the Asia Insurance Building, a few hundred metres away, rose to 82 metres and took the national title for itself. What the Bank of China Building genuinely was — the tallest building on the Singapore River waterfront, and for a generation the landmark of Raffles Place — is distinction enough.

The branch's hardest test came a decade later, and it came from politics rather than banking. In the Federation of Malaya, the bank's branches had already been made to close in 1959, under a banking law that barred banks under foreign government control; its Kuala Lumpur building passed to the Bank of Tokyo. Singapore, a separate jurisdiction, was unaffected — until December 1964, in the middle of a revenue dispute between Singapore and the federal government in Kuala Lumpur, when Malaysia's finance minister announced that the Bank of China in Singapore would be closed. The branch handled the financial arrangements for Singapore's trade and remittances with China, and Singapore's government said no. "S'pore Govt Opposes Closure of Bank," ran the Straits Times headline on the 31st of December 1964; the Minister for Culture, S. Rajaratnam, criticised the decision publicly. The closure was never carried out. When Singapore became independent in August 1965, its government decided the branch would be allowed to continue operating — a decision the bank publicly welcomed as good for Singapore's economy and for friendly relations between the two peoples. The tower on Battery Road had survived its second change of flags.

The building today is two buildings. In December 1990 the bank disclosed plans for a $90 million extension; the ground was broken on the 15th of April 1994, and in 2000 a new tower, 168 metres tall, was completed immediately beside the 1953 block on a shared podium — designed by Palmer and Turner again, nearly half a century on, deliberately inheriting the older building's style, with its rising lines and geometrised Chinese decoration. The old block still stands within the development, still the bank's Singapore headquarters and main branch at 4 Battery Road, its upper floors let as offices; in April 2015 the branch opened a historical gallery on its first floor, the first such exhibition mounted by any Bank of China overseas branch. The lions remain at the entrance. For all that, the 1953 block holds no conservation status — unlike the Asia Insurance Building, gazetted in 2007 as Singapore's first protected skyscraper, the Bank of China Building stands in the middle of the financial district unlisted and largely unremarked, a first-generation skyscraper hiding in plain sight.

**Where it fits in the bigger story:** This post began as a follow-up to [the exchange banks of Raffles Place](/2026/10/26/the-exchange-banks-of-raffles-place-chartered-hsbc-and-mercantile/), where this plot was the Chartered Bank's palace. Its second life is the stranger one: a site bought in 1937 as an option on the future, held through a refused eviction, an occupation and a tenants' sit-in, until the purchaser could build on it; a branch that closed for a war, took its building orders from a new capital in 1950, and was kept open in 1965 by a brand-new country's government deciding it belonged. Battery Road has been rebuilt many times over, but few of its corners carry the whole arc — colonial bank palace, department store, squat, skyscraper — in a single address.
