Along Upper Changi Road North stands a fragment of a prison: an entrance gate, 180 metres of high wall and two corner turrets, kept when everything behind them was demolished in 2004. The fragment is a national monument, gazetted in 2016, but it stands inside a restricted area and almost nobody can walk up to it. The gaol it belonged to was, in turn, Singapore's most modern prison, an internment camp for the colony's own rulers, the most crowded prisoner-of-war cage in Singapore, the place where Japanese war criminals were hanged, and then the country's main prison for another half-century. This is the gaol's own story.

## The model prison

By the early 1930s, Singapore's only prison, at Pearl's Hill — later known as Outram Road Prison — was overcrowded and could not keep different classes of prisoner apart. In January 1933 the Legislative Council approved a new convict gaol at Changi, out on the island's rural east coast, and the local firm Woh Hup began building it that year. The design came from the Public Works Department — an academic study of the prison's heritage names the government architect Frank Dorrington Ward as the man responsible — and it followed the "telephone-pole" plan of the modern prison: two four-storey cell blocks branching off a central covered corridor, so that warders could reach any block quickly. Around the cells stood work rooms, a hospital block, punishment cells, a separate small block for European prisoners, and a perimeter wall six metres high with a watchtower turret at each of its four corners, entered through a double-leafed steel gate.

It was built for 600 long-sentence prisoners, and by the standards of its day it was remarkably civilised in its fittings: a modern sewerage system, a central laundry and kitchen, showers by the exercise yards, and in every cell an electric light and a flushing latrine. Roots, the National Heritage Board's portal, calls the finished prison "the most modern institution of its kind in the East". It was gazetted as a prison on 24 December 1936 and received its first prisoners on 4 January 1937, long-term men transferred from Pearl's Hill, which settled down to holding short-term convicts instead.

One of its cell doors survives in the National Museum of Singapore, a heavy blue steel door with its inspection flap and food hatch, labelled simply as a Changi Prison cell door of the 1930s. It is worth remembering that door when the numbers start to climb, because everything that happened to this building happened one cell at a time.

## A word about "Changi"

"Changi" the wartime camp was much bigger than Changi Prison the building. Most Allied prisoners of war spent the war in and around the British Army's own barracks at Changi — Selarang, Roberts and Kitchener — and this blog has told two of those stories already: the [Selarang Square Squeeze of September 1942](/2026/10/24/the-selarang-square-squeeze-1942/), and [Stanley Warren's chapel murals at Roberts Barracks](/2026/10/25/the-changi-murals-stanley-warrens-chapel-paintings-at-roberts-barracks/). The gaol had a different war: first it held civilians, and only at the end was it filled with soldiers.

## The internees

Singapore fell on 15 February 1942. Two days later, on 17 February, the colony's European civilians were rounded up and marched to Changi Prison. The National Library Board's breakdown of who went in is 2,200 men, 359 women and 61 children — 2,620 people, other accounts putting the total somewhere between 2,400 and 2,500 — into a building designed for 600. Among them, in one of the neatest reversals of the war, was the Governor of the Straits Settlements himself, Sir Shenton Thomas.

The Japanese largely left the internees to run the place themselves. Each block elected its leader; the internees maintained the camp, ran schools for the children (history and geography were banned subjects), and organised concerts, theatre and lectures. Men and women were held in separate blocks and forbidden to meet, but families passed messages to each other in code through the scripts and songs of the concerts each side performed. The women produced a camp newspaper, POW WOW, led by a former Malaya Tribune journalist, Freddy Bloom, which ran from April 1942 for almost nineteen months; the men had the Changi Guardian. Food in the first year was adequate if basic, and the building's modern plumbing spared the internees some of the worst of camp disease — but dysentery and malaria were constant, and as the war ground on, malnutrition diseases such as beri-beri and tuberculosis took hold.

### The quilts

In 1942, mainly between March and August, the women internees made three quilts. Each is a signature quilt of 66 embroidered squares, every square signed by the woman who made it. The idea came from Ethel Mulvany, a Canadian internee who had been a Red Cross representative in Singapore and acted as the women's Red Cross representative in the camp. The quilts were partly to relieve boredom and raise morale — but their real purpose was to let the men in the other camps know that the women and children were alive. Each woman was asked to put something of herself into her square, with her name.

Mulvany won the Japanese commandant's permission by presenting the quilts as gifts for the wounded, and by making one of the three for wounded Japanese soldiers. Even then, the word "prison" was not acceptable and had to be unpicked from the squares as they were assembled; the word "gaol" was allowed. The Australian War Memorial, which now holds the Australian and Japanese quilts — the British quilt is with the British Red Cross in London — is careful about what the quilts were: there is no evidence they were ever used by the wounded, and they were not made as objects of resistance. They were, more simply and more movingly, a list of names in thread, sent out of the wire in the hope of being read. No photograph of them is freely licensed, so on this blog, as in the camp, they travel in words.

### The Double Tenth

On the night of 26–27 September 1943, Australian commandos of Operation Jaywick sank or damaged seven Japanese vessels in Singapore's harbour. The Kempeitai, the Japanese military police, were convinced the attack had been directed by a spy network among the Changi internees. On 10 October 1943 — the Double Tenth, the tenth day of the tenth month — they raided the gaol, and over the following months arrested 57 internees. The 57 were taken not back to cells but to the YMCA building on Orchard Road, the Central Police Station and Smith Street Police Station, and interrogated under torture until April 1944. Fifteen died. One more, an ambulance driver named John Long, was executed. Among the tortured were the Anglican Bishop of Singapore, John Leonard Wilson, and, in the wider sweep, Elizabeth Choy, who had run a canteen at Tan Tock Seng Hospital and passed food and money to prisoners; she survived nearly 200 days of interrogation. In May 1944 the surviving civilians were moved out of the gaol to Sime Road Camp, where they stayed until the war ended.

## Five to a cell

The civilians' departure made room for a grimmer population. In May 1944 the Japanese concentrated the Allied prisoners of war of the Changi area in and around the gaol. The Australian War Memorial's figures give the shape of it: about 5,000 men squeezed into the prison building itself — five men to a cell built for one — and some 11,700 prisoners in all, in less than a quarter of a square kilometre around it, with attap huts put up in the courtyards for the overflow; the National Library Board rounds the complex's population to about 12,000.

Conditions in the gaol were worse than in the barracks the men had come from: tighter control, shorter rations, and in July 1944 the Japanese revoked the authority of the Allied senior officers over their own men. Even so, the prisoners opened a makeshift Playhouse Theatre inside in September 1944. By the last months of the war, food was so short that men were eating sparrows and rats to supplement what they were given.

## Liberation, and the reckoning

Japan surrendered on 2 September 1945, and the prisoners at Changi were released on 6 September, six days before the formal surrender ceremony in the city. The photograph at the head of this post — men pouring out of a gate, waving, half of them in nothing but shorts — is that week.

![Men of the 2/18th Battalion looking out from their hut at Changi Gaol, 10 September 1945](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/2/28/2-18th_Changi.jpg)

*Men of the 2/18th Australian Infantry Battalion, newly liberated, looking out from their hut at Changi Gaol on 10 September 1945. (Australian War Memorial, Public domain, via Wikimedia Commons)*

The gaol then changed sides. It now held Japanese war-crimes suspects, and it became the place where Singapore's war-crimes sentences were carried out: the National Library Board records that most of the executions for those convicted with a death sentence — by hanging or by firing squad — took place at Changi, and the newspapers of 1946 and 1947 carry the brief reports to prove it. One published count puts the number executed there at 135. In all, 131 British military trials were held in Singapore between January 1946 and March 1948, most of them in the Supreme Court Building.

The trial with the most direct line back to the gaol was the Double Tenth trial, heard from 18 March to 15 April 1946. Twenty-one Kempeitai officers and interpreters stood accused of the torture of the 57 internees. Fourteen were found guilty; eight, including the Singapore Kempeitai commander, Lieutenant Colonel Sumida Haruzo, were sentenced to death by hanging, and three to life imprisonment — though two of the condemned, interpreters who were British subjects, were later retried in the local courts and imprisoned instead. On 15 October 1947 the prison was handed back to the Prisons Department, and the long-sentence men from Pearl's Hill came back to fill it, as they had in 1937.

## The peacetime prison

For the next half-century Changi was simply Singapore's main prison, and its peacetime history is quieter and stranger than its wartime fame suggests. It was the first prison in Singapore with land enough for a farm: from July 1949 a 30-acre prisoner-run farm outside the walls grew vegetables for the prisoners' meals, followed by pigs in 1950, chickens in 1951 and cattle in 1953, until the prison was largely self-sufficient in fresh food. Good-conduct prisoners worked the farm under light supervision, and though there were escapes, the authorities kept it for what it did for the men. A graded earning scheme for prison labour followed in 1950, paying eight to fifteen cents a day. In 1955 Changi introduced corrective training for young offenders and preventive detention for older recidivists — Singapore reportedly the first place in Southeast Asia to do so — and from 1960 the National Library supplied the prison with books in all languages. A Prisons Department report of 1948 had counted 597 cells and reckoned the gaol could hold 1,000 inmates comfortably, a long way from five to a cell. Between the late 1970s and the early 1980s the prison was upgraded with two new five-storey blocks to relieve overcrowding.

The gaol also held the political prisoners of the new nation. Trade unionists and suspected communists were detained there through the 1950s and 1960s, among them many swept up in Operation Cold Store in 1963 — Lim Chin Siong, Fong Swee Suan, Lim Hock Siew, James Puthucheary, Poh Soo Kai, Said Zahari and Devan Nair among the names the National Library Board records. Nair and others arrested in the unrest of October 1956 were released from Changi on 4 June 1959, greeted by more than 2,000 supporters, after Lee Kuan Yew made their release a condition of forming Singapore's first elected government. And through all these decades, hanging remained the gaol's other function: capital punishment was, and in the new complex still is, carried out at Changi.

## The wall and gate were kept

By the late 1990s the government had decided to gather Singapore's scattered prisons into one modern complex on the Changi site. Ground was broken for the new 48-hectare Changi Prison Complex on 31 December 1999 — and the old gaol stood in its way. The plan to demolish it drew resistance from heritage advocates, former prisoners of war and Australian politicians, for whom the building was a war memorial as much as a prison. The compromise, announced in March 2004, was a fragment: the entrance gate, a 180-metre stretch of the perimeter wall and two of the four corner turrets would be kept. Everything else — the cell blocks, the hospital, the yards, the farm — was demolished that year. In August 2004 the new complex's Cluster A was unveiled, and the old prison's inmates were transferred into it along with those of three other institutions. A second cluster opened in January 2010, and the complex can now hold 11,000 prisoners.

![The Prison Link Centre of the present Changi Prison Complex, photographed in 2007](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/b/b2/Changi_Prison%2C_Singapore.jpg)

*The Prison Link Centre of the present Changi Prison Complex, photographed in 2007. (Waycool27, Public domain, via Wikimedia Commons)*

On 15 February 2016 — chosen deliberately, the 74th anniversary of the fall of Singapore — the National Heritage Board gazetted the Changi Prison Entrance Gate, Wall and Turrets as Singapore's 72nd National Monument. Announcing it, the Board's Director of Preservation of Sites and Monuments, Jean Wee, said the prison "stands today as an enduring symbol of the suffering of those who defended Singapore during the tumultuous war years between 1942 and 1945". The monument stands inside a restricted area and is not open to the public; there is an information board on Upper Changi Road North for anyone who goes to look at the wall from the road.

[See more photos of Changi Prison and what survives of it](/gallery/changi-prison-1936-to-2004-the-gaol-whose-wall-and-gate-were-kept/)

**Singapore demolished almost all of its most famous prison and kept the one part every prisoner passed through and none could pass: the gate, a stretch of wall, and the towers that watched it. The gaol is gone; the threshold remains, still guarded, still closed — a lesser-known monument precisely because it can only be read from the road.**
