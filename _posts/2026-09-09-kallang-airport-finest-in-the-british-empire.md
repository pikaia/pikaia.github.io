On the north bank of the Kallang Basin, across the water from the Singapore Sports Hub, stands a low, curved building the colour of pale butter, with a round tower rising from its centre. Its address today is 9 Stadium Link, just off Nicoll Highway. Tens of thousands of people pass it every week on the way to a football match or a run along the promenade, and most of them have no idea what it is. For eighteen years it was the passenger terminal of Kallang Airport, Singapore's first purpose-built civil aerodrome, described in its day as the finest in the British Empire and, by Amelia Earhart, as "an aviation miracle of the East." The airport closed in 1955. The terminal is still there, still empty, still waiting for someone to decide what to do with it.

## "The best site, beyond all question"

Before Kallang, civil aircraft arriving in Singapore shared the Royal Air Force fields at Seletar and Sembawang, both laid out in the 1920s. Civil aviation was growing fast, and the colony wanted an airport of its own, close to town. On 31 August 1931 the Governor of the Straits Settlements, Sir Cecil Clementi, announced where it would go: "an aerodrome which is equally suitable for land planes and for sea planes; and the best site, beyond all question, is the Kallang Basin."

The basin was a tidal swamp where the Kallang, Geylang and Rochor rivers met the sea, fringed by Malay villages built on stilts over the mud. Turning it into an airfield took roughly 300 acres of reclamation, filled largely with earth cut from the surrounding hills, and the resettlement of the families who lived there — many of them moved a short way inland to a new village that took the name Kampung Melayu. The work ran for most of the 1930s. Kallang Airport opened on 12 June 1937, when Sir Shenton Thomas, by then Governor, declared it open to the public.

What made Kallang unusual was that it did two jobs at once. Land planes used a large circular grass field, which let them take off and land into the wind from any direction; flying boats came down on a marked-out stretch of the basin and taxied to a slipway beside the terminal. That mattered in the 1930s, because the trunk route between Britain and Australia — the Imperial Airways and QANTAS "Empire" service — was flown by Short Empire flying boats that put down on water at each stop. Kallang was one of their scheduled halts. Smaller operators used the land field: Wearne's Air Service for the Malayan towns, and the Dutch airline KNILM across to Batavia.

The terminal itself was the work of Frank Dorrington Ward, the chief architect of the Public Works Department, who would go on to design the Old Supreme Court. It was built in the streamline Moderne style of the moment — a symmetrical, gently curved frontage with a cylindrical control tower at the centre and a public viewing gallery on the roof, from which Singaporeans could watch the aircraft for a small fee. The building drew visitors in its own right. When Amelia Earhart stopped at Kallang during her round-the-world attempt in June 1937, a few weeks before she disappeared over the Pacific, she called it "an aviation miracle of the East"; in its early years it was routinely described as the finest airport in the British Empire.

## Eighteen years

The airport had a short life, and much of it was spent at war.

By late 1941 the grass field was RAF Station Kallang, and when the Japanese advanced down Malaya it became the only fighter airfield on Singapore island still in operation. Its Brewster Buffalo squadrons were outmatched from the start; the Hawker Hurricanes that replaced them fared a little better but were too few and came too late. On 9 February 1942, with the field cratered and under artillery fire, the surviving Hurricanes were withdrawn to Sumatra, and Singapore spent its last week without fighter cover. During the occupation the Japanese military replaced the grass landing ground with a concrete runway and extended it to 5,500 feet — a hard runway the pre-war airport had never had.

![Brewster Buffalo fighter aircraft outside a hangar at RAF Station Kallang, 1941](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/7/72/Kallang_1941.jpg)

*Brewster Buffalo fighters outside the Kallang hangar in 1941, in the months before the Japanese invasion. The Buffalo was no match for the aircraft it met over Malaya and Singapore. (Air Force Museum of New Zealand, William Christiansen album; released under Creative Commons Zero, via Wikimedia Commons.)*

Civil flights resumed properly in 1949, and for a few years Kallang was busy again: a Straits Times headline in April 1950 called it the second-busiest airport in the Far East, handling the propeller airliners of BOAC, KLM, Qantas, Air France and the young Malayan Airways. The runway was lengthened once more to take the first BOAC Comet jets in 1951, but hemmed in by the sea on one side and the city on the other, it was never going to be long enough for what was coming.

The airport also saw its share of the era's larger events. On 13 March 1954 a BOAC Lockheed Constellation flying the Sydney–London route came in short of the runway at Kallang, struck the seawall and burst into flames; 33 of the 40 people on board were killed. The public inquiry put the crash down to a fatigued crew — the captain had been on duty more than 21 hours — and found the airport's fire and rescue service partly responsible for the death toll. Two years later, on 18 March 1956, some 20,000 people gathered at the terminal for a *merdeka* (independence) rally addressed by Chief Minister David Marshall; it turned into a riot as the crowd attacked police and tried to force their way into the building, and at least fifty people were hurt.

## Paya Lebar, and the end of the field

There was no room to make Kallang bigger, so the government built a replacement inland. Singapore International Airport at Paya Lebar opened in 1955 with a runway more than 8,000 feet long, and Kallang Airport closed to traffic that year, eighteen years after it opened. The name lingered on the map: the road along the old runway became Old Airport Road, and the MRT station built there decades later was named Dakota, after the Douglas DC-3 airliners that had once used the field.

## What the ground became

The airfield never went back to being anything small. Nicoll Highway was cut across the northern edge of the old airport in 1956. The terminal building and its side blocks passed to the Singapore Youth Sports Council, and from 1960 to 2009 the main building housed the headquarters of the People's Association; between 1967 and 1972 part of the premises was the Central Manpower Base, where a generation of young men reported for their national service medicals and enlistment. In 1973 the National Stadium opened on what had been the runway area, and for the next three decades the "Kallang Roar" and the "Kallang Wave" belonged to a football ground built on an airfield. That stadium closed in 2007 and was pulled down; the Singapore Sports Hub opened on the same ground in 2014.

![The former Kallang Airport terminal building seen head-on in 2021, empty, with its symmetrical curved wings and central control tower](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/f/f3/Kallang_Airport_Terminal.jpg)

*The former terminal in 2021, empty and awaiting a new use. The symmetrical streamline frontage, the rooftop viewing deck and the round control tower are all intact, with the window frames restored in the original green. (Photo: Gaodanielbo / Wikimedia Commons, released under Creative Commons Zero.)*

The terminal itself was restored and reopened in March 1994 after conservation works costing S$4.16 million, with the green-tinted windows, the steps down to the old Kallang Road entrance and the airport's "lion against a coconut tree" emblem all put back. It was formally gazetted for conservation on 5 December 2008 and served as a venue for the Singapore Biennale in 2011. Since the People's Association moved out in 2009, though, it has mostly stood empty, and the Urban Redevelopment Authority's long-term plans for the district fold the building into a heritage and lifestyle precinct, provisionally called Old Airport Square, within the wider redevelopment of the area as Kallang Riverside. For now it is a listed building in search of a tenant.

**Where it fits in the bigger story:** Singapore has kept the shell of Kallang Airport and lost almost everything it was for. The terminal that once stood for the colony's arrival in the modern world — the rooftop viewing gallery, the Empire flying boats on the basin, Earhart's "miracle of the East" — is now a conserved façade waiting on a master plan, on ground far better known for football than for flight.
