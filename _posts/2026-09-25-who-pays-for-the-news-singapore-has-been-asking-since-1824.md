Singapore's first newspaper was printed on rough Chinese-made paper, folded into four small pages and sold for 18 Spanish dollars a year. It got through its first five years on a government subsidy of 60 Spanish dollars a month, and once that subsidy was withdrawn and competitors arrived, it did not last another decade. Nearly two centuries later, the question that decided the Singapore Chronicle's fate, who pays for local news in a small market, is being answered again, and public money is once more part of the answer.

## A gazette with a subsidy

The Singapore Chronicle's first issue appeared on 1 January 1824. The British Resident, John Crawfurd, had applied in mid-1823 for permission to publish it on behalf of Francis James Bernard, a former master attendant who then ran the police department, and Crawfurd himself was its principal contributor, so the paper functioned as a semi-official gazette. It was printed at first by the Mission Press and later on its own press at 26 Commercial Square, on a single sheet folded into four quarto pages of three columns each, and it came out fortnightly before moving to a weekly schedule in 1830.

It was a paper for the port. The Chronicle carried official notifications, advertisements, editorial notes on local events, news from other countries, letters from correspondents and lists of imports and exports. A year's subscription cost 18 Spanish dollars and a single copy cost 50 cents, at a time when the Spanish dollar was still the coin the whole town ran on. But subscriptions did not cover its costs. The editor also received a fixed government subsidy of 60 Spanish dollars a month, which was withdrawn in 1829 in order to save money.

## Competition arrives

The larger change came in 1835, when restrictions on publishing were removed. That October, the Singapore Free Press and Mercantile Advertiser began, in response to the sale of the Chronicle to a new owner. Its founders included William Napier and Edward Boustead, and its first editor was John Henry Moor, who had until then edited the Chronicle. The older paper, now facing a rival edited by its own former editor, ceased publication after 30 September 1837.

## The paper nobody wanted to buy

The Straits Times and Singapore Journal of Commerce launched on 15 July 1845 at 7 Commercial Square, a decade after the Free Press began. It was the work of an editor from Bombay, Robert Carr Woods, and an Armenian merchant, Catchick Moses, who had bought printing equipment originally ordered by a fellow Armenian merchant, Marterus Thaddeus Apcar, before Apcar died without realising his own publishing plans. The first issue ran to eight folio pages, printed in new type on fine English paper, and sold for half a Spanish dollar. It began as a Tuesday weekly and went to twice weekly within four months, though in a reduced size.

Woods had struggled to convince Moses that the paper could make money. By September 1846, only fourteen months after launch, Moses tried to sell it and found no buyers.

## Two centuries later

The same question is now being asked of a much larger industry, in a country of roughly six million people. The publisher of the Straits Times, Lianhe Zaobao, Berita Harian and Tamil Murasu has been restructured into a not-for-profit body, SPH Media Trust, which took over the media business on 1 December 2021. On 16 February 2022 the government announced that it would provide up to S$900 million over five years, about S$180 million a year, tied to targets for the reach and engagement of its products, including among vernacular-language readers and young people. The Ministry of Digital Development and Information reported that in the 2024 financial year the trust received S$260.6 million in base funding, plus up to S$28.9 million in performance-linked incentives that depend on meeting agreed targets. The trust also disclosed in 2023 that its reported daily circulation for 2022 had been inflated by between 85,000 and 95,000 copies, or 10 to 12 percent, through double counting and other methods.

Television is on a similar footing. Mediacorp, owned by the state investment company Temasek, receives about S$380 million a year in government allocations, the minister for digital development, Josephine Teo, told Parliament in October 2025. She set that against the roughly S$780 million a year received by the national broadcasters of Finland and Denmark, which she said have similar population sizes, and said Mediacorp reached more than 90 percent of Singapore's population across its own and social media platforms. The government, she added, now measures reach across all platforms rather than television alone, reflecting how audience habits have changed.

The figures for online audiences are of a different kind again. CNA, Mediacorp's news brand, reported nearly 12 million unique visitors a month to its website between January and July 2025, according to its own analytics. The Straits Times put its website online in 1994 and introduced a paywall in 2005. Visitors, subscribers and printed copies are not the same thing, so these numbers cannot be ranked against each other, but they show that the traditional and the broadcast newsrooms are now competing for attention in the same place, and that both are supported with public money.

The stated rationale for that support is about trust and reach rather than control. Minister Teo has said that "no one gains if these products lack credibility and are ignored by audiences." Research for the Reuters Institute for the Study of Journalism, drawing on interviews with journalists, recorded concerns about deference to authority and government communications influence in newsrooms, while an SPH Media editor countered that the relationship with the government had not changed after the restructuring. Whether public funding preserves a local view of events or shapes it is a question people answer differently, and the Chronicle's story suggests it is an old one.

**Where it fits in the bigger story:** In 1824, a colonial government paid a printer 60 dollars a month so that its notices and the port's shipping lists would be published, and after the subsidy ended and competition arrived the paper folded. In 1846 the paper nobody would buy was the one that survived. Small markets have always struggled to pay for their own news, and Singapore's two centuries of newspapers show the same tension between subsidy and the open market from the very first issue to the present.
