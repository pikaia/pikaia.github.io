A $10 note from the Japanese Occupation, its face printed with a bunch of bananas, sells today to collectors for a few dollars — which is, almost to the cent, what it was actually worth by the time Japan surrendered in 1945. The note promises "THE JAPANESE GOVERNMENT PROMISES TO PAY THE BEARER ON DEMAND TEN DOLLARS." It never did. Within three and a half years, it went from being worth exactly as much as the currency it replaced to not being worth the paper it was printed on — literally.

*A $10 Japanese Occupation note for Malaya and Borneo, 1944 — the banana-tree design that gave "banana money" its name. (Photo: National Numismatic Collection, National Museum of American History, Smithsonian Institution, CC BY-SA 4.0, via Wikimedia Commons)*

After Singapore fell on 15 February 1942, the Japanese military administration introduced its own currency to replace the Straits and Malayan dollars, at first pegged at par with them. The earliest notes — cents through $10 — carried the usual anti-counterfeiting features: serial numbers, a watermark, a security thread. The $10 note's design, a bunch of bananas under a banana tree, gave the whole currency its lasting nickname. Malays had their own name for it, reportedly ruder than the English one and unprintable in the papers of the day.

The problem was never the design. It was that nothing backed the currency except Japan's word, and Japan printed more of it every time it needed money — to pay troops, to pay for requisitioned goods, to fund a war it was steadily losing. There was no gold, no silver, no limit. As the printing accelerated, the security features were the first thing to go: later notes carried no serial numbers at all, and counterfeiting became rampant on top of the official inflation. New, larger denominations followed the money down — a $100 note by 1944, a $1,000 note by 1945, for a currency that had started out topping at $10.

The results were exactly what that combination produces. An egg that cost 3 cents before the war cost $100 by the end of the occupation. A hundred catties of rice — about 60 kilograms — went from $5 to $5,000. By August 1945, the Japanese administration had issued something like $4,000 million worth of the currency, against a normal pre-war circulation for the whole region of about $220 million: nearly twenty times the money chasing the same goods.

Then, on 8 September 1945, less than a month after Japan's surrender, the British Military Administration announced what everyone already suspected: banana money would be given no value whatsoever. There was no exchange rate to negotiate down to, no gradual write-off — the currency simply ceased to be money. Anyone who had quietly kept pre-war Straits or Malayan dollars through the occupation had those honoured the moment the British returned. Anyone whose entire savings existed only in banana money woke up with nothing. Japan has never compensated holders of the notes, then or since.

<div style="clear: both;"></div>

What happened to the actual paper afterwards is its own small irony. Banks and buildings across the city were still sitting on vaults and basements full of it — by one account, more than 400 tons of it, some 20,000 cases, found in Singapore alone. Rather than let it circulate as a curiosity, the British had it repulped into newsprint. The same notes that, months earlier, hadn't been enough to buy an egg became the paper people read the next morning's news on. The demonetisation also left a legal mess that took years to sort out: whether debts paid in banana money during the occupation counted as paid at all became a real courtroom question, still being argued as a High Court test case in 1949 and settled by a Debtor and Creditor (Occupation) ordinance only toward the end of that decade.

**Why it matters today:** the collectors who now pay a few dollars for a "banana" ten-dollar note are, in a strange way, finally paying it its 1945 exchange value. The currency was never really money in the way the Straits dollar it replaced was money — it was a wartime promise printed on demand, worth exactly as much as the government behind it, and once that government surrendered, so did the promise.
