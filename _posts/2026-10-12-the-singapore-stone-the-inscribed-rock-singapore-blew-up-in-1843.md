In January 1843, on the orders of the man in charge of Singapore's public works, a great slab of red sandstone at the mouth of the Singapore River was blown apart to clear the ground for Fort Fullerton. The slab had stood on the point since long before the British arrived, and its face carried about fifty lines of carved writing. It was the only ancient inscription ever found on the island, and in more than two hundred years of trying, nobody has managed to read it.

## Found in the clearing

In June 1819, a few months after Stamford Raffles founded the settlement, workers clearing the jungle at the point on the south side of the river mouth uncovered the slab. According to Dr Montgomerie, one of the colony's first surgeons, the men who found it were Bengali sailors working for Captain Flint, the settlement's first Master Attendant. They were frightened by the writing and refused to go on clearing, and Chinese workers had to finish the job.

![A hand-coloured survey map of the Singapore River winding to the sea, with the town on both banks and a headland at the river mouth labelled Rocky point](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/2/2d/Part_of_Singapore_Island_%28British_Library_India_Office_Records%2C_1825%2C_detail%29_-_cropped.jpg/1280px-Part_of_Singapore_Island_%28British_Library_India_Office_Records%2C_1825%2C_detail%29_-_cropped.jpg)

*A survey map of 1825 marking "Rocky point" at the mouth of the Singapore River, where the stone stood. (Unknown author, British Library India Office Records, public domain, via Wikimedia Commons)*

The stone was about three metres high and three metres wide. It was not a single block but half of a great boulder that had been split in two, and the two halves leaned towards each other at an angle of about 40 degrees. The writing was cut into the flat inner face of one half: some fifty lines inside a raised rim, covering an area about 1.5 metres high and 2.1 metres wide.

## Everyone came to look

<div style="float: right; max-width: 220px; width: 36%; margin: 0.25em 0 1em 1.5em;">
<img src="https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/f/f2/Artist_Unknown%2C_Portrait_of_John_Crawfurd%2C_British_Resident_of_Singapore_%281823%E2%80%931826%29%2C_1857%2C_Oil_on_canvas%2C_210_x_134_cm%2C_National_Museum_Singapore.jpg" alt="A painted portrait of an elderly man with white hair in a dark coat, seated in a red chair with papers beside him" style="width: 100%; display: block; border-radius: 4px;">
<em style="display: block; font-size: 0.8em; margin-top: 0.5em;">John Crawfurd, who examined the stone in 1822 and later served as Resident of Singapore, in a portrait of 1857 now in the National Museum of Singapore. (Artist unknown, public domain, via Wikimedia Commons)</em>
</div>

The first careful description came from John Crawfurd, who stopped in Singapore on his way to Siam as the Governor-General's envoy and examined the stone early on the morning of 4 February 1822. He found the carving far rougher than anything he had seen in Java or India, and the writing so worn, more by the decay of the rock than by age, that it was "quite illegible as a composition". Here and there a few letters still stood out. He guessed that the script was Pali, the religious script of the Buddhists.

Munshi Abdullah, the Malay scribe whose memoir is one of the few local accounts of these years, remembered that people of every community crowded round the stone as soon as it was found. The Hindus said the writing was Hindu and the Chinese said it was Chinese. Abdullah went with Raffles and the missionary Claudius Thomsen, and from the shape of the letters he thought it might be Arabic, but he could not read it either. Visitors pressed flour and lard into the grooves and lifted them out, hoping to get the shape of the letters, and others poured a black liquid over the face, without success. Raffles himself, according to an account published in 1834, tried strong acids to bring out the characters, and that failed too. (The [Munshi Abdullah post](/2026/10/10/munshi-abdullah-the-scribe-who-watched-singapore-begin/) tells the rest of his story.)

<div style="clear: both;"></div>

## The strongman's stone

![A page of a Malay manuscript in Jawi script, written in black ink with some words picked out in red](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/2/2d/Excerpt_from_the_Sulalatus_Salatin_manuscript%2C_copied_in_the_year_1808%2C_chapter_on_the_Death_of_Hang_Tuah.jpg)

*A page from a copy of the Sejarah Melayu, the Malay Annals, made in 1808. The Annals tell the story of Badang, the strongman of old Singapura. (Osman bin Kuri bin Amat Utat, the copyist, CC0, via Wikimedia Commons)*

The Sejarah Melayu, the court chronicle of the Malay kings of Singapura and Melaka, has a story that many people have connected with the stone. Badang was a slave who cleared forest for his master. One day he caught a spirit in his fishing net, and in return for its freedom the spirit made him the strongest man in the land. The king of Singapura brought him to court and made him a commander. When a king in India sent his own champion to challenge him, the contest was to lift a huge rock that lay in front of the palace. The Indian champion lifted it and dropped it, but Badang lifted it and threw it towards the mouth of the Singapore River.

From the 1830s, writers on Singapore linked the inscribed slab with this story, either as the rock that Badang threw or as one of the stones said to have marked his grave. Nothing beyond the legend ties the two together, and the stone itself has never been read well enough to settle it.

## The blast and what was saved

<div style="float: left; max-width: 240px; width: 38%; margin: 0.25em 1.5em 1em 0;">
<img src="https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/8/88/SingaporeStone-drawings.jpg" alt="Black and white drawings of three irregular stone fragments, each covered in rows of looped and curling letters" style="width: 100%; display: block; border-radius: 4px;">
<em style="display: block; font-size: 0.8em; margin-top: 0.5em;">Drawings of three of the surviving fragments, published in Calcutta in 1848. The lowest one is the piece now in the National Museum of Singapore. (Possibly J. W. Laidlay, public domain, via Wikimedia Commons)</em>
</div>

In November 1842, Captain D. H. Stevenson took over as Superintendent of Convicts and Public Works. Two months later he ordered the stone blasted, because the point was needed for the commander's quarters at Fort Fullerton, the fort that guarded the river mouth. Antiquarians protested, among them Lieutenant-Colonel James Low of the East India Company, but the work went ahead.

The blame was often laid on someone else. The 1921 history *One Hundred Years of Singapore* called the man responsible "a vandal at the head of the Public Works Department", and some accounts named George Drumgoole Coleman, the architect who had held that post. But Coleman had left for Europe in 1841, and the order was Stevenson's.

After the explosion, Low picked out the fragments with the most legible writing and had a Chinese mason chisel them down into smaller slabs. He gave one to the Governor, Samuel George Bonham, and kept three. Bonham's piece did not stay safe for long: it ended up as a seat for the guards at the Treasury, until his successor, William John Butterworth, recognised it and took it into his own house. Other pieces fared worse. One merchant later recalled a large fragment lying at the corner of Government House on Government Hill, today's Fort Canning, which convict labourers broke up to build a road.

Abdullah's verdict on the whole affair was blunt. He thought it was done from "ignorance or stupidity", that now "we can never know the nature of this ancient writing", and he ended with a Malay saying: "What you can't make, don't break."

## Calcutta and back

![A large two-storey colonial building with arched windows and a balustraded roof, on a wide street with lamp posts and a few pedestrians](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/1/1b/The_Museum%2C_Chowringhee.jpg/1280px-The_Museum%2C_Chowringhee.jpg)

*The museum on Chowringhee in Calcutta, today's Indian Museum, in a photograph published in 1905. The fragments of the Singapore Stone went into its collection. (India Illustrated, public domain, via Wikimedia Commons)*

In 1848 J. W. Laidlay of the Asiatic Society of Bengal asked for the fragments, so that the Society could study them. Butterworth's piece left Singapore on 19 February 1848 aboard the barque *Rob Roy*, and Low sent his three after it. By July all four were in Calcutta, where Laidlay swept fine charcoal dust into the worn grooves to make the letters stand out and drew them for the Society's journal. The fragments later passed to the Calcutta museum.

They stayed there for seventy years. In 1918 the Raffles Museum in Singapore asked for them back. Calcutta returned one fragment, on indefinite loan, and it arrived in 1919, the year Singapore marked the centenary of its founding. What happened to the others is not known.

## Still unread

<div style="float: right; max-width: 220px; width: 36%; margin: 0.25em 0 1em 1.5em;">
<img src="https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/1/13/Pengkalan_Kempas_tomb_Kawi_script_south_side_in_1919_by_C._Boden_Kloss.png/1280px-Pengkalan_Kempas_tomb_Kawi_script_south_side_in_1919_by_C._Boden_Kloss.png" alt="A tall stone slab with a rounded hole near its base and several lines of dark curling script carved across its upper part" style="width: 100%; display: block; border-radius: 4px;">
<em style="display: block; font-size: 0.8em; margin-top: 0.5em;">A stone carved in the Kawi script, the old writing of Java and Sumatra, at Pengkalan Kempas in Negeri Sembilan, photographed in 1919. Scholars have compared the Singapore Stone with inscriptions like this one. (C. Boden Kloss, public domain, via Wikimedia Commons)</em>
</div>

The scholars who have tried to read the stone have not even agreed on what language it is in. Bland, who made the 1837 copy, called the script "ancient Ceylonese, or Pali", and James Prinsep, the Calcutta scholar who deciphered the ancient Brahmi script of India, partly agreed. Laidlay, working on the fragments in 1848, thought it was Kawi, the old script of Java, and suggested it might record a Javanese victory.

Later Dutch and Indonesian scholars each came to a different answer. J. H. C. Kern made out a few words and dated the writing to around 1230. N. J. Krom thought it looked like the script of Majapahit, the Javanese empire, but from before 1360. J. G. de Casparis suggested the 10th or 11th century and possibly Old Javanese. Boechari placed it no later than the 12th century, with links to Sumatra, and suggested the language was Sanskrit rather than Javanese.

John Miksic, the archaeologist best known for his work on Fort Canning, has argued that the person who had it carved was more likely Sumatran than Javanese, and that the inscription will probably never be fully deciphered. The National Museum dates it to between the 10th and 14th centuries and describes it as written in Kawi with some Sanskrit words.

The newest readings keep the question open. In 2019 the researcher Iain Sinclair reported a sequence of letters, "kesariva", that he suggested could be part of Parakesarivarman, a title used by several kings of the Chola dynasty of south India. If that is right, the stone could date from the early 11th century. It remains one researcher's proposal. In 2023 the linguists I-Shiang Lee and Francesco Perono Cacciafoco compared the stone's letters with Kawi writing and found close similarities, but also differences in how the vowel marks were used.

## A National Treasure

![A large weathered fragment of reddish sandstone in a dark museum case, its surface covered in faint rows of carved letters](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/0/08/SingaporeStone-NationalMuseumofSingapore-20090712.jpg/1280px-SingaporeStone-NationalMuseumofSingapore-20090712.jpg)

*The returned fragment on display at the National Museum of Singapore in 2009. (Jon Callas from San Jose, California, USA, CC BY 2.0, via Wikimedia Commons)*

The piece that came back from Calcutta is about 74 centimetres wide, a small part of the slab that Crawfurd saw. It is the only fragment in Singapore, and in 2006 the National Museum of Singapore named it one of its 11 National Treasures. It is on show in the museum's Singapore History Gallery, where it is described as the oldest writing ever found in Singapore.

The stone also turns up in the country's public celebrations. The 2016 National Day Parade told the story of Badang and showed him lifting a glowing stone covered in letters. And in 1972 the Merlion statue was unveiled at the mouth of the river, a short distance from where the slab had stood. (The [Merlion post](/2026/08/19/two-men-behind-singapore-merlion-statue/) covers that statue's story.)

## The point today

![An aerial view of a grand granite building with tall columns on the waterfront at the mouth of a river, with office towers behind it](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/7/7a/Singapore_%28SG%29%2C_The_Fullerton_Hotel_--_2019_--_4708.jpg/1280px-Singapore_%28SG%29%2C_The_Fullerton_Hotel_--_2019_--_4708.jpg)

*The Fullerton Hotel, built as the Fullerton Building on the site of Fort Fullerton, photographed in 2019. The stone stood on the point at the river mouth beside it. (Dietmar Rabich, CC BY-SA 4.0, via Wikimedia Commons)*

Fort Fullerton, the fort the stone was cleared for, has gone too. Its site is now the Fullerton Building, opened in 1928 as the General Post Office and today the Fullerton Hotel (the [Fullerton Building post](/2026/09/20/the-fullerton-building-the-post-office-with-a-lighthouse-on-the-roof/) tells its story). Nothing on the waterfront marks the place where the stone stood.

In 1843 there was no law in Singapore to protect an old monument, and the stone stood in the way of a building. That changed in the early 1970s, when the Preservation of Monuments Act set up a board to protect historic sites, and the first eight national monuments were gazetted in 1973. Today the National Heritage Board's Preservation of Sites and Monuments division looks after more than 70 of them. The Singapore Stone came too early for any of that: what survives is one fragment in a museum case, a set of drawings and a copy made with dough.

[See 10 more historical photos related to this post →](/gallery/the-singapore-stone-the-inscribed-rock-singapore-blew-up-in-1843/)

**Why it matters today:** Singapore's written history is usually said to begin with Raffles in 1819, and its older past with a few lines in the Malay Annals. The Singapore Stone is the one piece of writing from that older past that was actually found on the island, carved centuries before the British arrived. Most of it was lost in a morning in 1843, and the fragment that survives still has not been read. It is a reminder that the record of early Singapore is thinner than it needs to be, partly because of decisions made by the people who were building the new town.
