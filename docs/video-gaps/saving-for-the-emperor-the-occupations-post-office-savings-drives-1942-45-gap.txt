=== slide 27 (SS440424): 32.9s [416.2s - 449.1s] ===
When savings passed $5 million on the 21st of April 1944, depositors who showed their passbooks at the post office were given free tickets to cinema shows from the 28th of April to the 7th of May. The notice said the shows were meant "to enable the depositors also to celebrate Tencho Setsu", the Emperor's birthday on the 29th of April. There was also a competition to guess the date on which postal savings would pass $10 million.

=== slide 28 (SS440208): 34.9s [449.1s - 484.0s] ===
The banks' own campaign used prize draws. Under the Malai Banking Association's "Fixed Deposit with Bonus" scheme, which ran from the 15th of February to the 15th of March 1944, every $100 placed on fixed deposit for a year earned a lottery number. Each block of $2 million in deposits carried prizes of $1,000, $500, $100 and $50, and one number across the whole scheme would win a special prize of $10,000.

=== slide 31 (RATION): 34.4s [508.9s - 543.2s] ===
As the war turned against Japan, the drives reached further into workplaces. In Penang in October 1944, the Controller of Posts told the managers of large firms, trade associations and amusement and gambling parks that "with the war now reaching a decisive stage" their help "should be increased manifold to ensure final victory". Under a new group savings system, the managers would collect savings from their staff and stallholders and send the names and amounts to the post office each month.

=== slide 33 (chart): 38.6s [566.5s - 605.1s] ===
On paper, the drives worked. The Syonan Shimbun reported that postal savings across Malaya passed $5 million in April 1944 and $10 million in October. At the end of 1944 they stood at $16.6 million in 404,271 accounts. At the end of June 1945 they stood at $30.2 million in 506,950 accounts, against a pre-war peak, the paper said, of 180,000 depositors.

=== slide 45 (ST1947): 32.1s [788.4s - 820.5s] ===
The position did not change. In February 1947 savings bank officials in Kuala Lumpur told the Straits Times that deposits made during the Occupation were "still considered worthless", and that any account opened in the "Dai Nippon Savings Bank of Malai" was "not recognised as a liability today". They added that withdrawals made from the old British accounts during the Occupation had been treated as withdrawals "for value", so they stood.
