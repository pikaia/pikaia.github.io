## Summary

10 ranked candidates on an **energy** theme, prompted by the week's news: on **24 October 2026** Indonesia's upstream regulator SKK Migas cut pipeline natural gas exports to Singapore by about 31 million standard cubic feet per day (15 from Natuna, 16 from Sumatra), diverting the gas to Indonesia's own power sector (Batam, Bintan and Sumatra, after a 16-hour blackout in the Riau Islands earlier in the month). The posts themselves are history pieces in the channel's usual style — the news is the hook, not the subject.

**The facts of the hook, as verified today (26 Oct 2026):** Singapore generates **about 95% of its electricity from natural gas**. In 2025 roughly **40–43% of its gas imports arrived by pipeline** (from Indonesia and Malaysia) and the rest as **LNG** shipped in from world markets. Singapore's Energy Market Authority (EMA) said importers reported a reduction of **17 BBTUD over 18 days — less than 2% of total daily gas demand** (which exceeds 1,000 BBTUD) — that electricity supply would remain stable, and that Singapore GasCo stood ready to import more LNG. Prime Minister Lawrence Wong said Singapore expects commercial contracts to be honoured and pointed to the LNG terminal as exactly the diversification built for this situation; Minister Tan See Leng said electricity prices were unlikely to be affected. Indonesia's energy minister replied that deliveries still meet the contracts' minimum levels. **Framing note for the channel:** the honest headline is not "crisis" but "the scare that the system absorbed" — every candidate below connects to that story of how Singapore came to burn imported gas, and how it insured itself. Nothing below criticises Singapore's government or energy policy; Indonesia's domestic needs are stated factually, as reported.

### 1. The Pipelines That Feed Singapore: Malaysia 1992, West Natuna 2001, Grissik 2003
**Story:** Singapore's gas age arrived under the sea, in three deals. The first trans-border gas pipeline in Southeast Asia was completed in **1992**, bringing Malaysian gas from Kertih, Terengganu, down the peninsula and across the Johor Strait to **Senoko Power Station**. On **15 January 1999** Pertamina and its West Natuna contractors (Conoco, Gulf, Premier Oil) signed a **22-year** gas sale agreement with Sembawang's SembGas consortium — 325 million cubic feet a day through a ~640 km pipeline, worth over **US$8 billion** in gross revenue; first gas flowed in **January 2001**, months ahead of schedule. A second Indonesian deal followed from **Grissik and South Sumatra** (350 MMcfd for 20 years, ~US$1 billion invested, first gas mid-2003, sold to Singapore Power). These contracts — extended and renegotiated since — are the very pipes in this week's news: the Natuna line lands at Sembcorp Gas's receiving facility, the Sumatra line feeds Gas Supply Pte Ltd, and both were reported this week as running to 2028.
**Tags:** Systems; Money
**Connection to today's focus:** direct — this post *is* the history of the supply now being trimmed, and it ends where the news begins: a system built on three pipelines from two neighbours, later balanced by LNG (candidate 4).
**Images: workable.** There is no meaningful period photo pool of offshore pipeline-laying that is freely licensed, and press photography of the receiving stations is in copyright. What carries this post is cartography, which suits it: self-made OSM-based route maps of the three pipelines (Kertih→Senoko; West Natuna→Sakra/Jurong; Grissik→Batam→Singapore) are the natural hero visuals, supplemented by Commons' general LNG/pipeline map material, present-day CC photos of Senoko and Jurong Island, and charts of the gas share of electricity generation (<20% in 2000 → ~95% today). **Key facts to check before drafting:** exact first-gas dates and contract extensions/renegotiations since 2001 (the 2023 Sembcorp–Indonesia deal reported by Rigzone is one); the reported 2002 supply disruption on the West Natuna line (an emergency-valve fault that blacked out parts of Singapore — trade-press sourced, verify against EMA/NLB before dramatising); which entity imports what today (Sembcorp Gas vs Gas Supply vs Singapore GasCo, the central procurer set up in 2025). **Sources:** Oil & Gas Journal, "W. Natuna gas project marks progress" — https://www.ogj.com/home/article/17225981/w-natuna-gas-project-marks-progress ; Petromindo, "Pertamina to sign gas deal with Singapore Power" — https://www.petromindo.com/news/article/pertamina-to-sign-gas-deal-with-singapore-power ; Premier Oil, "First gas from West Natuna" (15 Jan 2001) — https://www.petromindo.com/news/article/premier-oil-first-gas-from-west-natuna ; ASEAN Centre for Energy pipeline survey (Malaysia–Singapore pipeline, 1992) — https://1library.net/article/cross-border-pipeline-s-natural-gas-pipeline-development.zpmedp0z ; Reuters (24 Oct 2026) — https://www.reuters.com/business/energy/indonesia-cuts-pipeline-gas-exports-singapore-by-about-9-2026-10-24/

### 2. Pulau Bukom: The Kerosene Island That Became Singapore's First Refinery
**Story:** Before Singapore refined oil, it stored it. In **1891** M. Samuel & Co. of London, importing Russian kerosene for Asian distribution, set up a petroleum tank depot on Pulau Bukom through local agents Syme & Co. — the first of its kind in the East — and the enterprise became part of Shell Transport and Trading (1897), then Royal Dutch Shell (1907). By the 1950s Bukom was Southeast Asia's biggest centre for oil storage, blending, packing and bunkering. On **26 July 1961** Shell opened Singapore's **first oil refinery** there (about US$30 million), officially opened by Finance Minister **Goh Keng Swee** — and the refinery earned Shell the government's **"Pioneer Certificate No. 1"**, the first pioneer-industry award of the industrialisation drive. Some 5,000 people, mostly Shell families, lived on the island in the early 1960s. The coda is live: Shell ran a strategic review of its Bukom/Jurong energy and chemicals assets from 2023, with a sale widely reported — check the outcome before drafting.
**Tags:** Money; Places; Systems
**Connection to today's focus:** the deep backstory — Singapore's energy role began with imported fuel for other people's lamps and ships; the refining/trading hub built on Bukom is why energy shocks here are economic events, and why gas-fired power sits inside a much older import-and-trade system.
**Images: workable.** Commons holds ~43 file hits for Pulau Bukom: distant panoramas and views from Sentosa, worker-accommodation and mosque photographs — enough for establishing shots of the island, but no freely licensed close-ups of the refinery itself (Shell/NAS imagery is rights-held — do not use). **Map/chart load:** strong — an OSM map of Bukom and the southern islands, and a chart of Singapore's refining capacity growth from 1961, carry the industrial sections; the 1891–1961 story can also draw on pre-1956 NewspaperSG pages for the kerosene era. **Key facts to check:** the Pioneer Certificate wording and date; resident population figures; the final outcome and buyer of Shell's 2023–25 divestment (reported bidders included Sinopec, Vitol, Trafigura and a Glencore-linked venture — verify the completed transaction, not the shortlist). **Sources:** NLB, "Shell opens Singapore's first oil refinery at Pulau Bukom" — https://www.nlb.gov.sg/main/article-detail?cmsuuid=37c6a7dd-c7de-4378-819e-e2407839f8d5 ; NHB Roots, "Shell in Singapore" — https://www.roots.gov.sg/Collection-Landing/listing/1579795 ; DPM Lee Hsien Loong's speech at Shell's centenary (NAS, 1991) — https://www.nas.gov.sg/archivesonline/data/pdfdoc/lhl19910902s.pdf ; Reuters via Global Banking & Finance (2023 review) — https://www.globalbankingandfinance.com/shell-taps-goldman-sachs-to-explore-singapore-refinery-sale-sources
**Coverage flag:** Shell's centenary material exists, but the 1891 kerosene depot and the Pioneer Certificate story are not mainstream heritage fare; the island itself is restricted and little photographed by the public, which is part of the appeal.

### 3. St James Power Station: The Coal-Fired Cathedral of Power, 1926
**Story:** Singapore's first large power station rose on reclaimed tidal swamp at Cape Saint James, on land that had once belonged to the Temenggong. Work began in 1924; the first unit was commissioned on **30 September 1926**, and Governor **Sir Hugh Clifford** officially opened the station on **7 November 1927**. At full capacity it generated **22,000 kW** — against the 3,200 kW of everything that came before — yet its early years were plagued by breakdowns and blackouts. It burned coal shipped in by sea and rail, and for its first decade the coal came largely from **Manchuria, supplied by Japanese firms** that had won the tenders, until that supply stopped in the late 1930s as Japan's war expanded, and Malayan coal took over. Reopened after improvement works by Goh Keng Swee in 1960, it shut in **1976**, became a warehouse, then a famous multi-venue nightclub (2006–2018), was gazetted a **national monument in 2009**, and is now **Dyson's global headquarters**, with a heritage gallery.
**Tags:** Places; Systems; Japan angle (the Manchurian coal contracts)
**Connection to today's focus:** the coal chapter of the same dependency story — Singapore has *always* imported its fuel; what changed across a century is the fuel (coal → oil → gas) and the number of suppliers.
**Images: workable †.** Commons holds multiple CC photographs of the building today (exterior, night shots, the chimney) — a strong present-day pool for a gazetted monument — but period operational photography is thin; the pre-1956 rule helps: NewspaperSG whole pages for the 1927 opening and the station's early troubles count as cleared stills. **Map/chart load:** good — a chart of capacity growth (3,200 kW → 22,000 kW) and an OSM site map of the Keppel/Telok Blangah shore. **Copyright:** the Dyson-era interior and gallery photography in press coverage is not free — Commons building shots only. **Key facts to check:** the "Singapore's first power station" label needs the channel's usual care — the Tanjong Pagar Dock Company's **Mackenzie Road station (1906)** preceded it for port/tram supply (see candidate 9); St James was the first built for general public supply. Verify the Manchurian-coal detail against NLB's St James material before leaning on it. **Sources:** Wikipedia (starting point) — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St_James_Power_Station ; NHB national monuments feature via Wonderwall — http://wonderwall.sg/places/national-monuments-of-singapore-st-james-power-station ; Docomomo Singapore, "Industrial Heritage II – Power Stations and Jurong Industrial Estate" — https://www.docomomo.sg/happenings/industrial-heritage-ii-power-stations-and-jurong-industrial-estate
**Coverage flag:** ⚠ partial — as a *building* it is well covered by NHB (national monument, heritage gallery). The lesser-known material is the *energy* story: the fuel supply chain, the early blackouts, and the capacity jump. Lead with those, not the architecture.

### 4. The LNG Terminal: The Insurance Policy Singapore Built in 2013
**Story:** Until 2013, every molecule of Singapore's gas arrived by pipeline from two neighbours. The decision to break that dependence was taken in **2006**; the **S$1.7 billion** Singapore LNG terminal on Jurong Island was built from 2010 and began commercial operations on **7 May 2013**, deliberately sized with spare capacity so it could, if needed, meet the country's entire gas demand. Its effect is measurable: in 2025 LNG supplied roughly **57–60%** of Singapore's gas imports against the pipelines' 40–43%, and the terminal now supplies about half the gas used for power generation. This month it performed exactly as designed — EMA's response to the Indonesian cut was that GasCo could import more LNG at any time, and a **second, offshore LNG terminal** is planned by the end of the decade. Prime Minister Wong cited the terminal by name this week as the preparation that made the difference.
**Tags:** Systems; Money
**Connection to today's focus:** the most direct of all — the post explains, with the receipts, why a 9% cut in pipeline exports became a <2% event.
**Images: thin–workable.** The terminal itself is photographed mainly in SLNG/corporate and press imagery (not free); Commons offers general LNG-carrier and terminal photography from elsewhere plus present-day Jurong Island aerials. The post must lean on graphics: a self-made chart of the pipeline-vs-LNG import split (2000–2025) and of gas's share of generation, plus an OSM map of the Jurong Island site. Feasible, but this is the most graphics-dependent of the top four. **Key facts to check:** the year-by-year import split (EMA statistics); the second terminal's current status and form (FSRU vs onshore); Singapore GasCo's founding (2025) and mandate. **Sources:** SLNG, "Prime Minister Lee Hsien Loong Officially Opens The Singapore LNG Terminal" — https://www.slng.com.sg/prime-minister-lee-hsien-loong-officially-opens-singapore-lng-terminal ; SLNG, "Catalysing the LNG Ecosystem and the Energy Transition" — https://www.slng.com.sg/stories/catalysing-lng-ecosystem-and-energy-transition ; Rigzone (terminal facts; second-terminal agreements) — https://www.rigzone.com/news/singapore_starts_building_new_loading_facility_for_trucked_lng-28-aug-2025-181615-article/ ; Bloomberg via BusinessMirror (EMA figures, 25 Oct 2026) — https://businessmirror.com.ph/2026/10/25/indonesia-begins-trimming-pipeline-gas-exports-to-singapore/
**Coverage flag:** business-press covered; no heritage treatment. It sits at the recent end of the channel's range — pair it with candidate 1 as a two-part "gas supply" mini-series rather than standing alone.

### 5. Kallang Gasworks and the Town Gas Century, 1862–1998
**Story:** Singapore's first piped gas was made from coal at **Kallang Gasworks**, built in **1862** by the **Singapore Gas Company** (formed in London in 1861) beside the Kallang Basin at Kampong Bugis, where coal could arrive by barge. The works ran around the clock, guarded by Gurkhas, and was nicknamed ***huay sia* — "fire city"** — in Hokkien, from the fear it might explode. It began by lighting streets; the municipality took over in **1901**; electricity took the street lighting from 1906 and the **last gas street lamp vanished in 1956**; the works switched from coal to oil gasification in 1958, and gas found its lasting job in cooking and water heating as HDB estates spread. It closed on **23 March 1998**, after 137 years, production moving to Senoko Gasworks — and town gas is still piped to Singapore homes today, now produced from the same natural gas that is in this week's headlines.
**Tags:** Places; Systems
**Connection to today's focus:** "gas" in Singapore homes is a century older than "gas" in its power stations — and the modern town-gas supply is a direct, little-noticed consumer of the imported natural gas now in the news.
**Images: thin.** This is the set's weakest pool: a Commons file search for "Kallang Gasworks" returns essentially one modern sculpture photograph; the familiar historical photographs are NLB holdings credited "All rights reserved" (e.g. the Lee Kip Lin collection) — **NOT usable**. What rescues it partially: pre-1956 NewspaperSG whole pages (the works' first nine decades, including gas-lamp era coverage) count as cleared stills under the channel's rule, and the story leans well on a self-made map of the gasworks/Kampong Bugis site and present-day photos of the (now cleared) site. Treat as a graphics-and-pages-led post; if the channel wants a photo-led one, rank this lower. **Key facts to check:** the "largest gasworks in Southeast Asia" claim (Wikipedia-carried — verify); the four gasholder tanks' dates; the date town-gas production switched to natural gas as feedstock. **Sources:** NLB, "Kallang Gasworks" — https://www.nlb.gov.sg/main/article-detail?cmsuuid=093ecbc7-b83d-4916-9868-9d8465378096 ; NLB, "Singapore Gas Company is established" — https://www.nlb.gov.sg/main/article-detail?cmsuuid=542cf8b2-d3a2-4824-afe5-5498bd053c4b ; NHB Roots, "Kallang Gasworks" (historic site) — https://www.roots.gov.sg/places/places-landing/Places/historic-sites/kallang-gasworks ; Wikipedia (starting point) — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kallang_Gasworks
**Coverage flag:** an NHB historic site with an NLB entry, but no mainstream long-form treatment; the *huay sia* nickname and the 1998 public opening before demolition are the fresh details.

### 6. Jurong Island: Seven Islands Into One
**Story:** Off Jurong lay seven low islands — Ayer Chawan, Ayer Merbau, Merlimau, Pesek, Pesek Kecil, Sakra and Seraya — with Malay fishing villages in stilt houses, until oil companies arrived in the late 1960s (Esso on Ayer Chawan, the Singapore Refining Company on Merlimau, Mobil on Pesek). EDB chairman **Philip Yeo** proposed joining the islands into one great petrochemical island; JTC was appointed agent in **1991**, reclamation began in **1995** (Penta-Ocean Construction, a Japanese firm, was the major contractor, finishing well ahead of schedule), and Prime Minister Goh Chok Tong opened **Jurong Island on 14 October 2000**. From 991 hectares of islets it grew past 3,000 hectares; a **S$7 billion** public investment drew over **S$50 billion** in plant, 100+ companies and some 26–27,000 jobs, making Singapore one of the world's top chemical exporters — and, not incidentally, the land on which the LNG terminal (candidate 4) and much of the gas system stand.
**Tags:** Money; Places; Systems
**Connection to today's focus:** Jurong Island is where the gas lands and is consumed — the petrochemical complex the West Natuna pipeline was built partly to feed.
**Images: workable.** Commons holds ~87 file hits: aerials and locator maps (including a proper locator map), views from Jurong Hill, and company-era shots; the vanished-villages layer has almost no free photography, so the early sections lean on maps — the before/after island outlines are a genuinely strong self-made graphic. **Map/chart load:** excellent (island growth 991→3,000+ ha; investment figures). **Key facts to check:** the resettlement dates for the island villages (1970s–80s sources vary — handle factually and briefly); reclamation completion date (25 Sep 2009 per Wikipedia/JTC); Penta-Ocean's exact role. **Sources:** Wikipedia (starting point) — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jurong_Island_Bus_Terminal (article text on Jurong Island) ; SG101, "Jurong Island" — https://www.sg101.gov.sg/resources/archives/til-jurong-island/ ; SG101, "Jurong Island: The Chemical Island" — https://www.sg101.gov.sg/economy/case-studies/jurong2/
**Coverage flag:** ⚠ the *achievement* is well covered by government and JTC media — the lesser-known layer is the seven islands and their villages before amalgamation (a natural companion to the published Sisters' Islands post and the backlog's Semakau item). Distinct from the published "Japan's quiet hand in building Jurong" post, which concerns the mainland industrial estate, not the island amalgamation.

### 7. Senoko: The Giant on the Northern Shore, 1976
**Story:** Planned from **1971** — in the middle of the oil-shock decade — **Senoko Power Station** was built by PUB in three stages (1976, 1979, 1983) at a total cost of **S$1.2 billion**, its contracts going repeatedly to Japan's **Marubeni Corporation**; by 1983 its 1,610 MW supplied **60% of Singapore's electricity**, and its 182-metre chimney remains the tallest structure in the north, visible across the strait from Johor Bahru. Its later life tracks the whole energy story: a **1992 fire** caused a major island outage; that same year it began burning **Malaysian pipeline gas** (candidate 1) and was repowered into Singapore's first combined-cycle plant; a submarine cable links it to Johor's Pasir Gudang station so each side can back the other up; and in **2008** Temasek sold Senoko to a Marubeni-led consortium for **S$3.65 billion** — the builder returning as owner.
**Tags:** Places; Money; Japan angle (Marubeni, twice)
**Connection to today's focus:** Senoko is where the first pipeline gas landed and remains Singapore's largest station — the demand end of the pipes in the news.
**Images: thin–workable.** Commons holds only a handful of files (station views, including CC shots taken from the Johor side); Senoko is post-1956, so *Straits Times* construction coverage does **not** count as imagery. The pool is modern exterior shots + a strong chart story (capacity stages; share of national demand) + OSM map. **Key facts to check:** the 1992 fire's date and outage extent (NewspaperSG reporting is citable as text, not usable as images); the Johor interconnection's terms; official opening (the *Straits Times* of 31 July 1977 reported a formal opening by a visiting royal — verify whose). **Sources:** NLB, "Senoko Power Station" — https://www.nlb.gov.sg/main/article-detail?cmsuuid=a04a0e83-c6c3-4e03-8200-cc9926865969 ; Wikipedia (starting point) — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Senoko_Power_Station
**Coverage flag:** an NLB Infopedia entry exists, but the Marubeni-through-line (builder 1972 → owner 2008) is not the angle any existing treatment leads with.

### 8. 1973: The Oil Shock and the Winter Singapore Turned Down the Lights
**Story:** When the October 1973 war triggered the Arab oil embargo, crude deliveries to Singapore's refineries fell about **12.5%**. A declassified US State Department cable from Singapore (21 December 1973) records the response: the government called on the public to cut electricity and petrol consumption by **12.5%** — electricity being about half of domestic energy use — and within weeks consumption was down 5–10%. The strategic decision underneath was the famous one: Lee Kuan Yew told the oil companies Singapore would **not** block exports of their oil stocks, even though seizing them would have covered two years of local consumption — because dependability was worth more than the oil. The restraint was repaid: by the late 1990s Singapore was the world's **third-largest refining centre**, after Houston and Rotterdam, and third-largest oil trading centre after New York and London.
**Tags:** Systems; Money
**Connection to today's focus:** the template for 2026 — a supply scare met with conservation, contract-keeping and diversification, remembered now mainly by the people who lived through the dimmed lights.
**Images: workable.** 1973 is post-1956, so *Straits Times* pages do **not** count. The pool: public-domain US government material of the era, Commons' generic 1973 oil-crisis imagery (queues abroad — use sparingly and labelled as such), and above all charts: the world oil price line 1970–80 and Singapore's consumption-cut figures from the cable. **Key facts to check:** the cable's figures against NLB/NewspaperSG reporting of the 1973 energy-saving campaign (the campaign's local name and start date); whether any local rationing beyond voluntary restraint was imposed; the "two years of stocks" quotation against Lee's memoirs. **Sources:** US State Department cable 1973SINGAP04904_b (declassified) — https://www.wikileaks.org/plusd/cables/1973SINGAP04904_b.html ; Yao & Shi, "Singapore, the Energy Economy: From the First Refinery to the End of Cheap Oil, 1960–2010" — https://www.researchgate.net/publication/297770397_Singapore_the_Energy_Economy_From_The_First_Refinery_To_The_End_Of_Cheap_Oil_1960-2010
**Coverage flag:** the embargo is textbook history globally; the Singapore-specific campaign and the cable's numbers are not — keep the frame local or it reads as a general explainer.

### 9. First Currents: Trams, Docks and the Mackenzie Road Power Station, 1897–1906
**Story:** Electricity came to Singapore not for homes but for machines. The Singapore Harbour Board was generating power for the port by **1897**; the **Singapore Tramway Company** built supply for its electric trams and street lighting from **1902**; and in **1906** the **Tanjong Pagar Dock Company** opened the **Mackenzie Road Power Station** — arguably Singapore's first true power station — to run its docks and workshops, selling surplus current into the town. Electric light followed the tram lines: districts like Tanglin were wired because the trams already were. A prequel post that sets up candidate 3, and a natural follow-up to the blog's published Tanjong Pagar Dock Company post, told from the power side rather than the harbour side.
**Tags:** Systems; Places
**Connection to today's focus:** the origin point of the arc — every later chapter (coal station, oil station, gas network) is an answer to the demand these first dynamos created.
**Images: workable †.** Entirely pre-1956, so NewspaperSG whole pages count; Commons holds period KITLV street and tramway photography of the era (the blog already uses KITLV tram-era material), though no verified photograph of the Mackenzie Road station itself was found — if none surfaces at drafting, the station's absence becomes part of the story and maps carry it. **Key facts to check:** the competing "firsts" (SHB 1897 vs tramway 1902 vs Mackenzie Road 1906) against NLB's electricity-history material; when domestic supply began in earnest; the Mackenzie Road station's fate. **Sources:** Docomomo Singapore, "Industrial Heritage II" — https://www.docomomo.sg/happenings/industrial-heritage-ii-power-stations-and-jurong-industrial-estate ; St James Power Station history section (SHB 1897 / tramway 1902 dates) — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St_James_Power_Station
**Coverage flag:** none significant; the dock-company post covers the firm, not its power station.

### 10. Solar on the Reservoirs: The Tengeh Floating Solar Farm, 2021
**Story:** The modern coda. On **14 July 2021** Sembcorp and PUB opened the **Sembcorp Tengeh Floating Solar Farm**: **122,000 panels** across **45 hectares** of Tengeh Reservoir — one of the world's largest inland floating solar systems at **60 MWp**, enough for ~16,000 four-room HDB flats, built to power Singapore's five water treatment plants and make its waterworks fully green-powered. In a country with no land to spare, the reservoirs became power stations — the one fuel Singapore imports not at all.
**Tags:** Systems; Places
**Connection to today's focus:** the forward-looking end of the diversification story the news has revived (solar is the largest home-grown source in EMA's plans, alongside the second LNG terminal and regional power imports).
**Images: thin.** A Commons search for the farm returns **no files**; the aerial photography in circulation is Sembcorp/PUB press material (not free). A post would need present-day reservoir photography from public vantage points, charts of solar deployment growth, and maps — or wait for the user's own photographs on a future Singapore trip. Ranked last for this reason, and flagged as a deliberate exception to the channel's history-first pattern — best used as the closing episode of an energy mini-series rather than a standalone. **Key facts to check:** current national solar capacity and the 2030 target (raised to at least 3 GWp per recent EMA reporting — verify); the further reservoir farms (Pandan, Lower Seletar) status. **Sources:** Sembcorp/PUB joint press release (14 Jul 2021) — https://cms.infrastructureasia.org/assets/a820bb96-03e8-4207-a392-6d80cc65e8a2.pdf ; Sembcorp — https://www.sembcorp.com/sg/news-and-insights/insights-and-stories/singapore/one-of-the-worlds-largest-inland-floating-solar-farms/
**Coverage flag:** ⚠ heavily covered by mainstream media at opening — included only as a series coda.
