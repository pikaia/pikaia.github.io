## History

- **Bank of America and the founding of Singapore's Asian Dollar Market** — BoA was one of the founding banks of Singapore's offshore US-dollar market (established 1968), at one point running many branches across the island; a genuinely obscure piece of how Singapore became a financial centre, buried under the more famous story of its port and manufacturing economy. **Personal connection:** Chris worked as an FX trader at BoA in Singapore; an older colleague once showed him an old photo (judging by the colleague's long hair, likely 1960s/70s) of a godown BoA had managed — possibly on the Singapore River itself, shown to Chris to illustrate how far back the bank's Singapore roots went. *(Money angle — strong fit; person-focused via Chris's own banking career.)* *(Images: thin — no Commons hits for "Asian Dollar Market Singapore"; would lean on present-day CBD/Raffles Place photos and likely a self-made chart of the market's growth.)*
- **Marina Barrage and the end of Singapore's water-dependency anxiety** — completed in 2008, the barrage dammed the mouth of the Kallang and Singapore Rivers to create Marina Reservoir, the largest and 15th reservoir on the island, folding decades of anxiety over imported Malaysian water (the 1961/1962 Water Agreements, expiring 2011/2061) into a landmark most visitors know only as a rooftop picnic lawn. *(Money/policy angle — water security ties to national self-sufficiency; needs the standard govt-mention accuracy/tone pass since it touches the Malaysia water agreements.)* *(Images: strong — the Barrage and its rooftop lawn are among the most CC-photographed spots in Singapore; older reservoirs and water-agreement-era photos exist too.)*
- **The Fullerton Building: the post office with a lighthouse on the roof** — the 1928 General Post Office on the waterfront also housed the Chamber of Commerce, the Marine Department, the exchange and the Singapore Club, and carried a rooftop beacon — the Fullerton Light — that guided ships into the harbour until 1958. Named after the first Governor, cleared of its tenants in 1996, and now a luxury hotel that most guests don't know was the GPO. *(Money/architecture angle; hidden-landmark.)* *(Images: strong — the Fullerton is one of the most CC-photographed buildings in Singapore, and 1930s GPO / waterfront photos are public domain.)*
- **The bumboats and the river that was scrubbed clean** — for over a century twakow lighters ferried cargo between ships in the roads and the godowns lining the Singapore River, and the river itself was an open drain of boat traffic, upstream pig and duck farms, and hawker waste; a ten-year government clean-up from 1977 moved the lighters out to Pasir Panjang, relocated the trades, and turned the banks into today's bar-and-restaurant stretch. A vanished-livelihood story wrapped around a dateable state campaign (worth a Straits Times archive check). *(Money/entrepôt angle.)* *(Images: strong — historical bumboat and godown photos are public domain; the river and quays today are among the most CC-photographed spots in Singapore.)*
- **The Cathay Building, Singapore's first skyscraper** — the 1939 Art Deco tower on Mount Sophia was the tallest building in South-East Asia when it opened, with a cinema, a hotel, flats and a broadcasting station; the Japanese used it as a propaganda HQ and information ministry during the Occupation; the facade is conserved today over a modern mall. *(Money/architecture angle; hidden-landmark.)* *(Images: strong — heavily photographed historical and present-day, on Commons.)*
- **The Botanic Gardens and the rubber that remade Malaya** — Henry "Mad" Ridley, the Gardens' director from 1888, spent two decades pushing para rubber on sceptical planters and worked out the tapping method still used today; the Gardens' seedlings seeded a plantation economy that dwarfed everything before it. A vanished-context story that pairs with the pineapple and gutta-percha ideas. *(Money angle.)* *(Images: strong — the Gardens are a UNESCO site, hugely photographed CC; Ridley portraits and historical Gardens photos are public domain.)*
- **Cold Storage and the frozen-meat trade** — founded 1903 to ship chilled Australian meat and butter to Singapore's European community, the Singapore Cold Storage Company built the island's first refrigerated warehouse on the wharves and grew into the supermarket chain; a money/food-retail story about how refrigeration changed what Singapore ate, a natural companion to the Whampoa-ice idea. *(Money angle.)* *(Images: workable — the old Cold Storage depot and early branches were photographed; present-day stores are heavily CC-photographed; some period advertising is public domain.)*
- **Opium farming funded colonial Singapore** — opium was legal and taxed for decades; opium-farm revenue was one of the largest sources of government income before WWII. A bigger, more systemic story than the usual founding-of-Singapore fare. *(Money angle.)* *(Images: workable — colonial opium-den and shop photos from c.1900 are public domain; the revenue story itself would carry a self-made chart.)*
- **The 1915 Singapore Mutiny** — a WWI-era revolt by sepoys of a Muslim Indian regiment, fuelled partly by rumours that the Kaiser had converted to Islam; put down with help from Russian, French and Japanese troops, with 47 executed by firing squad. Barely discussed compared to WWII history. *(Images: workable — at least one Commons photo; 5th Light Infantry regiment photos and execution-site imagery are public domain by age.)*
- **The gutta-percha boom that stripped Malaya's forests** — before rubber, Singapore was the world's entrepôt for gutta-percha, a latex tapped from Malayan trees that insulated the first submarine telegraph cables (the Victorian internet); the trade nearly wiped the trees out within decades, then collapsed. A vanished-commodity fortune in the vein of the pineapple kings. *(Money angle.)* *(Images: workable — Palaquium gutta botanical plates and Victorian submarine-cable imagery are public domain; no single obvious hero, would lean on a map/chart.)*

## People

- **William Farquhar, the co-founder written out of the story** — Singapore's first Resident did much of the actual early nation-building, but was overshadowed and later pushed out by Raffles, who gets sole founding-father credit today. *(Images: strong — portraits are public domain; the William Farquhar Collection of natural history drawings is partly on Commons; early Singapore maps and town plans are public domain.)*
- **Whampoa: the merchant who brought Singapore its first ice** — Hoo Ah Kay ("Whampoa"), a Cantonese provisioner who became the only Asian on the colonial Legislative Council, imported Singapore's first ice (shipped as blocks from a lake near Boston), built an ice house, and hosted governors at his famous Bendemeer garden. Street names (Whampoa, Bendemeer) nobody connects to a person. *(Money angle; person-focused.)* *(Images: workable–strong — a 19th-century portrait of Whampoa is public domain; Bendemeer House and Legislative Council imagery exist.)*
- **Song Ong Siang, the man who wrote Singapore's Chinese history and became its first Chinese knight** — a third-generation Peranakan, Singapore's first Chinese Queen's Scholar (1888) and first Asian lawyer in the colony, co-founder of the Straits Chinese British Association and (with Lim Boon Keng) the Straits Chinese Magazine; his 600-page "One Hundred Years' History of the Chinese in Singapore" (1923) is still the primary source this blog itself has cited for other posts (the pineapple kings, Lim Peng Siang). Knighted in 1936, the first Chinese in Malaya to be — the chronicler of Singapore's history is himself largely unknown today. *(Person-focused; meta hook about this blog's own sourcing.)* *(Images: strong — multiple public-domain portraits of Song and his wife, plus his book's own cover, are already on Commons.)*
- **Lim Peng Siang, the forgotten "greatest magnate"** — founder of the Ho Hong empire spanning shipping, banking, cement and rice, once ranked among Singapore's biggest tycoons in the 1930s, now almost entirely absent from popular memory. *(Money angle.)* *(Images: workable — a portrait is likely in Song Ong Siang's public-domain "One Hundred Years' History of the Chinese in Singapore" (1923), the same source used for the pineapple post; Ho Hong buildings may survive.)*
- **David Marshall, the reluctant first Chief Minister** — a Sephardic Jewish criminal lawyer who became Singapore's first Chief Minister in 1955, then resigned within a year on a point of principle over self-government talks, later serving as a globe-trotting ambassador. *(Images: workable — 1950s political photos exist, though mid-century Singapore press images are often still under SPH copyright; some official/public-domain material circulates.)*
- **The Chetti Melaka (Peranakan Indians)** — a centuries-old community blending Chinese, Malay and South Indian heritage into its own Creole language and dress, now down to only a few hundred officially recorded members. A genuinely obscure counterpart to the much more famous Chinese Peranakan story. *(Images: workable — present-day dress, temples and festivals are CC-photographed; historical imagery is thinner.)*
- **P. Govindasamy Pillai, from cloth peddler to retail pioneer** — a South Indian immigrant who built the PGP department store chain and became a major community philanthropist, overshadowed today by better-remembered Chinese business dynasties. *(Money angle, weaker.)* *(Images: thin — obscure; a portrait may exist but not on Commons, and PGP store photos are scarce.)*
- **P. S. Hunter, the health officer whose one report built Tiong Bahru** — a municipal health officer whose study of Chinatown's overcrowding and disease (sometime before 1925) recommended clearing it for "a well-planned suburb nearby," a recommendation that led directly to the Singapore Improvement Trust's founding in 1927 and its Tiong Bahru estate — the model Singapore's entire public-housing system, including HDB, was built on. **Genuinely obscure**: a search beyond the one BiblioAsia article that names him (used as a source for this blog's own Tiong Bahru/SIT post) turns up no full name, no dates, no other career details, and no portrait. The angle may need to be less "his life story" and more "the report that quietly set 20th-century Singapore's housing trajectory," unless NLB/NewspaperSG archival digging turns up more about the man himself when this post's turn comes. *(Person-focused; ties to the already-published SIT/Tiong Bahru post.)* *(Images: thin — no portrait located; would lean on Tiong Bahru's own imagery and perhaps a period municipal-report document.)*
- **Ya'acob Mohamed, the forgotten pioneer** — a first-generation PAP leader whose career spanned the formative years of Singapore and Malaysia's political journeys, now far less remembered than his contemporaries; the Malay-medium schools he helped establish have since closed. *(Images: thin — obscure; likely no Commons portrait.)*

## Japan–Singapore

- **"Banana money": the wartime currency that inflated to nothing** — the Japanese military administration issued unbacked occupation dollars (nicknamed "banana money" for the banana-tree note), printed at will; by 1945 runaway inflation had made a suitcase of it worthless, wiping out ordinary savings overnight and leaving the notes as a stock relic of the Occupation. A money + Japan story with a dateable currency collapse. *(Japan + money angle — both perform well.)* *(Images: strong — occupation notes are extensively scanned and public domain on Commons.)*
- **Tani Yutaka, the "Tiger of Malaya"** — a Japanese Cemetery Park burial, a bandit-turned-spy who guided Japanese troops through Malaya's jungles before the 1942 invasion, mythologised in Japanese films as a folk hero and reviled locally as a traitor-collaborator. *(Japan angle.)* *(Images: workable — the Japanese Cemetery Park grave is CC-photographed; period photos may exist via Japanese sources, though film imagery is likely under copyright.)*
- **"Little Japan" on Middle Road** — from the 1900s to 1941 the Middle Road area was the centre of a sizeable Japanese civilian community: provision shops, photographers, dentists, a Japanese school and consulate, and (earlier) the karayuki-san brothels, all erased by the war and post-war repatriation. *(Japan angle.)* *(Images: workable — pre-war Japanese community photos exist in archives and some are public domain; Middle Road today is photographable.)*
- **Bahau: the "Catholic colony" Singaporeans were sent to** — in 1943 the Japanese administration and the Catholic Church (Bishop Adrien Devals) resettled several thousand Singaporeans, mostly Catholic Chinese and Eurasians, to farmland at Bahau in Negeri Sembilan to ease the wartime food shortage; the soil was poor, malaria rife, and many died, Bishop Devals among them. **Has a family thread:** Chris's wife's late mother was one of those relocated (see the Aukang / Holy Innocents' Lane connection). *(Japan / Occupation angle.)* *(Images: workable–thin — Catholic archives (history.catholic.sg) hold Occupation-era material; a Devals portrait may exist; Commons itself is thin.)*
- **The pre-war Japanese fishing fleet** — by the 1930s Japanese trawler crews supplied much of Singapore's fresh fish and were clustered around Kampong Wak Hassan on the northern coast; colonial authorities suspected the boats doubled as coastal reconnaissance, and the whole fleet vanished in 1941–42. A pre-Occupation economic story with an espionage undertone. *(Japan angle.)* *(Images: thin — very obscure; may yield a trawler photo or two, otherwise a map.)*

## Food & culture

- **Change Alley, the money-changers' bazaar that vanished** — a narrow lane between Raffles Place and Collyer Quay, packed for decades with money-changers, touts and stalls, a fixed stop on every 1960s–80s tourist itinerary, cleared in 1989 for the Hitachi Tower / Caltex House redevelopment and reduced to an air-conditioned overhead link bridge that kept the name. A demolished-institution story in the vein of Bugis Street, money angle built in. *(Images: workable–strong — Change Alley was a heavily photographed tourist draw; 1960s–70s photos circulate on Flickr Commons, and the modern bridge is photographable.)*
- **Robinsons, the 162-year department store, and the 1972 fire** — founded 1858, Singapore's oldest department store and a fixture of Raffles Place, gutted by a catastrophic fire in November 1972 that killed nine and destroyed the flagship building, rebuilt and carried on, then quietly shut every Singapore store in 2020–21. A money/retail story with a dateable disaster at its centre (worth a Straits Times archive check). *(Money angle.)* *(Images: workable — old Robinsons and Raffles Place photos exist; the fire made front pages.)*
- **The three "Worlds": Singapore's vanished amusement parks** — New World, Great World and Happy World (later Gay World) were the great night-time pleasure grounds from the 1920s to the 1980s — cabaret, boxing, ronggeng, wayang, cinemas, food — where a whole generation spent its evenings, all gone now except a mall that kept the "Great World" name. *(Images: workable — entrance and interior photos of Great World and New World circulate; Great World City today is photographable.)*
- **The Van Kleef Aquarium** — Singapore's first public aquarium opened at River Valley in 1955, funded by a Dutch businessman's bequest, a fixture of school excursions for four decades before it closed in 1996 and was demolished in 1998, its fish moved to Sentosa. A small, fondly-remembered lost institution. *(Images: workable–thin — the building was photographed and a few images exist; demolished, so no present-day.)*

## Present-day / policy

*(Image availability is less of a gate here — these lean on self-made charts, present-day CC photos and maps rather than historical photography.)*

- **Follow-up to the Benjamin Sheares post: has fertility policy actually changed anything?** — the Sheares post's Short is performing well (1,134 views vs. the full video's 161, per YouTube Studio, 2026-09-04); a genuine follow-up would take the government's full policy U-turn since his "Stop at Two" era — the 1980s pivot to "Have Three or More," the Baby Bonus, today's pronatalist cash incentives and Baby Bonus top-ups — and ask directly whether any of it moved the total fertility rate, which has kept sliding regardless of which direction the policy pointed. *(Capitalises on a topic already shown to perform; money/policy angle.)*
- **The Platform Workers Act's slow-motion phase-in** — CPF contributions for delivery riders and ride-hail drivers only became mandatory in 2025, with transition support covering 100% of the worker's added cost in 2025 but sliding to 75% (2026), 50% (2027) and 25% (2028) — a safety net for gig workers that's already scheduled to quietly thin out just as it's introduced. *(Money angle.)*
- **The COE: paying for permission to own a car, then paying again to drive it** — the Vehicle Quota System's Certificate of Entitlement began on 1 May 1990 (first bidding 2 April), introduced after 1975–90 saw car ownership growing ~12% a year with no room left to build more roads; a COE now often costs more than the car itself, making Singapore's cost of car ownership among the highest in the world. Pairs naturally with Electronic Road Pricing, the *other* half of the cost of driving here — introduced 1998 as the world's first road congestion-pricing system, and now itself being replaced: gantry-based ERP is being phased out for ERP 2.0, satellite/GNSS-based on-board units that track a vehicle by location rather than a fixed toll point, mandatory in every Singapore-registered vehicle by full rollout in 2027. Own the car, then pay per road; both levers exist because of the same original land constraint, and the two are always discussed as a pair (Chris's framing). ERP rates have climbed enough that avoiding certain gantries/routes is now a real factor in where people choose to live, not just how they commute — a hidden-effect angle worth leading with. *(Money angle — strong fit.)* *(Images: workable — ERP gantries (old and new) are heavily CC-photographed; COE itself has little to photograph directly, so the ownership-cost side would lean on a self-made chart of COE prices over time.)*
- **"Quiet cracking" at work** — roughly 30% of Singapore workers report this quieter, harder-to-spot form of disengagement/burnout in 2026 surveys; unlike "quiet quitting," employees keep performing while struggling, so managers often miss it entirely.
