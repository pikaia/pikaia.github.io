## History

- **The exchange banks of Raffles Place: Chartered, HSBC and Mercantile** — before local banks arrived, Raffles Place was the preserve of the British exchange banks that financed the colony's trade, led by the Chartered Bank (in Singapore from 1859) and the Hongkong and Shanghai Bank (from 1877), with the Mercantile Bank alongside. They set exchange rates, issued notes and handled the rubber and tin money, and their domed and columned buildings defined the square until the towers replaced them. A money-angle companion to the UOB and OCBC posts. *(Money; places.)* *(Images: strong — KITLV has several CC BY 4.0 photos of the Chartered Bank and Raffles Place, plus public-domain postcards; pre-1956 Straits Times pages for openings and new buildings. Opening dates and the note-issuing history need checking against NLB Infopedia and the banks' own histories.)*
- **The Tanjong Pagar Dock Company and the government buyout of 1905** — founded in 1864, the private dock company grew with the steamship trade after the Suez Canal opened into the owner of most of Singapore's wharves, docks and coal stores, and in 1905 the colonial government took it over compulsorily, with the price settled by arbitration; the new Tanjong Pagar Dock Board was the forerunner of the Singapore Harbour Board and today's port authority. A money story about how a private company built the port and how the state ended up owning it. Found while researching the forts post (the company traded the top of Mount Palmer for a road to its docks). *(Money angle; systems.)* *(Images: strong — the Rijksmuseum's CC0 photo album of the Tanjong Pagar Dock Co. (RP-F-F01140) has dozens of 1890s-1900s views of the docks, workshops and staff, and the buyout and arbitration are dateable events for the Straits Times archive check. The founding date, the arbitration award and the Dock Board's dates need checking against NLB Infopedia before drafting.)*
- **Bank of America and the founding of Singapore's Asian Dollar Market** — BoA was one of the founding banks of Singapore's offshore US-dollar market (established 1968), at one point running many branches across the island; a genuinely obscure piece of how Singapore became a financial centre, buried under the more famous story of its port and manufacturing economy. **Personal connection:** Chris worked at Security Pacific National Bank in Singapore (Jul 1987 to Nov 1997; later part of Bank of America), on the FX and later the short-term interest rate trading desks; an older colleague once showed him an old photo (judging by the colleague's long hair, likely 1960s/70s) of a godown BoA had managed — possibly on the Singapore River itself, shown to Chris to illustrate how far back the bank's Singapore roots went. *(Money angle — strong fit; person-focused via Chris's own banking career.)* *(Images: thin — no Commons hits for "Asian Dollar Market Singapore"; would lean on present-day CBD/Raffles Place photos and likely a self-made chart of the market's growth.)*
- **The gutta-percha boom that stripped Malaya's forests** — before rubber, Singapore was the world's entrepôt for gutta-percha, a latex tapped from Malayan trees that insulated the first submarine telegraph cables (the Victorian internet); the trade nearly wiped the trees out within decades, then collapsed. A vanished-commodity fortune in the vein of the pineapple kings. *(Money angle.)* *(Images: workable — Palaquium gutta botanical plates and Victorian submarine-cable imagery are public domain; no single obvious hero, would lean on a map/chart.)*
- **Rubber by coupon: the 1934 restriction scheme and the trade in paper rubber** — to end the Depression's price collapse, the producing countries signed the International Rubber Regulation Agreement in 1934, and Malaya rationed exports with quota coupons issued to each estate and smallholding. The coupons became a currency of their own: by 1936 the Straits Times was reporting that thousands of acres had gone out of tapping because owners found it more profitable to sell their coupons than their rubber. A money story about how the slump's cure created a market in permission to sell, and who gained from it (estates over smallholders). Found while researching the Great Depression post. *(Money angle; systems.)* *(Images: workable — 1934-36 Straits Times pages on the scheme and the coupon trade are whole-page candidates, and the rubber-estate and Pulau Brani photos used in the Depression post can be reused sparingly; check the agreement's dates, quotas and the coupon prices against NLB Infopedia and the newspapers before drafting.)*
- **Why Singapore's merchants asked to leave British India: the 1867 transfer to Crown rule** — until 1 April 1867 the Straits Settlements were governed from Calcutta. Singapore's merchants petitioned London for years over Calcutta's rule: its attempt to make the Indian rupee the local currency, plans to tax the free port, and the convicts it kept sending. The campaign, led by men such as W. H. Read, ended with the Straits Settlements becoming a Crown colony, Singapore's first real break from India. *(Money angle; systems.)* *(Images: workable — Commons has Government House and 1860s Singapore views, and the transfer is a dateable event, so the Straits Times of April 1867 is a likely whole-page scan. Check the currency and tax grievances against NLB Infopedia before drafting, and keep the tone on the colonial-era politics balanced.)*

## People

- **The Chetti Melaka (Peranakan Indians)** — a centuries-old community blending Chinese, Malay and South Indian heritage into its own Creole language and dress, now down to only a few hundred officially recorded members. A genuinely obscure counterpart to the much more famous Chinese Peranakan story. *(Images: workable — present-day dress, temples and festivals are CC-photographed; historical imagery is thinner.)*
- **Wee Bin, the shipping magnate whose firm outlived its own name** — founded Wee Bin & Co. in the mid-nineteenth century, one of the major Straits Chinese shipping firms of the era; run after him by his son-in-law Lim Ho Puah, then liquidated in 1911, when his own grandson Lim Peng Siang bought its steamships at auction and folded them into the Ho Hong group. A three-generation business story discovered while researching the Lim Peng Siang post. *(Money angle.)* *(Images: workable — a strong period portrait (Song Ong Siang's 1923 book, via Commons) is already in hand, already used once in the Lim Peng Siang post; a Wee Bin post would need a second image pool beyond it, plus period Singapore River/harbour photos for context.)*
- **P. Govindasamy Pillai, from cloth peddler to retail pioneer** — a South Indian immigrant who built the PGP department store chain and became a major community philanthropist, overshadowed today by better-remembered Chinese business dynasties. *(Money angle, weaker.)* *(Images: thin — obscure; a portrait may exist but not on Commons, and PGP store photos are scarce.)*
- **Ya'acob Mohamed, the forgotten pioneer** — a first-generation PAP leader whose career spanned the formative years of Singapore and Malaysia's political journeys, now far less remembered than his contemporaries; the Malay-medium schools he helped establish have since closed. *(Images: thin — obscure; likely no Commons portrait.)*
- **Munshi Abdullah, the scribe who wrote Singapore's first eyewitness account** — Abdullah bin Abdul Kadir (1796–1854) was Raffles's Malay teacher and scribe, and his *Hikayat Abdullah* remains one of the main first-hand Malay accounts of Singapore's founding years, including Farquhar, Raffles and Crawfurd as he saw them — a native-language counterpoint to the British-written record the Farquhar and Crawfurd posts lean on. *(Person-focused.)* *(Images: thin — no known portrait on Commons; manuscript pages of the Hikayat, his signature and the BnF digitisation are available.)*
- **Duxton Plain: the police quarters built for the Gurkhas in 1949** — the Straits Times of 16 February 1949 reported that the first Gurkha Contingent recruits and their families would live in 12 blocks of new police quarters on Duxton Plain, off Neil Road, built for $490,000 on five and a half acres; the contingent later moved to its own cantonment at Mount Vernon. A place-focused follow-up to the Gurkha post, tracing what Duxton Plain was before and after (the area is now a park beside the Pinnacle@Duxton). *(Places; systems.)* *(Images: thin — the 1949 Straits Times page is already in hand; Commons has modern Duxton Plain Park and Pinnacle@Duxton photos. When the quarters were built and demolished, and when the contingent moved to Mount Vernon, need sourcing from NLB Infopedia and the Straits Times before drafting.)*

## Japan–Singapore

- **The $50 million "gift": what the Occupation demanded of Malaya's Chinese, 1942** — soon after the fall of Singapore the Japanese military ordered the Chinese of Malaya to raise $50 million as a "gift" in atonement for their support of the China Relief Fund; the newly formed Overseas Chinese Association, headed by the elderly Lim Boon Keng, struggled to collect it and had to borrow the balance from the Yokohama Specie Bank. A money story at the centre of the Occupation, following on from the Nanyang volunteers post. *(Money; Japan angle.)* *(Images: workable — the Syonan Shimbun's 1942 pages on the collection and its handover are whole-page candidates, as used in the Yokohama Specie Bank post, and Commons has public-domain Lim Boon Keng portraits. The amount raised, the loan and the handover date need checking against NLB Infopedia's Overseas Chinese Association article before drafting.)*
- **Tani Yutaka, the "Tiger of Malaya"** — a Japanese Cemetery Park burial, a bandit-turned-spy who guided Japanese troops through Malaya's jungles before the 1942 invasion, mythologised in Japanese films as a folk hero and reviled locally as a traitor-collaborator. *(Japan angle.)* *(Images: workable — the Japanese Cemetery Park grave is CC-photographed; period photos may exist via Japanese sources, though film imagery is likely under copyright.)*
- **Interned in India: Singapore's Japanese civilians, 1941–46** — on 8 December 1941 the colonial authorities arrested Singapore's Japanese residents, merchants, bank staff, fishermen, shopkeepers and their families, and shipped most of them to internment camps in India, first the Purana Qila in New Delhi and then Deoli in Rajasthan; some were exchanged and sent home in 1942, the rest stayed until 1946, and few returned to Singapore. The other side of the Occupation story, found while researching the Yokohama Specie Bank post. *(Japan angle.)* *(Images: thin — little on Commons for the camps; the December 1941 Straits Times and Malaya Tribune are whole-page candidates, and the Japanese Cemetery Park and prewar Middle Road photos could fill in. The de Matos and Ward study in the Journal of Contemporary History (2021) is the main secondary source; check numbers and dates against it before drafting.)*
- **"Little Japan" on Middle Road** — from the 1900s to 1941 the Middle Road area was the centre of a sizeable Japanese civilian community: provision shops, photographers, dentists, a Japanese school and consulate, and (earlier) the karayuki-san brothels, all erased by the war and post-war repatriation. *(Japan angle.)* *(Images: workable — pre-war Japanese community photos exist in archives and some are public domain; Middle Road today is photographable.)*
- **Bahau: the "Catholic colony" Singaporeans were sent to** — in 1943 the Japanese administration and the Catholic Church (Bishop Adrien Devals) resettled several thousand Singaporeans, mostly Catholic Chinese and Eurasians, to farmland at Bahau in Negeri Sembilan to ease the wartime food shortage; the soil was poor, malaria rife, and many died, Bishop Devals among them. **Has a family thread:** Chris's wife's late mother was one of those relocated (see the Aukang / Holy Innocents' Lane connection). *(Japan / Occupation angle.)* *(Images: workable–thin — Catholic archives (history.catholic.sg) hold Occupation-era material; a Devals portrait may exist; Commons itself is thin.)*

## Food & culture

- **Change Alley, the money-changers' bazaar that vanished** — a narrow lane between Raffles Place and Collyer Quay, packed for decades with money-changers, touts and stalls, a fixed stop on every 1960s–80s tourist itinerary, cleared in 1989 for the Hitachi Tower / Caltex House redevelopment and reduced to an air-conditioned overhead link bridge that kept the name. A demolished-institution story in the vein of Bugis Street, money angle built in. *(Images: workable–strong — Change Alley was a heavily photographed tourist draw; 1960s–70s photos circulate on Flickr Commons, and the modern bridge is photographable.)*
- **Robinsons, the 162-year department store, and the 1972 fire** — founded 1858, Singapore's oldest department store and a fixture of Raffles Place, gutted by a catastrophic fire in November 1972 that killed nine and destroyed the flagship building, rebuilt and carried on, then quietly shut every Singapore store in 2020–21. A money/retail story with a dateable disaster at its centre (worth a Straits Times archive check). *(Money angle.)* *(Images: workable — old Robinsons and Raffles Place photos exist; the fire made front pages.)*
- **The three "Worlds": Singapore's vanished amusement parks** — New World, Great World and Happy World (later Gay World) were the great night-time pleasure grounds from the 1920s to the 1980s — cabaret, boxing, ronggeng, wayang, cinemas, food — where a whole generation spent its evenings, all gone now except a mall that kept the "Great World" name. *(Images: workable — entrance and interior photos of Great World and New World circulate; Great World City today is photographable.)*
- **The Van Kleef Aquarium** — Singapore's first public aquarium opened at River Valley in 1955, funded by a Dutch businessman's bequest, a fixture of school excursions for four decades before it closed in 1996 and was demolished in 1998, its fish moved to Sentosa. A small, fondly-remembered lost institution. *(Images: workable–thin — the building was photographed and a few images exist; demolished, so no present-day.)*
- **Sungei Road's Thieves' Market, Singapore's last flea market** — an unlicensed street market on Sungei Road that traded in secondhand goods (some, per its nickname, of dubious origin) for close to a century, tolerated by authorities for decades before finally being cleared in 2017 to make way for a housing/MRT redevelopment; long-time hawkers scattered, some to a smaller relocated pitch that itself didn't last. A vanished-institution story about the informal economy the city usually writes out of its own narrative. *(Money/informal-economy angle.)* *(Images: workable — Commons has a February 1965 patent-medicine-stall photo plus multiple 2000s-era street-market shots from just before closure.)*

## Present-day / policy

*(Image availability is less of a gate here — these lean on self-made charts, present-day CC photos and maps rather than historical photography.)*

- **Follow-up to the Benjamin Sheares post: has fertility policy actually changed anything?** — the Sheares post's Short is performing well (1,134 views vs. the full video's 161, per YouTube Studio, 2026-09-04); a genuine follow-up would take the government's full policy U-turn since his "Stop at Two" era — the 1980s pivot to "Have Three or More," the Baby Bonus, today's pronatalist cash incentives and Baby Bonus top-ups — and ask directly whether any of it moved the total fertility rate, which has kept sliding regardless of which direction the policy pointed. *(Capitalises on a topic already shown to perform; money/policy angle.)*
- **The Platform Workers Act's slow-motion phase-in** — CPF contributions for delivery riders and ride-hail drivers only became mandatory in 2025, with transition support covering 100% of the worker's added cost in 2025 but sliding to 75% (2026), 50% (2027) and 25% (2028) — a safety net for gig workers that's already scheduled to quietly thin out just as it's introduced. *(Money angle.)*
- **The COE: paying for permission to own a car, then paying again to drive it** — the Vehicle Quota System's Certificate of Entitlement began on 1 May 1990 (first bidding 2 April), introduced after 1975–90 saw car ownership growing ~12% a year with no room left to build more roads; a COE now often costs more than the car itself, making Singapore's cost of car ownership among the highest in the world. Pairs naturally with Electronic Road Pricing, the *other* half of the cost of driving here — introduced 1998 as the world's first road congestion-pricing system, and now itself being replaced: gantry-based ERP is being phased out for ERP 2.0, satellite/GNSS-based on-board units that track a vehicle by location rather than a fixed toll point, mandatory in every Singapore-registered vehicle by full rollout in 2027. Own the car, then pay per road; both levers exist because of the same original land constraint, and the two are always discussed as a pair (Chris's framing). ERP rates have climbed enough that avoiding certain gantries/routes is now a real factor in where people choose to live, not just how they commute — a hidden-effect angle worth leading with. *(Money angle — strong fit.)* *(Images: workable — ERP gantries (old and new) are heavily CC-photographed; COE itself has little to photograph directly, so the ownership-cost side would lean on a self-made chart of COE prices over time.)*
- **"Quiet cracking" at work** — roughly 30% of Singapore workers report this quieter, harder-to-spot form of disengagement/burnout in 2026 surveys; unlike "quiet quitting," employees keep performing while struggling, so managers often miss it entirely.
