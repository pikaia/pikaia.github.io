## History

- **The Bank of China Building on Battery Road** — the Chartered Bank's old palazzo at Battery Road and Flint Street became the Bank of China's Singapore office in 1937, and after the war the bank pulled it down and put up a tall modern building on the site, one of the first towers on the waterfront. Its story runs through the war, the 1949 revolution in China and Singapore's own independence. A follow-up to the exchange banks post. *(Money; places.)* *(Images: unknown — check Commons for the building, old and new; the construction and opening dates, its height and its claim to be one of Singapore's first skyscrapers need checking against NewspaperSG and Roots.gov.sg before drafting.)*
- **The Chinese Protectorate, 1877 to the Ministry of Labour** — set up in 1877 after the riots of the 1870s, with William Pickering as the first Protector of Chinese in a shophouse on North Canal Road, the Protectorate registered the secret societies, supervised the arrival of labourers from China and ran a home for women rescued from forced prostitution; it moved to Havelock Road in the 1880s, and its later building there became the Ministry of Labour's, now conserved. A follow-up to the Post Office riot and Ghee Hin posts, told from the office's side. Found while researching the Post Office riot post. *(History; systems.)* *(Images: workable — Pickering's 1898 portrait (British Library, public domain) and present-day photos of the Old Ministry of Labour Building are on Commons; the 1877 founding date, the moves, the 1887 attack on Pickering and the building's dates need checking against NLB Infopedia, Roots.gov.sg and the Straits Times (pre-1956 whole pages usable) before drafting. The post would name government bodies, so give those passages extra scrutiny.)*
- **Pulau Semakau: from fishing village to the island made of rubbish** — a southern island with Malay and Chinese fishing villages until the 1970s, when its people were moved to the mainland; from 1999 it was joined to Pulau Sakeng by a seawall to make Semakau Landfill, Singapore's only landfill, where incineration ash is dumped while mangroves and reefs survive at its edges and a coral nursery was set up in 2007. A companion to the Sisters' Islands post. *(History; places; present-day.)* *(Images: workable — Commons has landfill and shore photos; the village resettlement dates, the landfill's opening, its capacity and the 2026 Southern Islands reclamation proposals need checking, and the government mentions need extra scrutiny.)*
- **Clifford Pier, where Singapore came ashore** — opened in 1933 on Collyer Quay to replace the old Johnston's Pier, Clifford Pier was for decades the landing point for ships' passengers, crews and servicemen coming in from the roads by launch, and the departure point for bumboats to the southern islands; locals called it the "red lantern pier" after the lamp that hung at Johnston's Pier before it. It closed as a working pier in 2006 when the Marina Barrage cut the bay off from the sea, and the building is now part of a hotel. Found while researching the Change Alley post (the pier fed the alley its customers). *(Places; systems.)* *(Images: workable — a 1971 colour view (Wilford Peloquin, CC BY 2.0), a 1930s KITLV harbour view (CC BY 4.0), 2005 Commons photos, and pre-1956 Straits Times pages for the 1933 opening. The opening date, the "red lantern" naming and the 2006 closure need checking against NLB Infopedia before drafting.)*
- **Bank of America and the founding of Singapore's Asian Dollar Market** — BoA was one of the founding banks of Singapore's offshore US-dollar market (established 1968), at one point running many branches across the island; a genuinely obscure piece of how Singapore became a financial centre, buried under the more famous story of its port and manufacturing economy. **Personal connection:** Chris worked at Security Pacific National Bank in Singapore (Jul 1987 to Nov 1997; later part of Bank of America), on the FX and later the short-term interest rate trading desks; an older colleague once showed him an old photo (judging by the colleague's long hair, likely 1960s/70s) of a godown BoA had managed — possibly on the Singapore River itself, shown to Chris to illustrate how far back the bank's Singapore roots went. *(Money angle — strong fit; person-focused via Chris's own banking career.)* *(Images: thin — no Commons hits for "Asian Dollar Market Singapore"; would lean on present-day CBD/Raffles Place photos and likely a self-made chart of the market's growth.)*
- **The gutta-percha boom that stripped Malaya's forests** — before rubber, Singapore was the world's entrepôt for gutta-percha, a latex tapped from Malayan trees that insulated the first submarine telegraph cables (the Victorian internet); the trade nearly wiped the trees out within decades, then collapsed. A vanished-commodity fortune in the vein of the pineapple kings. *(Money angle.)* *(Images: workable — Palaquium gutta botanical plates and Victorian submarine-cable imagery are public domain; no single obvious hero, would lean on a map/chart.)*
- **Rubber by coupon: the 1934 restriction scheme and the trade in paper rubber** — to end the Depression's price collapse, the producing countries signed the International Rubber Regulation Agreement in 1934, and Malaya rationed exports with quota coupons issued to each estate and smallholding. The coupons became a currency of their own: by 1936 the Straits Times was reporting that thousands of acres had gone out of tapping because owners found it more profitable to sell their coupons than their rubber. A money story about how the slump's cure created a market in permission to sell, and who gained from it (estates over smallholders). Found while researching the Great Depression post. *(Money angle; systems.)* *(Images: workable — 1934-36 Straits Times pages on the scheme and the coupon trade are whole-page candidates, and the rubber-estate and Pulau Brani photos used in the Depression post can be reused sparingly; check the agreement's dates, quotas and the coupon prices against NLB Infopedia and the newspapers before drafting.)*
- **Gambier and pepper: Singapore's first plantation fortune** — before rubber and tin, the island's interior was cleared for gambier (a tanning and dyeing extract) grown alongside pepper by Teochew planters, and by the 1840s there were hundreds of plantations worked by thousands of labourers, with Seah Eu Chin, the "gambier king", among the first to grow it on a large scale. The crop exhausted the soil and the forest that fuelled its boiling sheds, so the planters kept moving on, eventually into Johor under the kangchu system. Found while researching the remittance-houses post (Seah's 1847 account ties remittance seasons to the price of gambier). *(Money angle; systems.)* *(Images: workable — Uncaria gambir botanical plates and the Song Ong Siang portrait of Seah Eu Chin are on Commons; gambier-shed photos are thinner. Plantation counts, dates and Seah's role need checking against NLB Infopedia and Song Ong Siang before drafting.)*
- **The Chettiars of Market Street: Singapore's South Indian moneylenders** — before the banks would lend to small traders, Nattukottai Chettiar firms from Tamil Nadu ran their business from the kittangi (shared offices) of Market Street, lending to shopkeepers, smallholders and planters across Malaya, and built the Sri Thendayuthapani Temple on Tank Road. P. Govindasamy Pillai bought his first shop in 1929 with $2,000 borrowed from Chettiars. A money story about how the system worked (credit by caste network, hundi bills, the 1930s slump and the post-war decline). Found while researching the PGP post. *(Money angle; systems.)* *(Images: workable — G. R. Lambert's c.1890 "Chetti lender" portrait (already used once in the PGP post) and many CC photos of the Sri Thendayuthapani Temple are on Commons; Market Street photos are thinner. Firm counts, the kittangi and loan terms need sourcing from NLB Infopedia/BiblioAsia before drafting.)*
- **Jalan Kubor: the Old Malay Cemetery of Kampong Glam** — one of Singapore's oldest Muslim burial grounds, between Victoria Street and Jalan Kubor ("cemetery road"), said to hold members of Sultan Hussein's family and other early Malay, Bugis and Arab notables among its weathered, mostly unmarked stones; closed to burials long ago and left standing while the cemeteries around the city were cleared. A follow-up to the Hajjah Fatimah and cemeteries posts. *(Places; people.)* *(Images: workable — Commons has a 2010-14 photo set of the cemetery's graves, gateposts and banyan trees; its founding date, who is buried there, its closure and its legal status today need checking against NLB Infopedia and the Roots/URA listings before drafting.)*
- **The Prince of Wales's 1922 tour and the Cenotaph** — on 31 March 1922 Edward, Prince of Wales, laid the foundation stone of the Cenotaph on the Esplanade during his tour of the East on HMS Renown, and seven weeks later he landed on Labuan (a memorial stone there records 17 May 1922). A dateable visit with a full programme of parades, illuminations and receptions, and a monument still standing, that was found while researching the Labuan post. History through to present: the war dead it was built for, the 1922 visit, the Second World War names added later, and the Cenotaph today. *(Places; people.)* *(Images: workable — Commons has present-day Cenotaph photos and some period views of the Esplanade; check the Straits Times of 31 March and 1 April 1922 for the visit, a whole pre-1956 page being usable.)*

## People

## Japan–Singapore

- **Changi Prison, 1936 to 2004: the gaol whose wall and gate were kept** — opened in 1936, Changi Prison held European civilian internees during the Japanese Occupation (the Changi quilts were sewn there), then thousands of prisoners of war from 1944, and Japanese war-crimes suspects after the surrender. It was demolished in 2004 to make way for the new Changi Prison Complex, and only the front wall, the entrance gate and two turrets were kept; they are now a national monument. A place story that follows the Selarang and Changi Murals posts. *(Japan / Occupation angle; place-focused.)* *(Images: workable — Commons has 1936 and 2025 photos of the gate wall and turrets, IWM liberation photos, and Leslie Cole's official war paintings; check NewspaperSG for the 1936 opening (pre-1956 ST pages usable) and the 2004 demolition debate, and Roots.gov.sg for the monument's gazette date and any public debate over the demolition before drafting.)*
- **Ibuse Masuji in Syonan: the novelist who ran Singapore's newspaper in 1942** — conscripted into the Japanese army's propaganda unit in November 1941, the writer later famous for Black Rain arrived in Singapore with the invasion and ran the English-language Syonan Times (the renamed Straits Times) for about two months; his name is on the paper's early pages as printer and publisher. He kept a Syonan diary and later wrote about the occupied city in fiction, often as parody to get past the censors. A person story about how the Occupation's propaganda was made, and by whom. Found while researching the Nippon-go post. *(Japan angle; person-focused.)* *(Images: workable — 1952 portrait and earlier photos of Ibuse on Commons, and whole pre-1956 Syonan Times pages from February–April 1942; the Takiguchi paper (Waseda) and the Sumitomo Foundation report cover his Syonan writings, so check dates and his exact role against them before drafting.)*
- **Echigoya: the kimono shop that outlived the war, 1907–77** — founded in 1907 by Takahashi Chuhei to sell kimono to the karayuki-san, then turned to a European clientele after licensed Japanese prostitution ended in 1920; run from 1933 by Fukuda Kurahachi, who came to Singapore at 13, studied at the Anglo-Chinese School, surrendered the firm's assets intact to the British in 1941, was interned in India, and in November 1955 reopened it on Coleman Street as the first Japanese firm in Malaya after the war with local capital. It closed in 1977. A business biography that spans the whole arc of Singapore's Japanese community. Found while researching the Interned in India post. *(Japan angle; money; person-focused.)* *(Images: thin — the Singapore Standard of 8 November 1955 (page 10, with Fukuda's photograph) is already in hand; a 1920s photo of the store appears in Shimizu and Hirakawa, Japan and Singapore in the World Economy (not free); check NewspaperSG for Echigoya advertisements (pre-1956 whole pages) and NLB Infopedia's Echigoya article before drafting.)*
- **"Little Japan" on Middle Road** — from the 1900s to 1941 the Middle Road area was the centre of a sizeable Japanese civilian community: provision shops, photographers, dentists, a Japanese school and consulate, and (earlier) the karayuki-san brothels, all erased by the war and post-war repatriation. *(Japan angle.)* *(Images: workable — pre-war Japanese community photos exist in archives and some are public domain; Middle Road today is photographable.)*
- **Bahau: the "Catholic colony" Singaporeans were sent to** — in 1943 the Japanese administration and the Catholic Church (Bishop Adrien Devals) resettled several thousand Singaporeans, mostly Catholic Chinese and Eurasians, to farmland at Bahau in Negeri Sembilan to ease the wartime food shortage; the soil was poor, malaria rife, and many died, Bishop Devals among them. **Has a family thread:** Chris's wife's late mother was one of those relocated (see the Aukang / Holy Innocents' Lane connection). *(Japan / Occupation angle.)* *(Images: workable–thin — Catholic archives (history.catholic.sg) hold Occupation-era material; a Devals portrait may exist; Commons itself is thin.)*
- **The Padang and the Singapore Cricket Club, 1852 to today** — the open field Raffles set aside in front of the town, the Cricket Club founded beside it in 1852, and its later life as the stage for parades, the 1945 surrender ceremony on the steps of the Municipal Building, the first National Day Parade in 1966 and today's National Day shows. History through to present, with the field's uses stage by stage. *(Government ceremonies, so extra scrutiny on wording; check the Straits Times for the 1966 parade.)* *(Images: strong — 19th-century Padang prints and early-1900s postcards on Commons, plus many modern photos.)*

- **John Little, Raffles Place's other department store, 1842 to 2016** — Robinsons' neighbour and rival on the square for more than a century: a merchant's shop founded in the 1840s that became one of the colony's grand stores, with its arcaded building on Raffles Place, later taken over by Robinsons, moved to the Specialists' Shopping Centre on Orchard Road, and closed its last store in 2016. - **The three "Worlds": Singapore's vanished amusement parks** — New World, Great World and Happy World (later Gay World) were the great night-time pleasure grounds from the 1920s to the 1980s — cabaret, boxing, ronggeng, wayang, cinemas, food — where a whole generation spent its evenings, all gone now except a mall that kept the "Great World" name. *(Images: workable — entrance and interior photos of Great World and New World circulate; Great World City today is photographable.)*
- **The Van Kleef Aquarium** — Singapore's first public aquarium opened at River Valley in 1955, funded by a Dutch businessman's bequest, a fixture of school excursions for four decades before it closed in 1996 and was demolished in 1998, its fish moved to Sentosa. - **Sungei Road's Thieves' Market, Singapore's last flea market** — an unlicensed street market on Sungei Road that traded in secondhand goods (some, per its nickname, of dubious origin) for close to a century, tolerated by authorities for decades before finally being cleared in 2017 to make way for a housing/MRT redevelopment; long-time hawkers scattered, some to a smaller relocated pitch that itself didn't last. ## Energy

*(Added 2026-10-26, prompted by the news of 24 October 2026, when Indonesia's regulator SKK Migas cut pipeline gas exports to Singapore to divert the gas to its own power sector. Singapore makes about 95% of its electricity from natural gas, and in 2025 roughly 40-43% of its gas imports came by pipeline and the rest as LNG. EMA said the reduction was under 2% of daily gas demand and that supply would stay stable. The news is the hook, not the subject: these are history posts about how Singapore came to burn imported fuel and how it insured itself. Frame the story as "the scare the system absorbed", not a crisis, keep any government or minister mention neutral and factual, and state Indonesia's domestic needs as reported. - **Choosing who sells you power: the Open Electricity Market, 2018** — for decades every household bought its electricity from one supplier at one tariff; from a soft launch in Jurong on 1 April 2018, rolled out island-wide by November 2018, households could choose among licensed retailers or stay with SP Group at the regulated tariff. A plain-language explainer for readers who have never thought about where the bill comes from: what a retailer actually does when it owns no power station, how the half-hourly wholesale market works, the fixed-price and discount-off-tariff plans, and what happened when retailers left the market. A follow-up to the pipelines post. *(Systems; money; present-day.)* *(Images: thin — no Commons photos of the market itself, so it would lean on a self-made diagram of the supply chain (the pipelines post has a simple one), charts of the regulated tariff over time from SP Group's published tariffs, and present-day photos of HDB estates and power stations. Check the retailer numbers then and now, the tariff figures and any retailer exits against EMA releases before drafting, and keep the government and agency passages neutral.)*
- **Pulau Bukom: the kerosene island that became Singapore's first refinery** — in 1891 M. Samuel & Co., importing Russian kerosene, set up a tank depot on Pulau Bukom through its agents Syme & Co., the first of its kind in the East. The business became part of Shell, and on 26 July 1961 Shell opened Singapore's first oil refinery there, opened by Goh Keng Swee, which earned Shell the government's "Pioneer Certificate No. 1". About 5,000 people, mostly Shell families, lived on the island in the early 1960s. *(Money; places; systems.)* *(Images: workable — about 43 Commons files of the island from a distance, its housing and its mosque, but no free close-ups of the refinery (Shell and NAS images are rights-held); an OSM map, a refining-capacity chart and pre-1956 Straits Times pages for the kerosene era fill out the set. Check the Pioneer Certificate wording and date, the population figures, and the completed outcome of Shell's 2023-25 sale of its Bukom and Jurong assets.)*
- **St James Power Station: the coal-fired power station of 1926** — built on reclaimed swamp at Cape St James, with its first unit commissioned on 30 September 1926 and an official opening by Governor Sir Hugh Clifford on 7 November 1927. At 22,000 kW it dwarfed the 3,200 kW of everything before it, but its early years brought breakdowns and blackouts. For its first decade its coal came largely from Manchuria through Japanese firms, until the late 1930s. It closed in 1976, became a warehouse and then a nightclub, was made a national monument in 2009, and is now Dyson's global headquarters. *(Places; systems; Japan angle.)* *(Images: workable † — many CC photos of the building today; period photos are thin, but pre-1956 Straits Times pages on the opening and the blackouts are usable. Lead with the fuel and supply story, since NHB already covers the building well. Check the Manchurian-coal detail against NLB, and don't call it Singapore's first power station: the Mackenzie Road station of 1906 came first.)*
- **Jurong Island: seven islands into one** — off Jurong lay seven low islands, Ayer Chawan, Ayer Merbau, Merlimau, Pesek, Pesek Kecil, Sakra and Seraya, with Malay fishing villages, until the oil companies arrived in the late 1960s. JTC began joining them by reclamation in 1995, with Japan's Penta-Ocean Construction as a major contractor, and Jurong Island was opened on 14 October 2000. It grew from 991 to over 3,000 hectares and is where the LNG terminal and much of the gas system stand. A companion to the Sisters' Islands post. *(Money; places; systems.)* *(Images: workable — about 87 Commons files, including aerials and a locator map; the vanished villages have almost no free photos, so before-and-after island outlines make the strongest self-made graphic. - **1973: the oil shock and the winter Singapore turned down the lights** — when the Arab oil embargo cut crude deliveries to Singapore's refineries by about 12.5%, a declassified US State Department cable of 21 December 1973 records that the government asked the public to cut electricity and petrol use by 12.5%, and that consumption fell 5-10% within weeks. It also records the decision not to stop the oil companies exporting their stocks. *(Systems; money.)* *(Images: workable — 1973 is after the 1956 line, so no Straits Times pages; lean on a chart of the world oil price 1970-80 and the cable's figures, with generic public-domain oil-crisis imagery used sparingly and labelled. Keep the frame local, and keep the government passages neutral.)*
- **First currents: trams, docks and the Mackenzie Road power station, 1897-1906** — electricity came to Singapore for machines, not homes: the harbour was generating power by 1897, the Singapore Tramway Company built supply for its electric trams from 1902, and in 1906 the Tanjong Pagar Dock Company opened the Mackenzie Road power station for its docks and workshops, selling surplus current to the town. A prequel to St James and a follow-up to the Tanjong Pagar Dock Company post. *(Systems; places.)* *(Images: workable † — pre-1956 Straits Times pages and KITLV tram-era street photos; no photo of the Mackenzie Road station itself has been found, so maps may have to carry it. - **The LNG terminal: the insurance policy Singapore built in 2013** — until 2013 all of Singapore's gas came by pipeline from two neighbours. The S$1.7 billion terminal on Jurong Island, decided on in 2006, began commercial operations on 7 May 2013, sized to meet the whole country's gas demand if needed. In 2025 LNG supplied about 57-60% of gas imports, and in October 2026 EMA said more LNG could be brought in to cover the Indonesian cut; a second, offshore terminal is planned. Best paired with the pipelines post as a two-part series. *(Systems; money.)* *(Images: thin-workable — the terminal is photographed mainly in corporate and press images, so the post leans on a chart of the pipeline-versus-LNG split, a chart of gas's share of generation and an OSM map of the site. - **Senoko: the giant on the northern shore, 1976** — planned from 1971, Senoko Power Station was built by PUB in three stages (1976, 1979, 1983) for S$1.2 billion, with contracts repeatedly going to Japan's Marubeni; by 1983 its 1,610 MW supplied 60% of Singapore's electricity. In 1992 it began burning Malaysian pipeline gas, a submarine cable links it to Pasir Gudang in Johor, and in 2008 it was sold to a Marubeni-led consortium for S$3.65 billion, so the builder returned as owner. *(Places; money; Japan angle.)* *(Images: thin-workable — only a handful of Commons files, some taken from the Johor side; the post leans on a chart of capacity by stage and an OSM map. Check the date and extent of the 1992 fire and outage, the Johor interconnection, and who opened the station in 1977.)*
- **Kallang Gasworks and the town gas century, 1862-1998** — Singapore's first piped gas was made from coal at the Kallang Gasworks, built in 1862 by the London-formed Singapore Gas Company beside the Kallang Basin, where coal came in by barge; locals called it huay sia, "fire city", for fear it would explode. The municipality took it over in 1901, the last gas street lamp went in 1956, it switched from coal to oil in 1958, and it closed on 23 March 1998 after 137 years. Town gas is still piped to homes today, made from the same natural gas now in the news. *(Places; systems.)* *(Images: thin — Commons has almost nothing, and the familiar photos are NLB-held with all rights reserved, so not usable; this would be led by pre-1956 Straits Times pages, a self-made site map and present-day photos of the site. - **Solar on the reservoirs: the Tengeh floating solar farm, 2021** — opened on 14 July 2021 by Sembcorp and PUB, with 122,000 panels on 45 hectares of Tengeh Reservoir (60 MWp), enough to make Singapore's five water treatment plants fully green-powered. A present-day coda to an energy series rather than a standalone post. *(Systems; places.)* *(Images: thin — no Commons files of the farm, and the aerials in circulation are Sembcorp and PUB press images, so it would need photos from public viewpoints (or Chris's own on a future trip), charts of solar growth and maps. ## Present-day / policy

*(Image availability is less of a gate here — these lean on self-made charts, present-day CC photos and maps rather than historical photography.)*

- **Follow-up to the Benjamin Sheares post: has fertility policy actually changed anything?** — the Sheares post's Short is performing well (1,134 views vs. the full video's 161, per YouTube Studio, 2026-09-04); a genuine follow-up would take the government's full policy U-turn since his "Stop at Two" era — the 1980s pivot to "Have Three or More," the Baby Bonus, today's pronatalist cash incentives and Baby Bonus top-ups — and ask directly whether any of it moved the total fertility rate, which has kept sliding regardless of which direction the policy pointed. *(Capitalises on a topic already shown to perform; money/policy angle.)*
- **The Platform Workers Act's slow-motion phase-in** — CPF contributions for delivery riders and ride-hail drivers only became mandatory in 2025, with transition support covering 100% of the worker's added cost in 2025 but sliding to 75% (2026), 50% (2027) and 25% (2028) — a safety net for gig workers that's already scheduled to quietly thin out just as it's introduced. *(Money angle.)*
- **The COE: paying for permission to own a car, then paying again to drive it** — the Vehicle Quota System's Certificate of Entitlement began on 1 May 1990 (first bidding 2 April), introduced after 1975–90 saw car ownership growing ~12% a year with no room left to build more roads; a COE now often costs more than the car itself, making Singapore's cost of car ownership among the highest in the world. Pairs naturally with Electronic Road Pricing, the *other* half of the cost of driving here — introduced 1998 as the world's first road congestion-pricing system, and now itself being replaced: gantry-based ERP is being phased out for ERP 2.0, satellite/GNSS-based on-board units that track a vehicle by location rather than a fixed toll point, mandatory in every Singapore-registered vehicle by full rollout in 2027. Own the car, then pay per road; both levers exist because of the same original land constraint, and the two are always discussed as a pair (Chris's framing). ERP rates have climbed enough that avoiding certain gantries/routes is now a real factor in where people choose to live, not just how they commute — a hidden-effect angle worth leading with. *(Money angle — strong fit.)* *(Images: workable — ERP gantries (old and new) are heavily CC-photographed; COE itself has little to photograph directly, so the ownership-cost side would lean on a self-made chart of COE prices over time.)*
- **"Quiet cracking" at work** — roughly 30% of Singapore workers report this quieter, harder-to-spot form of disengagement/burnout in 2026 surveys; unlike "quiet quitting," employees keep performing while struggling, so managers often miss it entirely.
