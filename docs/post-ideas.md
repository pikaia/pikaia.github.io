## History

- **Opium farming funded colonial Singapore** — opium was legal and taxed for decades; opium farm revenue was one of the largest sources of government income before WWII. Bigger, more systemic story than the usual "founding of Singapore" fare. *(Money angle — YouTube stats favor this category.)*
- **The gutta-percha boom that stripped Malaya's forests** — before rubber, Singapore was the world's entrepôt for gutta-percha, a latex tapped from Malayan trees that insulated the first submarine telegraph cables (the Victorian internet); the trade nearly wiped the trees out within a few decades, then collapsed. A vanished-commodity fortune in the same vein as the pineapple kings. *(Money angle — YouTube stats favor this category.)*
- **Before HDB: the Singapore Improvement Trust and Tiong Bahru** — the colonial SIT built Singapore's first public housing at Tiong Bahru in the 1930s–40s in streamline-moderne Art Deco; the flats were aspirational (a nickname pinned them as a "den of beauties" for kept mistresses), survived, and are now conserved and gentrified. The pre-HDB chapter of Singapore's public-housing story. *(Housing/money angle — HDB-related posts perform well.)*
- **The 1915 Singapore Mutiny** — a WWI-era revolt by sepoys of a Muslim Indian regiment, fueled partly by rumors that the Kaiser had converted to Islam; put down with help from Russian, French, and Japanese troops, with 47 executed by firing squad. Barely discussed compared to WWII history.
- **When Singapore had wild tigers** — until the early 20th century tigers regularly crossed from Johor and roamed the island's interior; 19th-century gambier and pepper plantation workers were killed at a rate contemporaries described as roughly one a day at the trade's peak, bounties were paid per hide, and the most-told anecdote (a tiger shot under the billiard table of Raffles Hotel, 1902) points to how late the animals persisted. The last wild tiger is usually dated to a shooting near Choa Chu Kang around 1930. A vanished-wildlife story nobody connects to the built-up island today.
- **Coney Island, before it was a nature park** — the island off Punggol officially named Pulau Serangoon was bought by the Aw brothers of Tiger Balm, who built a beach villa and renamed it after New York's Coney Island; a 1950s plan to turn it into a seaside amusement resort came to nothing; the state later acquired and enlarged it with reclamation and, in 2015, opened it as a deliberately rustic park (casuarina forest, birdlife, a lone feral Brahman bull). A name every Singaporean knows, with a tycoon-and-failed-resort backstory almost nobody does. *(Money angle; direct follow-on from the Aw brothers / Tiger Balm post.)*

## People

- **Lim Peng Siang, the forgotten "greatest magnate"** — founder of the Ho Hong business empire spanning shipping, banking, cement, and rice, once ranked among Singapore's biggest tycoons in the 1930s, now almost entirely absent from popular memory. *(Money angle — YouTube stats favor this category.)*
- **P. Govindasamy Pillai, from cloth peddler to retail pioneer** — a South Indian immigrant who built the PGP department store chain and became a major community philanthropist, overshadowed today by better-remembered Chinese business dynasties. *(Money angle, weaker.)*
- **William Farquhar, the co-founder written out of the story** — Singapore's first Resident did much of the actual early nation-building work, but was overshadowed and later pushed out by Raffles, who gets sole founding-father credit today.
- **The Chetti Melaka (Peranakan Indians)** — a centuries-old community blending Chinese, Malay, and South Indian heritage into its own Creole language and dress, now down to only a few hundred officially recorded members. A genuinely obscure counterpart to the much more famous (Chinese) Peranakan story.
- **Ya'acob Mohamed, the forgotten pioneer** — a first-generation PAP leader whose political career spanned the formative years of Singapore and Malaysia's political journeys, now far less remembered than his contemporaries; the Malay-medium schools he helped establish have since closed.
- **David Marshall, the reluctant first Chief Minister** — a Sephardic Jewish criminal lawyer who became Singapore's first Chief Minister in 1955, then resigned within a year on a point of principle over self-government talks, later serving as a globe-trotting ambassador.
- **The nutmeg estates that became Orchard Road** — before it was a shopping belt, Orchard Road, Cairnhill and Claymore were nutmeg and clove plantations (1830s–60s), until a blight wiped the trees out in barely a decade and the land was sold off. "Orchard" is literally that, and Cuppage, Scotts, Claymore and Grange all carry planters' names nobody connects to a person. A vanished-crop-and-a-fortune story in the same vein as the pineapple kings. *(Money angle; hidden-etymology.)*
- **Whampoa: the merchant who brought Singapore its first ice** — Hoo Ah Kay ("Whampoa"), a Cantonese provisioner who became the only Asian on the colonial Legislative Council, imported Singapore's first ice (shipped as blocks from a lake near Boston), built an ice house, and hosted governors at his famous Bendemeer garden. A tycoon-and-a-first story, and street names (Whampoa, Bendemeer) nobody connects to a person. *(Money angle; person-focused.)*

## Japan–Singapore

- **Tani Yutaka, the "Tiger of Malaya"** — a Japanese Cemetery Park burial, a bandit-turned-spy who guided Japanese troops through Malaya's jungles before the 1942 invasion, mythologised in Japanese films as a folk hero and reviled locally as a traitor-collaborator.
- **"Little Japan" on Middle Road** — from the 1900s to 1941 the Middle Road area was the centre of a sizeable Japanese civilian community: provision shops, photographers, dentists, a Japanese school and consulate, and (earlier) the karayuki-san brothels, all erased by the war and the community's post-war repatriation. An obscure Japan–Singapore social history that predates the Occupation. *(Japan angle — YouTube stats favor this category.)*
- **The pre-war Japanese fishing fleet** — by the 1930s Japanese trawler crews supplied much of Singapore's fresh fish and were clustered around Kampong Wak Hassan on the northern coast; colonial authorities suspected the boats doubled as coastal reconnaissance, and the whole fleet vanished in 1941–42. A pre-Occupation Japan–Singapore economic story with an espionage undertone. *(Japan angle — YouTube stats favor this category.)*
- **Bahau: the "Catholic colony" Singaporeans were sent to** — in 1943 the Japanese administration and the Catholic Church (Bishop Adrien Devals) resettled several thousand Singaporeans, mostly Catholic Chinese and Eurasians, to farmland at Bahau in Negeri Sembilan to ease the wartime food shortage; the soil was poor, malaria rife, and many died, Bishop Devals among them. An almost-forgotten Occupation story about Singaporeans sent away rather than invaded. *(Japan / Occupation angle.)*
- **Assembled in Singapore: the short life of local car plants** — under a 1960s–70s import-substitution policy Singapore assembled cars from kits (a Ford plant at Bukit Timah; Datsun, Austin, Mercedes and others elsewhere) until the home market proved far too small and the plants closed by the early 1980s. A vanished-industry story with a Japan thread. *(Japan + money angle.)*

## Food & culture

- **Change Alley, the money-changers' bazaar that vanished** — a narrow lane between Raffles Place and Collyer Quay, packed for decades with money-changers, touts and stalls, a fixed stop on every 1960s–80s tourist itinerary, cleared in 1989 for the Hitachi Tower / Caltex House redevelopment and reduced to an air-conditioned overhead link bridge that kept the name. A demolished-institution story in the same vein as Bugis Street, with a money angle built in.
- **Robinsons, the 162-year department store, and the 1972 fire** — founded 1858, Singapore's oldest department store and a fixture of Raffles Place, gutted by a catastrophic fire in November 1972 that killed nine and destroyed the flagship building, rebuilt and carried on, then quietly shut every Singapore store in 2020–21 after more than a century and a half. A money / retail story with a dateable disaster at its centre (worth a Straits Times archive check). *(Money angle — YouTube stats favor this category.)*
- **The three "Worlds": Singapore's vanished amusement parks** — New World, Great World and Happy World (later Gay World) were the great night-time pleasure grounds from the 1920s to the 1980s — cabaret, boxing, ronggeng, wayang, cinemas, food — where a whole generation spent its evenings, all gone now except a shopping mall that kept the "Great World" name. A demolished-institution story in the same vein as Bugis Street and Change Alley.
- **The Van Kleef Aquarium** — Singapore's first public aquarium opened at River Valley in 1955, funded by a Dutch businessman's bequest, and was a fixture of school excursions for four decades before it closed in 1996 and was demolished in 1998, its fish moved to Sentosa. A small, fondly-remembered lost institution.

## Present-day / policy

- **The Platform Workers Act's slow-motion phase-in** — CPF contributions for delivery riders and ride-hail drivers only became mandatory in 2025, with transition support covering 100% of the worker's added cost in 2025 but sliding to 75% (2026), 50% (2027), and 25% (2028) — a safety net for gig workers that's already scheduled to quietly thin out just as it's introduced. *(Money angle — YouTube stats favor this category.)*
- **"Quiet cracking" at work** — roughly 30% of Singapore workers report this quieter, harder-to-spot form of disengagement/burnout in 2026 surveys; unlike "quiet quitting," employees keep performing while struggling, so managers often miss it entirely.
