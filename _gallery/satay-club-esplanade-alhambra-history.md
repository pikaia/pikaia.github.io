Nine more images spanning the Satay Club's older haunts, the food's long journey out of the region, and where satay is found in Singapore today.

### A dhoby field, circa 1880 — how Dhoby Ghaut got its name

![A washing and bleaching field in Singapore, circa 1880](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/e/ec/KITLV_-_103979_-_Washing_and_bleaching_field%2C_Singapore_-_circa_1880.tif/lossy-page1-1280px-KITLV_-_103979_-_Washing_and_bleaching_field%2C_Singapore_-_circa_1880.tif.jpg)

*A washing and bleaching field in Singapore, circa 1880. Fields like this, worked by Indian washermen (dhobis), are how the Dhoby Ghaut area — near the Satay Club's second home in the mid-1950s — got its name. (Photo: KITLV collection / Leiden University Library / Wikimedia Commons, public domain)*

### Saté for a Dutch rijsttafel, 1943–45

![Saté being prepared for an official rijsttafel, Dutch East Indies, 1943-45](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/f/f6/Collectie_Wereldmuseum%2C_TM-FV-0586-104%2C_Foto%2C_%27Bereiding_van_sat%C3%A9_voor_een_offici%C3%ABle_rijsttafel_met_leden_van_de_luchtmacht_en_de_NICA%2C_tijdens_de_bevrijding_van_Nieuw-Guinea%27%2C_1943-1945.jpg)

*Saté being prepared for an official rijsttafel in the Dutch East Indies, sometime between 1943 and 1945 — one stage of the journey that carried the dish, spelled "saté", onto Dutch restaurant menus. (Photo: Wereldmuseum, Netherlands / Wikimedia Commons, public domain)*
