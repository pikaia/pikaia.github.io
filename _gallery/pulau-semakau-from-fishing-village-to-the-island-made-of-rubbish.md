The landfill, the shore it spared, and three of the creatures that make the island famous among naturalists — photographed on visits in 2006, when the island was newly open to the public, and on reefs elsewhere for the species portraits. No freely licensed photographs of the two villages survive in the open collections; the kampongs are the gap in this gallery, as they are on the island itself.

### Where it is

![A map of Singapore's main island with a marker in the sea to its south](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/d/d3/Singapore_location_map_%28main_island%29.svg)

*Pulau Semakau sits about eight kilometres south of the main island, among the southern islands — close enough that its ash barges make the run from Tuas overnight. (Seloloving, CC BY-SA 4.0, via Wikimedia Commons)*

### The receiving station

![The landfill's receiving station hall seen across the water](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/c/c9/Pulau_Semakau_32%2C_Nov_06.JPG)

*The transfer building where barges unload, built on the site of Pulau Seking — the Orang Selat village island is underneath the landfill's front door. (Sengkang, copyrighted free use, via Wikimedia Commons)*

### The Phase II cell

![A panorama across a vast shallow lagoon enclosed by rock bunds](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/c/c6/Pulau_Semakau%2C_panorama_3%2C_Nov_06.jpg)

*The great wet cell from the western bund. Before this lagoon was closed for filling in 2015, it had become a reef — more than 700 coral colonies were lifted out of it and moved to the Sisters' Islands. (Sengkang, copyrighted free use, via Wikimedia Commons)*

### The shore that was spared

![A sandy beach with palm trees and a lookout tower on Pulau Semakau](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/8/82/Pulau_Semakau_10%2C_Nov_06.JPG)

*The landfill took the island's eastern side; the western shore kept its beaches, mangroves and reef flats, and filled cells elsewhere on the island have grassed over. (Sengkang, copyrighted free use, via Wikimedia Commons)*

### The knobbly sea star

![A large orange sea star with dark knobs, resting on sand](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/d/d6/Knobbly_sea_star_%28Protoreaster_nodosus%29.jpg)

*The knobbly sea star, endangered in Singapore but still found in good numbers on Semakau's flats at low tide — the sight every guided walk hoped for. (Ria Tan, CC BY-SA 2.0, via Wikimedia Commons; photographed in Singapore)*

### Tape seagrass

![Long green blades of tape seagrass rising from a sandy seabed](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/b/be/Enhalus_acoroides_%2830774220094%29.jpg)

*Tape seagrass. Semakau holds Singapore's largest visitable bed of it, stretching for kilometres along the western shore, monitored by volunteers since 2007. (Christian Gloor, CC BY 2.0, via Wikimedia Commons; photographed in Indonesia)*

### The great-billed heron

![A tall grey heron standing alert at the edge of water](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/4/44/Great-billed_heron_%28Ardea_sumatrana_sumatrana%29_Danum_Valley.jpg)

*The great-billed heron, the shy giant of Singapore's southern shores — Semakau's long-term resident was nicknamed Jimmy by the guides. (Charles J. Sharp, CC BY-SA 4.0, via Wikimedia Commons; photographed in Borneo)*
